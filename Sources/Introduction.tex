\noindent Understanding the fundamental laws that govern matter and its interactions has guided particle physics since its early development. As new phenomena were discovered, the theoretical frameworks evolved to describe the behavior of elementary particles and forces, and the synthesis achieved in the last century culminated in the Standard Model of particle physics (SM)~\cite{Glashow:1961tr,Weinberg:1967tq,Salam:1968rm}, a quantum field theory that describes all known elementary particles and their electromagnetic, weak and strong interactions, the first two unified into the electroweak interaction.

The SM has been remarkably successful, both in its predictive accuracy and in the experimental evidence that supports it, much of it obtained at colliders such as the Large Hadron Collider (LHC)~\cite{Evans:2008zzb}. The final piece in the experimental exploration of the SM model, the Higgs boson, was proposed in 1964~\cite{Englert:1964et,Higgs:1964ia,Higgs:1964pj,Guralnik:1964eu} and discovered in 2012 by the ATLAS and CMS collaborations~\cite{ATLAS:2012yve,CMS:2012qbp,CMS:2013btf}, confirming that electroweak symmetry breaking (EWSB) takes place through the Brout--Englert--Higgs (BEH) mechanism. The discovery completed the particle content of the SM, but its interest goes beyond that, because of the unique properties of the particle itself.

As the only known elementary scalar, the Higgs boson opened a new frontier in the exploration of the scalar sector, and ushered the LHC physics program into a new era. During Run 1 of the LHC (2010--2012), which led to its discovery, the first measurements of its properties were found to be consistent with the SM predictions~\cite{ATLAS:2015yey,ATLAS:2016neq}. After Run I completion, a new phase of characterization and precision measurements focused on the mass and with of the Higgs boson, and its couplings to other SM particles began during Run 2 (2015-2018). The exploration of the Higgs sector across all probed features has shown that the Higgs particle behaves in a very SM-like manner thus far~\cite{CMS:2022dwd,ATLAS:2022vkf}.

The picture is nevertheless incomplete. Although the SM describes all the measurements performed at the GeV and TeV scales, it leaves fundamental questions unanswered, such as the origin of the matter-antimatter asymmetry~\cite{Sakharov:1967dj}, the nature of dark matter~\cite{Bertone:2004pz,Planck:2018vyg} or the quantum description of gravity, which suggest that it is only part of a more comprehensive theory. Physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM) has therefore become the focus of both theoretical and experimental efforts, and many models have been proposed to address these questions. The Higgs sector is a natural place to look for it, in light of the open questions on the stability of the electroweak vacuum~\cite{EliasMiro:2011aa} and the naturalness of the Higgs boson mass~\cite{tHooft:1979rat}.

One of the key elements of the Higgs sector that remain to be measured is the self-interaction of the Higgs boson, encoded in the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$, which the data collected so far by the LHC experiments have not yet determined. Since this coupling is directly related to the shape of the Higgs potential and to its nonzero vacuum expectation value (VEV), its measurement would be a powerful closure test of EWSB and of the SM. The most direct access to it is the production of Higgs boson pairs (HH)~\cite{Baglio:2012np,DiMicco:2019ngk}, which is also highly sensitive to BSM effects, either through loop contributions that modify its rate and kinematics or through the resonant decay of new states into a pair of Higgs bosons. Because the HH process explores the fundamental nature of the Higgs scalar field as it provides unique handle for directly accessing $\lambda_{HHH}$, its measurement has become a fundamental priority for the particle physics community~\cite{deBlas:2025gyz}.

   
And yet, the experimental measurement of the trilinear self-coupling remains an outstanding task: observing HH SM production at the LHC is extremely challenging due to its small cross section~\cite{LHCHWG:HHlegacy}. However, the amount of data collected during the operation of the LHC does provide a measure of the sensitivity achieved for this process. Through the study and combination of multiple Higgs boson decay modes, stringent upper limits on the HH production cross section and exclusion ranges for the self-coupling have already been derived using LHC Run 2 data~\cite{ATLAS:2024ish,CMS_combination,ATLASCMS:2026HH}. In this regard, the increase in statistics provided by the LHC Run 3 (2022-2026) will entail substantial benefits for investigating the Di-Higgs mechanism. Among the various decay channels of interest, the $bb\tau\tau$ final state stands out thanks to its relatively large branching fraction and the moderate background contamination, making it one of the most sensitive channels for HH searches~\cite{CMS-HIG-20-010,ATLAS-HH-bbtautau-Run2}.

Despite the statistical limitations -- that are gradually being overcome thanks to improved reconstruction techniques and identification methods --, the definitive breakthrough will come from the High-Luminosity LHC (HL-LHC). Scheduled to begin operation in 2030~\cite{ZurbanoFernandez:2020cco,HLLHC-plan-2025}, the HL-LHC is expected to significantly increase in instantaneous luminosity and collected data, paving the way for previously unexplored areas of physics. In particular, in the scalar sector, it is expected to provide the first evidence of Di-Higgs production~\cite{ATLASCMS:2025HLLHC}, with an expected significance above $7\sigma$, and a measurement of the trilinear self-coupling with a precision better than $30\%$~\cite{ATLASCMS:2025HLLHC}, allowing for a deeper exploration of the EWSB properties and the SM validity.

This thesis contributes to this effort through the search for HH production in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state with the Run 3 data. Besides the limited statistics, the sensitivity of this search depends on correctly identifying the objects of the final state, in an environment where several tens of proton-proton collisions overlap in each bunch crossing. The work presented here therefore focuses on the identification of the hadronically decaying $\tau$ leptons and on the selection of the jets of the event, both those from the $H\to b\bar{b}$ decay and those that characterize vector boson fusion production, as well as on the interpretation of the HH searches beyond the SM.

The thesis is organized in three parts. The first one sets the framework of the work. Chapter \ref{ch:theory} introduces the SM, the BEH mechanism and the phenomenology of Higgs boson pair production, together with the experimental status of its searches, and Chapter \ref{ch:LHC_CMS} describes the LHC, the CMS detector and the reconstruction of the physics objects.

The second part presents the search for Higgs boson pair production in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state with the Run 3 data, preceded by the identification of the objects to which this thesis has contributed most directly. Chapter \ref{ch:Tau} describes the identification of hadronically decaying $\tau$ leptons and its validation with the Run 3 data, which constitutes the contribution of this thesis to the Tau POG and has been published in Ref.~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-074}. Chapter \ref{ch:taggers} presents the algorithms that assign the jets of the event to the $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidate and to the VBF topology. The retraining of \textsc{HH-BTag} for the Run 2 search for resonant $HH$ production \cite{CMS-PAS-B2G-24-011}, its validation for the study of $ZZ$ and $ZH$ production in the same final state \cite{CMS:2026zzzh}, its retraining for the Run 3 analysis and the development of \textsc{VBFjTag} are contributions of this thesis. Chapters \ref{ch:bbtt_analysis} and \ref{ch:bbtt_results} describe the Run 3 analysis \cite{CMS-PAS-HIG-25-008} and its results, including their combination with the Run 2 dataset, to which this thesis has contributed \textcolor{red}{[completar: \textsc{HH-BTag} y \textsc{VBFjTag}, SFs QuadJet, validación del método ABCD para el Run2 donde lo meto? ...]}.

The third part addresses the interpretation of $HH$ searches beyond the SM. Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling} develops the modeling of the gluon fusion $HH$ signal in the HEFT framework, extending the matrix inversion method beyond the coverage of previous ATLAS and CMS results, which constitutes the main original contribution of this thesis to the BSM interpretation of $HH$ searches and is documented in Ref.~\cite{LHCHWG-2026-015}.
