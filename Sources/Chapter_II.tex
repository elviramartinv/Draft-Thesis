\chapter{The Large Hadron Collider and the CMS detector}

\noindent The European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN) hosts the world's leading laboratory for particle physics and positions itself as pioneering force in fundamental scientific research and technological development within a unique environment of international collaboration and education. Founded in 1954 and located on the French-Swiss border near Geneva, the CERN scientific collaboration brings together more than 17000 members from hundreds of institutes worlwide. Throughout its history, CERN has established itself as a global scientific epicenter through its groundbreaking accelerators and experiments. Facilities such as the Proton Synchrotron (PS), the Super Proton Synchroton (SPS), and the Large Electron-Positron Collider (LEP) have enabled historic discoveries, including the W and Z bosons. Furthermore, CERN has also driven innovation far beyond particle physics, contributing significant advances to technology, most notably the World Wide Web.

Today, the heart of CERN lies in the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), the most powerful particle accelerator ever built and the flagship facility of modern high-energy physics. The LHC is designed to collide proton beams at unprecedented energies and luminosities. This enables the study of extremely rare processes and the production of heavy particles that cannot be accessed elsewhere. Its 27-kilometer underground ring houses four interaction points, each equipped with a dedicated experiment tailored to different aspects of the physics program. The Compact Muon Solenoid (CMS), one of two general-purpose detectors, is designed to explore a wide range of phenomena, from precision measurements of SM processes to searches for new BSM physics. 

The CERN complex houses not only to the large, general-purpose LHC experiments but also to smaller, specialized detectors that expand the scientific scope of the laboratory. Taken together, this experimental diversity reflects the breadth of areas covered by modern high-energy physics and demonstrates how the CERN complex has evolved since its inception into a unique infrastructure capable of simultaneously addressing multiple frontiers of fundamental knowledge.

\section{The Large Hadron Collider}
\label{sec:LHC}
%
The initial proposal for the LHC was submitted in 1984, but the project was not officially approved by CERN until December 1994, and its construction took place in the $26.7$-km tunnel that had previously housed the LEP collider. Following its commissioning in 2008, the first proton-proton (pp) collisions occurred in late 2009, and the physics program began in 2010. The LHC is designed to produce pp collisions at a center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=14$ TeV and with an instantaneous luminosity of $\mathcal{L}=10^{34}$ cm$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$~\cite{Evans:2008zzb}, and it also operates with heavy-ion beams, whose program is not covered in this thesis. Originally conceived as a discovery machine, with the search for the Higgs boson as its primary goal, the LHC has established itself as a multipurpose collider, capable of supporting a very broad scientific program, ranging from the search for rare processes and BSM physics to precision measurements of SM properties. The discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 by the ATLAS and CMS collaborations confirmed the Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism of EWSB~\cite{ATLAS:2012yve,CMS:2012qbp}, and, to date, the LHC has completed three data-taking periods, the last of which concluded in 2026, and will continue to operate in its high-luminosity phase until the early 2040s.

This section first describes the accelerator chain that supplies proton beams to the LHC, in Section \ref{subsec:LHC_complex}. Next, Section \ref{subsec:LHC_design} presents the design of the machine and main parameters. Finally, Section \ref{subsec:LHC_operation} traces the operational history of the LHC, from Run 1 through Run 3, and briefly discusses the prospects for the HL-LHC phase.


\subsection{Accelerator complex}
\label{subsec:LHC_complex}
%
To accelerate protons from rest to energies on the order of TeV, a series of machines is required to progressively inject the beam at increasingly higher energies, since each accelerator can only operate within a limited energy range~\cite{Benedikt:2004wm,Vretenar:2020quc}. The LHC is the last stage of a chain of accelerators, most of them inherited from earlier experimental programs, in which each machine increases the energy of the beam before injecting it into the next one. In addition to feeding the LHC, several of these accelerators supply beams to their own experimental facilities, which carry out a physics program at lower energies in parallel. The layout of the complex is shown in Figure \ref{fig:cern_complex}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/chapter_II/cern_complex.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of the CERN accelerator complex~\cite{CERN-complex-2022}.}
    \label{fig:cern_complex}
\end{figure}

The chain begins with an H$^-$ ion source fed with hydrogen gas. The ions are extracted from the source with an energy of $45$ keV and injected into the linear accelerator (Linac4), where a radiofrequency quadrupole (RFQ) bunches the beam and accelerates it to $3$ MeV. A series of radiofrequency accelerating structures then raises their energy to $160$ MeV. The beam is subsequently injected into the Proton Synchrotron Booster (PSB), a synchrotron consisting of four vertically stacked rings, where a carbon foil strips the two electrons from each ion, resulting in a beam containing only protons. The PSB accelerates the protons to $2$ GeV before transferring them to the next stage in the chain.

The protons are then transferred to the Proton Synchrotron (PS), where they reach an energy of $26$ GeV and where where successive bunch splittings produce trains of bunches spaced by $25$ ns, the bunch spacing with which the LHC operates. The final stage prior to the LHC is the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS), which accumulates several trains of bunches from the PS and accelerates them to an injection energy of $450$ GeV.


From the SPS, the trains are transferred to the LHC via two independent transfer lines, so that the beams circulate in opposite directions in separate beam pipes. Once the planned number of bunches for each beam has been reached, the acceleration phase begins using superconducting radiofrequency cavities operating at $400$ MHz. These cavities provide the protons with about $0.5$ MeV per turn and, at the same time, keep the bunches longitudinally focused. In this manner, over the course of about twenty minutes, the beams reach their final energy — which during Run 3 was $6.8$ TeV per beam — and, once stabilized, are brought into collision at the four interaction points equipped with detectors.

\subsection{LHC Design and parameters}
\label{subsec:LHC_design}
%
The LHC is housed in the $26.7$ km-long tunnel that was originally built to accommodate the LEP collider. Given the small interior diameter of the tunnel — just $3.7$ m in the arcs — the LHC employs a \textit{two-in-one} design, in which the two beam pipes through which the beams travel in opposite directions are housed within a single mechanical structure and a single cryostat~\cite{Evans:2008zzb}.

The maximum energy that a beam can reach in a ring of a given circumference is limited by the magnetic field capable of bending its trajectory. Therefore, to keep $7$ TeV protons in orbit, the LHC uses $1232$ superconducting dipoles capable of generating a field of $8.33$ T when cooled to $1.9$ K, a temperature achieved using superfluid helium~\cite{Bruning:2004ej}. In addition to bending the trajectory, it is necessary to keep the beam focused; for this purpose, $392$ quadrupole magnets are located along the entire ring, complemented by higher-order multipole magnets that correct imperfections in the field. The ring is divided into eight arcs, where the dipoles are located, and eight straight sections, which contain the interaction points and the beam acceleration, collimation, and extraction systems.

With two $7$ TeV beams, collisions occur at a center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=14$ TeV, although the energy available in each interaction is only a fraction of this, determined by the momentum carried by the partons involved in the interaction, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:pp_pheno}.\\


%Los arcos están formados por celdas regulares en las que los dipolos se alternan con los cuadrupolos y los imanes correctores, mientras que las secciones rectas albergan los puntos de interacción y los sistemas de aceleración, colimación y extracción del haz, junto con los cuadrupolos que focalizan los haces antes de cada colisión~\cite{Evans:2008zzb}.

% Luminosidad
The reach of the LHC is also determined by the instantaneous luminosity $\mathcal{L}$, which is the parameter that relates the number of events of a given process occurring per unit time to its cross section $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dN}{dt} = \mathcal{L}\,\sigma 
    \label{eq:event_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ is a quantity that depends solely on the beam conditions. At the LHC, for Gaussian-profile beams colliding at a crossing angle $\theta_c$, the luminosity is expressed as~\cite{Bruning:2004ej}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \frac{\gamma\, f_\text{rev}\, n_b\, N_b^2}{4\pi\, \varepsilon_n\, \beta^*}\, F, \qquad F = \left(1+\left(\frac{\theta_c\,\sigma_z}{2\sigma^*}\right)^2\right)^{-1/2},
    \label{eq:lumi}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the Lorentz factor of the protons, $f_\text{rev}$ is the revolution frequency, $n_b$ is the number of bunches per beam, $N_b$ is the number of protons per bunch, $\varepsilon_n$ is the normalized transverse emittance, and $\beta^*$ is the value of the beta function at the interaction point, which characterizes the degree of beam focusing. The factor $F$ accounts for the geometric reduction due to the crossing angle $\theta_c$, and depends on the transverse $\sigma^*$ and longitudinal $\sigma_z$ sizes of the bunches.

Equation \ref{eq:lumi} shows the ways to increase the luminosity. First, the quadratic dependence on $N_b$ makes the number of protons per bunch the most effective parameter, and this, together with the emittance, is determined by the injection chain described in Section \ref{subsec:LHC_complex}. Second, $\beta^*$ can be reduced by focusing the beams just before the interaction point through the quadrupole magnets located on either side of each experiment. Finally, the number of bunches is limited by the $25$ ns spacing. However, with this spacing, the bunches of the two beams would also meet at points other than the interaction point and therefore the beams must cross at a small angle, which reduces the luminosity through the factor of $F$.\\

By integrating Equation \ref{eq:event_rate} over time, the total expected number of events for a process is $N=\sigma\int\mathcal{L}\,dt$. The integrated luminosity, typically expressed in fb$^{-1}$, is therefore the parameter that quantifies the size of the dataset used in an analysis, and its precise measurement is essential for converting a number of events into a cross section. In CMS, this measurement is performed using dedicated detectors, called luminometers, whose response is calibrated in van der Meer scans, in which the beams are shifted relative to one another to determine their overlap~\cite{CMS:2021xjt}. The precision achieved in each of the years analyzed in this thesis, at the percent level, is detailed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}.\\

% pileup
However, at high luminosity, there is not just a single proton-proton interaction per bunch crossing, but rather several simultaneous interactions, so that the average number of interactions per crossing is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \langle PU\rangle = \frac{\mathcal{L}_b\,\sigma_\text{inel}}{f_\text{rev}},
    \label{eq:pileup}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_b$ is the luminosity per bunch and $\sigma_\text{inel}$ is the proton-proton inelastic cross section. Under nominal conditions, $\langle PU\rangle$ is on the order of $25$. These additional interactions, known as \textit{pile-up}, are overwhelmingly low-momentum-transfer processes that overlap with the interaction of interest\footnote{Unlike the underlying event, which originates from the collision itself in which the hard interaction takes place, pile-up corresponds to independent pp collisions that coincide at the same bunch crossing.}, and to these are added those from previous and subsequent bunch crossings, whose signal persists in the detector electronics. Hence, pile-up affects the reconstruction of all objects in the event, and its mitigation is one of the central aspects of the algorithms described in Section \ref{sec:POR}. The $\langle PU\rangle$ distribution recorded by CMS during Run 3 is shown in Figure \ref{fig:pileup}.\\

In summary, the beam energy and luminosity together determine the reach of the LHC, since the former determines which processes are accessible and the latter determines how many events of each process are produced. However, the energy and luminosity values presented here correspond to the nominal design of the machine, whereas in practice, actual operating conditions have evolved over the various data-taking periods.

\subsection{Operation}
\label{subsec:LHC_operation}
%
In practice, the LHC has not operated continuously under its design conditions, but rather, its operation is organized into data-taking periods, called \textit{Runs}, separated by long shutdowns (LS) dedicated to maintenance and upgrades of both the machine and the experiments. Each of these shutdowns has made it possible to increase the collision energy or luminosity in preparation for the next period, so that operating conditions have evolved in steps since the commissioning of the LHC. The complete schedule, from Run 1 through the high-luminosity phase, is shown in Figure \ref{fig:lhc_schedule}.
%

    \label{fig:lhc_schedule}
\end{figure}
%
During Run 1, between 2010 and 2012, the LHC operated at an energy lower than its design energy, $3.5$ TeV per beam in 2010 and 2011 and $4$ TeV in 2012, as a result of the 2008 incident involving the electrical connections between superconducting magnets, which limited the current that could safely circulate through them. With a bunch spacing of $50$ ns apart, approximately $6$ fb$^{-1}$ were delivered to CMS at $7$ TeV and $23$ fb$^{-1}$ at $8$ TeV during this period — data that led to the discovery of the Higgs boson. The first long shutdown (LS1) between 2013 and 2014 was devoted primarily to consolidating these connections, which made it possible to increase the energy in Run 2, between 2015 and 2018, to $\sqrt{s}=13$ TeV, and to reduce the bunch spacing to the design value of $25$ ns. Throughout Run 2, the instantaneous luminosity exceeded the nominal value, reaching twice the design value, and CMS received a total of approximately $164$ fb$^{-1}$, of which $138$ fb$^{-1}$ were certified for physical analysis.

The second long shutdown (LS2), between 2019 and 2021, included upgrades to the injection chain, resulting in the configuration described in Section \ref{subsec:LHC_complex}, as well as upgrades to the experiments themselves. Thanks to these upgrades, Run 3, which began in 2022 at an energy of $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV, had beams with a higher number of protons per bunch, capable of producing an instantaneous luminosity greater than what the experiments can record without \textit{pile-up} degrading event reconstruction. For this reason the luminosity was leveled to mitigate the pile-up to the values shown in Figure \ref{fig:pileup}, extending the duration of each fill — the period during which the same beams circulate and collide in the LHC. The integrated luminosity delivered to CMS each year is shown in Figure \ref{fig:lumi_per_year}, where it can be seen that Run 3, which concluded in July 2026, provided a total of approximately $355$ fb$^{-1}$, more than double that of Run 1 and Run 2 combined. The analysis presented in this thesis uses data from 2022, 2023, and 2024, which correspond to a certified integrated luminosity of $172$ fb$^{-1}$.

With the end of Run 3, the third long shutdown, LS3, began. It is expected to last until mid-2030, during which time the LHC will be transformed into the High-Luminosity LHC (HL-LHC)~\cite{HLLHC-plan-2025}. Among other improvements, more powerful focusing quadrupoles — capable of further reducing $\beta^*$ — will be installed alongside ATLAS and CMS, and special radiofrequency cavities will rotate the bunches before the interaction point to compensate for the loss of luminosity due to the crossing angle. As a result, the HL-LHC will operate at a leveled luminosity between $5$ and $7.5$ times higher than the nominal value, with up to $200$ interactions per bunch crossing, and will accumulate between $3000$ and $4000$ fb$^{-1}$ by the end of tis operation, foreseen for 2041~\cite{ZurbanoFernandez:2020cco}. This data volume is an order of magnitude greater than what is currently available, and should make it possible to observe the production of Higgs boson pairs, as discussed in Chapter \ref{ch:theory}, but in turn requires a major overhaul of the experiments to cope with the new conditions of pile-up and radiation.\\\\

The collisions produced by the LHC are detected at the four interaction points of the ring where the experiments are located. The A Toroidal LHC ApparatuS (ATLAS) and the Compact Muon Solenoid (CMS) experiments are situated at two diametrically opposite points and receive the highest instantaneous collision luminosity. These are the two general-purpose experiments, designed to cover a broad and largely shared physics program, which allows their results to be cross-checked and combined. The LHC beauty (LHCb) experiment focuses on the study of CP violation and matter-antimatter asymmetry through the physics of hadrons containing $b$-quarks. A Large Ion Collider Experiment (ALICE) focuses primarily on heavy-ion collisions and quark-gluon plasma. The data analyzed in this thesis were recorded by the CMS experiment, whose design and operation are described in the following section.



\section{The CMS Experiment}
\label{sec:CMS}
%
The CMS experiment is located approximately $100$ meters underground at Point 5 of the LHC and is one of the two general-purpose experiments. As such, it must be capable of identifying and accurately measuring virtually all particles produced in collisions in order to provide the broadest possible coverage of the potential manifestations of the physics of interest. Consequently, the design requirements for CMS were established based on the most demanding processes in the LHC program, particularly the search for the Higgs boson in its various decay channels. These requirements translate into excellent muon identification and momentum resolution, efficient and precise reconstruction of charged particle tracks, energy resolution for electrons and photons at the percent level, and nearly hermetic coverage, which is necessary to infer the presence of particles that do not interact with the detector based on the momentum balance in the transverse plane~\cite{CMS:2008xjf}.

The central element is the superconducting solenoid, which generates a powerful magnetic field of $3.8$ T, and both the tracker system and the calorimeters are housed inside it — so that the energy of the particles is measured before they pass through the material of the magnet — while the muon system is housed in the steel flux-return yoke outside the solenoid. The result is a remarkably compact detector given the strength of its magnetic field, with a length of $22$ m, a diameter of $15$ m, and a weight of about $14000$ t. Since its commissioning, the detector has been upgraded during successive long shutdowns to adapt to the increase in luminosity and pile-up described in Section \ref{subsec:LHC_operation}, and the configuration used to record Run 3 data is described in detail in Reference~\cite{CMS:2023gfb}.

This section first describes the coordinate system used in CMS and the kinematic variables derived from it, which are used throughout the thesis. Next, the various subdetectors of the experiment are presented, starting from the interaction point and moving outward. Finally, the trigger system, which plays a crucial role in selecting in real time the events to be stored, is described, with special attention to the new features introduced during Run 3.


\subsection{Coordinate system}
\label{subsec:CMS_coordinates}
%
To describe the particles produced in collisions, it is necessary to define a reference frame common to all the subdetectors that make up the experiment. CMS adopts a reference frame with its origin at the nominal interaction point, which is located at the center of the detector, and in which the $x$ axis points radially toward the center of the LHC ring, the $y$ axis points vertically upward, and the $z$ axis is aligned along the beam direction, so that the resulting system is right-handed~\cite{CMS:2008xjf}. However, given the cylindrical symmetry of the detector around the beam, it is more natural to describe the directions using angular coordinates: the azimuthal angle $\phi$ is measured from $x$ axis in the the $x$-$y$ plane perpendicular to the beam, known as the transverse plane; the radial distance $r$ is defined in that same plane; and the polar angle $\theta$ is measured from the $z$ axis, as shown in the left panel of Figure \ref{fig:cms_coordinates}.


%
Nevertheless, in a hadron collider, the interacting partons carry different fractions of the momentum of their protons, so that the colliding system moves along the $z$-axis with an unknown longitudinal momentum that varies from one event to another. Consequently, it is preferable to use variables whose behavior does not depend on that motion, and therefore the polar angle is replaced by the rapidity
\begin{equation}
    y = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{E+p_z}{E-p_z},
    \label{eq:rapidity}
\end{equation}
whose differences between particles are invariant under Lorentz transformations along the $z$-axis. For particles whose mass is negligible compared to their momenta, the rapidity coincides with the pseudorapidity,
\begin{equation}
    \eta = -\ln\left[\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right],
    \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
which has the advantage of depending solely on the direction of the particle and, therefore, of being measurable directly from the polar angle. Pseudorapidity takes the value $\eta=0$ in the direction perpendicular to the beam and increases in absolute value as the direction approaches the beam, as illustrated in the right panel of Figure \ref{fig:cms_coordinates}, so that the coverage of each subdetector is typically expressed as an interval in $|\eta|$. The angular distance between two objects is defined in terms of $\eta$ and $\phi$,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta R = \sqrt{(\Delta\eta)^2+(\Delta\phi)^2},
    \label{eq:deltaR}
\end{equation}
which inherits the invariance of $\eta$ under longitudinal boosts and is used to define conical regions around an object, such as those used in jet clustering or in the definition of isolation variables.\\

Furthermore, since the colliding partons have virtually zero transverse momentum, by the conservation of momentum the vector sum of the momenta of all particles in the final state must be zero in the transverse plane. Consequently, the transverse momentum, $p_T=\sqrt{p_x^2+p_y^2}$, is used to describe the kinematics of the reconstructed objects, and any imbalance in the transverse plane reveals the presence of particles that escape detection, such as neutrinos. From this, the missing transverse momentum, $\vec{p}_T^{\,\text{miss}}$, is inferred, and its reconstruction is described in Section \ref{sec:MET}. No equivalent balance can be exploited in the longitudinal direction, since the initial momentum of the partons along the beam is unknown. The longitudinal momentum of each object is nevertheless recovered from $p_T$ and $\eta$, so that the set $(p_T,\eta,\phi)$, together with the mass of the object, fully describes its four-momentum and constitutes the parametrization used throughout this thesis.

Given that momentum balance can only be established in the transverse plane, the variables used to characterize an event are typically constructed from quantities defined within that plane, so that they do not depend on the unknown longitudinal dynamics of the colliding system. This is particularly useful when a particle decays into a final state that includes neutrinos — such as in the decay of a $W$ boson into a lepton and a neutrino — whose invariant mass cannot be reconstructed, and the transverse mass is used instead. Since the masses of the lepton and the neutrino are negligible compared to their momenta, the transverse mass, $m_T$, can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    m_T = \sqrt{2\,p_T\,p_T^\text{miss}\left(1-\cos\Delta\phi\right)},
    \label{eq:mT}
\end{equation}
where $p_T$ is the transverse momentum of the visible object and $\Delta\phi$ is its azimuthal difference from $\vec{p}_T^{\,\text{miss}}$, and from whose distribution this type of event can be identified, as is done in Chapters \ref{ch:Tau} and \ref{ch:bbtt_analysis}. Finally, the hadronic activity of the event is quantified by $H_T$, defined as the scalar sum of the $p_T$ values of the jets in the event.

\subsection{Detector layout}
\label{subsec:CMS_detector}
%
The particles produced in the collisions interact with matter in different ways, depending on their nature. Therefore, CMS is structured in concentric layers around the interaction point, each specialized in a specific type of interaction, and it is the combination of all the signals collected in the different layers that allows for the determination of the identity of each particle. The arrangement of the various subdetectors is shown in Figure \ref{fig:cms_schematic}. From the inside out, the tracker system, the electromagnetic calorimeter, and the hadron calorimeter are located inside the solenoid; outside the solenoid, the muon system is embedded in the steel flux-return yoke. Each of these subsystems is in turn divided into a central cylindrical section, called the \textit{barrel}, closed at both ends by two flat sections, called \textit{endcaps}, which together cover the region $|\eta|<2.5$ for the tracker, $|\eta|<2.4$ for the muon system, and $|\eta|<3.0$ for the calorimeters, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:cms_quadrant}. An additional subsystem located symmetrically on both sides of the detector, the forward calorimeter, extends this coverage up to $|\eta|=5.2$, ensuring the necessary hermeticity for the measurement of $p_T^\text{miss}$~\cite{CMS:2008xjf,CMS:2023gfb}.

The tracker is the layer of the detector closest to the interaction point, and its function is to reconstruct the trajectories of the charged particles, whose curvature in the magnetic field determines their momentum, as well as the vertices from which they originate. Given the large number of charged particles that traverse the detector in each bunch crossing, this system requires very fine granularity and rapid response, while, at the same time, it must incorporate minimal material to avoid disturbing the particles before they reach the calorimeters, and it must also be able to withstand the high level of radiation near the beam. To meet these requirements, silicon detectors are used, organized in turn into two subsystems. The innermost one, the pixel detector, has the finest granularity of the entire detector, allowing it to resolve trajectories in the region of highest particle density and determining the precision with which the vertices are reconstructed. Next is the silicon strip detector, which covers a much larger volume and provides the long lever arm needed for a precise measurement of the track curvature. Taken together, the tracker is capable of reconstructing particles with $p_T$ as low as $50$ MeV, with a resolution in $p_T$ close to $1\%$ and a resolution in the impact parameter\footnote{Defined as the minimum distance between the extrapolated track and the primary vertex.} of about $10$ $\mu$m for $100$ GeV tracks in the central region. It is this resolution that allows for the identification of the displaced vertices characteristic of $b$-hadron and $\tau$-lepton decays.

The next layer is the electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL), where electrons and photons deposit virtually all of their energy through electromagnetic showers. The ECAL consists of $75848$ lead tungstate (PbWO$_4$) scintillator crystals, which allow these showers to be contained within a volume compatible with the available space inside the solenoid while simultaneously separating closely spaced particles. The crystals cover the region $|\eta|<1.479$ in the barrel (EB) and $1.479<|\eta|<3.0$ in the endcaps (EE), in front of which there is also a \textit{preshower} detector with higher granularity. By combining the energy measured in the ECAL with the trajectory reconstructed in the tracker, the resolution in momentum for electrons originating from $Z\to e^+e^-$ decays, with $p_T\approx45$ GeV, ranges between $1.6$ and $5\%$, depending on the region of the detector and the amount of bremsstrahlung emitted by the electron before reaching the calorimeter. As irradiation progressively reduces the transparency of the crystals, their response is continuously monitored using a laser monitoring system and corrected accordingly.

The Hadron Calorimeter (HCAL) is responsible for measuring the energy of charged and neutral hadrons, thereby playing a critical role in the reconstruction of jets and $p_T^\text{miss}$. Since hadronic showers are much more extensive than electromagnetic ones, the HCAL is a sampling calorimeter in which layers of dense absorbing material — where the showers takes place — alternate with layers of scintillator material, which measure the fraction of the energy deposited in them. It consists of the barrel (HB), which covers the region $|\eta|<1.392$, and the endcaps (HE), which cover $1.305<|\eta|<3.0$. In the region of lower $|\eta|$, where the thickness of the material traversed by the particles is minimal, the highest-energy showers may not be completely contained within the space between the ECAL and the solenoid. The HB is therefore supplemented by an outer calorimeter (HO), located outside the magnet and covering $|\eta|<1.26$. Finally, the forward calorimeter (HF), located $11.5$ m from the interaction point, cover the region $3.0<|\eta|<5.2$, where the particle density per unit area is much higher than in the rest of the detector, and consequently, they are built using technology that is particularly resistant to radiation. The HF also constitutes one of the luminometers mentioned in Section \ref{subsec:LHC_design}. When the information from the entire detector is combined, the jet energy resolution is $15$–$20\%$ at $30$ GeV, $10\%$ at $100$ GeV, and $5\%$ at $1$ TeV.

Finally, muons are the only charged particles capable of passing through the calorimeters and the solenoid without being absorbed, so their detection in the outermost layers of the detector constitutes a practically unambiguous identification. The muon system is situated within the iron of the return yoke, where the magnetic field once again curves their trajectories. These trajectories are recorded in muon chambers based on different types of gaseous detectors depending on the particle flux and magnetic field conditions in each region. Thus, in the barrel, where the flux is low and the field is uniform, drift tube (DT) chambers are used, covering $|\eta|<1.2$. In the endcaps, by contrast, the flux is higher and the field is non-uniform, and therefore cathode strip chambers (CSCs) are employed, as these are faster and have finer segmentation, covering $0.9<|\eta|<2.4$. Both systems are complemented by resistive plate chambers (RPCs) placed at $|\eta|<1.9$, whose excellent time resolution allows each muon to be unambiguously assigned to the bunch crossing in which it was produced — information that is essential for the trigger system, as will be seen in Section \ref{subsec:TRG}. In addition, during LS2, gas electron multiplier (GEM) chambers were installed in the endcaps, covering $1.55<|\eta|<2.18$ and enhancing the trigger capability in the region of highest flux. By combining the trajectory reconstructed in the tracker system with that recorded in the muon system, the relative resolution in $p_T$ for the muons is $1\%$ in the barrel and $3\%$ in the endcaps for $p_T$ up to $100$ GeV, and remains below $7\%$ in the barrel up to $1$ TeV~\cite{CMS:2018rym}.\\

Taken together, the information from all the subdetectors allows for the complete reconstruction of each event, as will be detailed in Section \ref{sec:POR}. However, the high collision rate makes it impossible to store this information for all of them. To address this limitation, a trigger system capable of selecting in real time the events worth preserving is used, and is described below.



\subsection{Trigger System}
\label{subsec:TRG}
%
As mentioned, bunches cross intersect at the center of the detector every $25$ ns, which corresponds to a frequency of up to $40$ MHz. Since a full detector readout generates about $1$ MB of data per event, storing all collisions would result in a data rate of about $40$ TB per second — far exceeding the few GB per second that can be written to disk and subsequently processed. Under these conditions, it is necessary to implement a system capable of distinguishing in real time between events of interest and those that can be discarded, in order to reduce the event rate by a factor of approximately $10^4$. This selection is performed by the trigger system, which consists of two successive levels: a first level implemented in hardware, Level-1 (L1), which reduces the rate from $40$ MHz to about $110$ kHz, and a second level implemented in software, the High-Level Trigger (HLT), which reduces it to a few kHz~\cite{CMS:2016ngn,CMS:2023gfb}. Since events discarded at either level are irreversibly lost, the trigger selection criteria set an upper bound on the acceptance for any analysis.\\


The L1 makes decisions within a fixed time of about $4$ $\mu$s, during which the full detector data is temporarily stored in the readout electronics. This constraint requires implementing the trigger on dedicated electronic processors and basing the decision solely on coarse-granularity information from the calorimeters and the muon system, since track reconstruction in the tracker system is too slow for this level. Thus, the calorimeter trigger is responsible for constructing candidates for electrons and photons, jets, and hadronic $\tau$ leptons, along with energy sums such as $H_T$ and $p_T^\text{miss}$, while the muon trigger combines information from the various chambers to construct muon candidates and estimate their $p_T$. All these candidates are sent to the global trigger, which compares them against a set of several hundred selection algorithms — referred to as the \textit{menu} — in which each algorithm imposes requirements on the multiplicity, kinematics, and topology of the objects; the event is accepted if at least one of these algorithms is satisfied. For algorithms with lower thresholds — for which the event rate is too high — a \textit{prescale} factor is applied, allowing only a fraction of the events that satisfy them to be retained. This makes it possible to keep useful algorithms in the menu, such as those used for measuring efficiencies or for detector calibration, and to adjust their contribution throughout each fill as the luminosity decreases~\cite{CMS:2020cmk}. During Run 3, the L1 hardware has not undergone any major changes, but the logic programmed into its processors has been expanded with new algorithms — some of which are based on machine learning techniques — and information from the new GEM chambers has been incorporated~\cite{CMS:2023gfb}.\\

Reducing the $110$ kHz rate further requires the complete detector information, not just the limited information available at L1. Once an event is accepted by L1, the readout electronics of each subdetector send the stored data to the data acquisition system, where the fragments from all subdetectors are assembled into the complete event and transferred to the HLT. The HLT is a computer cluster that bases its decisions on a version of the reconstruction derived from the same software used later for the data, but optimized for speed. This allows for the application of criteria much closer to those used in analysis — such as lepton isolation, their association with the primary vertex, or the jet flavor identification — and thus reduces the event rate by a further factor of about $50$ without losing events of interest. The selection is organized into sequences of algorithms, called \textit{paths}, each of which reconstructs the necessary objects and applies criteria of increasing complexity to them: the steps that require only information from the calorimeters and the muon system are executed first, while track reconstruction is reserved for events that have passed the previous steps. The accepted events are finally grouped into primary datasets according to the type of \textit{path} that selected them, so that, for example, those selected by \textit{paths} requiring an isolated muon form the \texttt{Muon} dataset, while those selected by \textit{paths} based on hadronic $\tau$ leptons form the \texttt{Tau} dataset.

In the standard selection process, the reconstruction performed in the HLT is used only to determine whether an event is accepted, but what is actually stored is the complete, unprocessed detector data, which is transferred to the CERN computing center for final reconstruction, where more precise calibrations than those available in real time are used. Although the HLT drastically reduces the number of events, the number of events that can be preserved in this way remains limited by two distinct factors: the total volume of data that can be written to disk — which depends on the size of each event — and the computing capacity available to reconstruct them immediately.

To expand the available dataset without exceeding these limits, CMS employs two strategies that complement the standard selection process. In the first, called \textit{scouting}, only the information on the objects reconstructed by the HLT itself is stored instead of the complete detector data, so that the size of each event is reduced from about $1$ MB to about $13$ kB, allowing events to be recorded at much higher rates and with lower thresholds. In the second strategy, called \textit{parking}, the complete data are stored, but reconstruction is not performed immediately; rather, it is postponed until sufficient computational resources are available, typically during technical stops or after the end of each data-taking year. This strategy is particularly useful because the luminosity decreases throughout each fill as protons are consumed in collisions, and as a result the rate of events that pass the standard selection decreases. The remaining bandwidth is then used by progressively relaxing the thresholds of the \textit{parking} paths~\cite {CMS:2023gfb}.\\

Among the advancements made during Run 3, graphics processing units (GPUs) were added to the HLT cluster, which reduced the processing time per event by approximately $40\%$. Furthermore, for the first time in the HLT, identification algorithms based on neural networks, such as \textsc{ParticleNet}, are used, which classify jets according to their origin, including those originating from $b$ quarks and hadronic $\tau$ leptons. Figure \ref{fig:hlt_btag} shows how these algorithms substantially reduce the fraction of light quark or gluon jets misidentified compared to those used in Run 2, making it possible to require the presence of $b$ jets already in the trigger while maintaining an acceptable rate. An example directly relevant to this thesis is the trigger dedicated to the production of Higgs boson pairs introduced in 2023, which requires at least four jets, a minimum value of $H_T$, and that the two jets with the highest \textsc{ParticleNet} scores be consistent with originating from $b$ quarks, and is recorded through the \textit{parking} strategy. For the $bb\tau_h\tau_h$ final state, this trigger alone achieves an efficiency of $43\%$, higher than the $34\%$ achieved by triggers based on hadronic $\tau$ leptons, and its combination with these triggers and with those based on $p_T^\text{miss}$ raises the efficiency to $58\%$, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:hlt_hh}. In 2024, a variant was incorporated into this same data stream that replaces one of the $b$-jet identification requirements with the identification of a $\tau_h$, also using \textsc{ParticleNet}. This is the variant used in the analysis presented in this thesis, and its role in the trigger strategy is described in Section \ref{sec:triggers}.\\\\

To conclude, the CMS detector consists of a series of subdetectors arranged in concentric layers around the interaction point, each specialized in a specific type of interaction, with the goal of recording virtually all the particles produced in the collisions. Since it is not possible to store the information from every collision, the detector is complemented by a trigger system that decides in real time which events are kept, through a sequence of decisions designed to preserve those of physics interest without exceeding the available capacity for recording and processing. However, the fast reconstruction performed in the HLT serves only to take this decision, and what is stored for each selected event is the raw detector output, i.e., the set of signals left by the particles as they traverse the various subdetectors, and is the combination of all these signals that allows the nature of the particle to be determined. To interpret the complete event, it is therefore necessary to combine the information from the entire detector, with the most precise calibrations available, and translate it into the physics objects that make up the final state — electrons, muons, hadronic $\tau$ leptons, jets, and missing transverse momentum — a task carried out by the reconstruction algorithms described in the following section.





\section{Physics object reconstruction}
\label{sec:POR}
%
Cada tipo de partícula deja en CMS una firma característica de acuerdo a cómo interacciona con las sucesivas capas del detector. Los muones atraviesan todo el detector perdiendo una fracción muy pequeña de su energía, dejando plasmada su trayectoria tanto en el tracker como en las muon chambers, curvada en sentidos opuestos dentro y fuera del solenoide. Los electrones pasan igualmente por el tracker dejando ahí su trayectoria, pero son absorbidos antes de salir del detector en el calorímetro electromagnético. Para el caso de los fotones, en cambio, al ser neutros no interactuan con el tracker, pero son igualmente absorbidos en el ECAL. Por otro lado, los hadrones cargados dejan también una trayectoria y depositan su energía principalmente en el calorímetro hadrónico, donde son absorbidos, mientras que los hadronies neutros solo se manifiestan a través de este último depósito. Por último, los neutrinos atraviesan todo el material sin interaccionar, por lo que su presencia solo puede inverirse a partir del desequilibrio de momento en el plano transverso, como ya se ha explicado. La identidad de cada partícula queda así codificada en la combinación de subdetectores en los que deja señal, como puede verse representado gráficamente en la Figura \ref{fig:cms_slice}.

    \centering

El algoritmo de reconstrucción empleado por CMS, \textit{Particle Flow} (PF), explota estas firmas para identificar y reconstruir individualmente cada una de las partículas del estado final — muones, electrones, fotones y hadrones cargados y neutros — combinando de forma óptima la información de todas las capas~\cite{CMS:2017yfk}. La ventaja de esta reconstrucción por capas es que cada partícula se mide con el subdetector más preciso para ella, y que las señales de las distintas partículas se pueden separar, lo que resulta especialmente relevante en los jets. En jet típico, alrededor del $65\%$ de su energía es transportada por hadrones cargados, cuyo momento se mide en el sistema de trazado con una resolución muy superior a la del HCAL; en torno al $25\%$ corresponde a fotones, que son medidos con gran precisión en el ECAL; el $10\%$ restante lo transportan los hadrones neutros, y es únicamente esta parte la que depende de la medida del HCAL, cuya resolución es sensiblmente peor. El intenso campo magnético, que separa los depósitos de energía de las partículas cargadas de los de las neutras, junto con la fina granularidad del sistema de trazado y del calorímetro electromagnético, permite aprovechar esta complementariedad, explotando los puntos fuertes de las diferentes capas. A las partículas reconstruidas de esta forma se les denomina candidatos PF, y a partir de ellas se construyen posteriormente los objetos de más alto nivel, como los jets, los leptones $\tau$ hadrónicos o el momento transverso faltante.


Para formar los candidatos PF, el algorimo opera sobre elementos que se reconstruyen previamente, de forma independiente a partir de la información de cada subdetector. Las trayectorias de las partículas cargadas en el tracker, los \textit{clusters} de energía en los calorímetros y las trayectorias en el sistema de muones, cuya reconstrucción se describe en la Sección \ref{subsec:Muons}. De todos ellos, la reconstrucción de las trayectorias en el tracker es la más exigente, dado el elevado número de partículas cargadas que lo atraviesan en cada bunch crossing, y se realiza mediante un proceso iterativo en el que cada iteración busca trayectorias con criterios progresivamente menos restrictivos. 


Para establecer el punto de origen de la interacción principal y distinguirlo de las de pile-up se recurre precisamente a las trayectorias reconstruidas, agrupandolas según su posición a lo largo del eje del haz y ajustando la posición de cada grupo para obtener los vértices de interacción~\cite{CMS:2014pgm}. De entre ellos, se toma como vértice primaro el correspondiente a la interacción más energética del suceso, evaluada únicamente a partir de la información del sistema de trazado~\cite{CMS-TDR-15-02}. El resto de vértices se atribuyen a interacciones de pile-up, y la asociación de cada partícula cargada a uno u otro vértice constituye la base de las técnicas de mitigación del pile-up descritas en la Sección \ref{subsec:Jets}.

Una vez reconstruidos todos estos elementos se conectan mediante un algoritmo de enlace en función de su compatibilidad espacial, por ejemplo, asociando a cada trayectoria los \textit{clusters} de los calorímetros alcanzados por su extrapolación, o la trayectoria reconstruida en el sistema de muones con la del sistema de trazado. Los elementos así conectados forman bloques, y dentro de cada uno las partículas se asignan en orden según cuál presenta una identificación más clara. Así, en primer lugar se identifican los muones, seguidos de los electrones, junto con los fotones de radiación de freando que estos emiten, y de los fotones aislados. Las trayectorias restantes se identifican como hadrones cargados, cuya energía se determina comparando su momento con la energía de los \textit{clusters} asociados, y cuando la energía depositada en los calorímetros supera significativamente a la esperada a partir de las trayectorias, el exceso se interpreta como un fotón, si se encuentra en el calorímetro electromagnético, o como un hadrón neutro, si se encuentra en el hadrónico~\cite{CMS:2017yfk}.


Los mismos algoritmos de reconstrucción se aplican a los sucesos simulados, en los que la respuesta del detector a las partículas generadas se modela mediante \textsc{Geant4}~\cite{GEANT4:2002zbu} y a los que se superponen interacciones adicionales para reproducir las condiciones de pile-up, de forma que datos y simulación se procesan de manera idéntica. A continuación se describe la reconstrucción de cada uno de los objetos que intervienen en el estado final del análisis presentado en esta tesis.

\subsubsection{Hadronic \texorpdfstring{$\tau$}{τ} leptons}
\label{subsec:Taus}
\noindent The $\tau$-lepton is the heaviest lepton in the SM, with a mass of $1.777$ GeV. It is the only lepton that can decay both leptonically and hadronically, with a short lifetime of $2.9\times 10^{-13}$ s. Leptonic decays account for about $35\%$ of the $\tau$-lepton decay width, while hadronic decays account for approximately $64.8\%$, as shown in Table \ref{tab:tau_decays}, which reports the corresponding branching fractions. In all decays, neutrinos remain undetected, and it is not possible to measure the momentum of the decaying $\tau$ with exact precision.

In the leptonic decay mode, the lepton is accompanied by two neutrinos and is reconstructed simply as the corresponding electron or muon following the standard procedures describes in the previous sections, along with the to neutrinos emitted along the $\tau$-lepton flight direction. While neutrinos do not leave a direct trace upon escaping the detector, they do generate a momentum imbalance in the transverse plane. By measuring the missing transverse momentum in the event, their presence can be inferred. Therefore, the main challenge in identifying such decays lies in reconstructing and measuring the missing transverse momentum (MET), which serves as an indirect measurement of neutrinos, as described in Section \ref{sec:MET}. In addition, the energy fraction of the decaying $\tau$-lepton carried by the electron or muon falls within a continuous spectrum, which makes it difficult to distinguish between leptonic $\tau$ decays and prompt leptons. These effects makes the identification and reconstruction of $\tau$ leptonic decay a particularly challenging task.

Nevertheless, since leptonic decays are reconstructed based on the corresponding light lepton, discussing the reconstruction of $\tau$ leptons implicitly refers to hadronic $\tau$ ($\tau_h$) reconstruction. Hadronic decay can occur either directly or via intermediate resonances such as $\rho$(770) or $a_1$(1260), resulting in a variety of charged and neutral hadrons. Due to charge conservation, the decay products include and odd number of charged hadrons, typically one or three, which are reconstructed as hadron jets and a neutrino $\nu_{\tau}$. Due to the high particle multiplicity in hadronic $\tau$ decays, a reliable reconstruction algorithm must be able to identify the PF candidates associated with the charged hadrons, as well as the photons originating from $\pi^0$ decay; group them to determine the decay mode; and determine the kinematic properties of the $\tau_h$. To meet these requirements, the reconstruction of hadronically decaying $\tau$ leptons is operated in CMS with the Hadron-Plus-Strips (HPS) algorithm {\color{red}[\ref{}]}.

The HPS algorithm is employed to identify and reconstruct $\tau_h$ candidates by verifying the compatibility of all PF jet candidates with a $\tau$-lepton. To this end, the HPS algorithm is seeded by the AK4 PF jet candidates reconstructed as in Section \ref{sec:Jet}, and for each seeding jet, all particles within a cone of radios $\Delta R<0.5$ around the jet axis are tested as hadron candidates or clusters of electron and photon candidates originating form neutral pions decays (photons from $\pi^0$ decays may convert into electron-positron pairs). These reconstructed objects are referred to as \textit{strips}, and their reconstruction is performed iteratively: candidates within the clustering region around each strip are progressively added, and the strip position is recomputed using a $p_T$-weighted average. The $\eta \times \varphi$ window is dynamically adjusted in each iteration to adapt to the strip $p_T$ and ensure optimal energy collection. This procedure is repeated until no further candidates are found. At this stage, the HPS algorithm combines both charged hadrons and strips inside the PF jet to form all the possible combinations of hadrons for the various decay modes (listed in Table \ref{tab:tau_decays}): h$^{\pm}$, h$^{\pm}\pi^0$, h$^{\pm}\pi^0\pi^0$, h$^{\pm}$h$^{\mp}$h$^{\pm}$ and h$^{\pm}$h$^{\mp}$h$^{\pm}\pi^0$ (plus the additional $\nu_{\tau}$). Each decay mode hypothesis includes additional quality and invariant mass requirements to ensure it aligns with a genuine $\tau_h$. Among all candidates within a seed region, the hadronic $\tau_h$ with the highest $p_T$ is selected.

One of the major challenges in the $\tau_h$ reconstruction lies in accurately distinguishing genuine $\tau$ leptons from misidentified objects, such as background events involving QCD multi-jet production. To enhance the purity of the hadronic $\tau$ reconstruction, a convolutional neural network (CNN), the \textsc{DeepTau} identification method {\color{red}[\ref{}]}, has been adopted in CMS analyses since the end of the LHC Run 2 as a tool to discriminate real $\tau_h$ from quark and gluon jets, as well as from electrons and muons. This machine learning (ML)-based algorithm integrates information from high-level reconstructed $\tau_h$ features, the decay mode extracted from the HPS algorithm and low-level data used to construct the PF candidates. The \textsc{DeepTau} algorithm defines three different discriminators, each targeting a specificy type of background: jets \textsc{DeepTauVSjet}, electrons \textsc{DeepTauVSe}, and muons \textsc{DeepTauVSmu}. Each discriminator has a different set of working points (WP). 

Although the origins of this identification method date back to Run 2, the \textsc{DeepTau} algorithm received major updates at the start of Run 3. This new version includes upgrades such as reducing discrepancies between data and simulation in regions of high hadronic purity by integrating domain adaptation techniques, and improving data handling, resulting in overall enhanced performance. The \textsc{DeepTau} algorithm has been shown to achieve a significant improvement in the identification and reconstruction of $\tau_h$ compared to previous algorithm. {\textcolor{red}{Figure illustrates this enhancement for the three discriminators.}}

%For example, for a given jet rejection level, the efficiency for genuine tau h candidates is increased by 10–30%.

A further advancement in Run 3 pertains to boosted topologies. When $\tau$ leptons are produced by highly boosted resonances, their decay products become collimated within a narrow cone, often forming a single jet, similar to the behavior observed for AK8 jets. While no dedicated reconstruction existed in Run 2, an extension of the HPS algorithm has been developed for Run 3 to target high-momentum candidates by exploiting the jet substructure of the $\tau_h$ candidates. The algorithm reverses the final step in the clustering sequence that forms the large-radius jet to identify two sub-jets that are compatible with the expected topology of boosted $\tau$ decay. Specific requirements are in place to suppress misidentification with quark-gluon jets, electrons or muons. This approach has been shown to outperform the standard HPS for highly boosted $\tau$ leptons while maintaining optimal misidentification rates. The selected boosted $\tau$ candidates are then passed to the HPS algorithm for standard $\tau$ reconstruction.


Hadronically decaying tau leptons are key elements for analysis involving complex final states, such as the $HH\rightarrow bb\tau\tau$ analysis, where robust $\tau$ reconstruction plays a central role. Accurate and reliable reconstruction and identification of $\tau$ leptons are essential, as the performance of the algorithms described in this section directly impacts the sensitivity to such signatures. Therefore, their validation becomes a crucial component of this work, which will focus on a detailed assessment of the identification algorithms used in Run-3 and its performance across the 2022-2026 data-taking periods in Chapter \ref{ch:Tau}.
