\chapter{Modeling gluon fusion Di-Higgs signal in EFT interpretations}
%HH for BSM searches
As repeatedly stressed in this these, searches for Higgs boson pairs are of critical importance, as they are directly related to the trilinear Higgs coupling parameter $\lambda$ and, consequently, to the underlying Higgs potential. The measurement of this parameter is essential in order to answer fundamental questions about the BEH mechanism, with implications for issues ranging from the nature of EWSB and the structure of the scalar sector to the stability of our Universe. The primary analysis of this thesis has centered on the SM framework to address these searches. However, Higgs boson pair production is a powerful tool for exploring new physics scenarios beyond the SM.

%BSM approaches and interpretation frameworks
In the context of a comprehensive theory, the search for new physics can be pursued through different approaches, each characterized by its own assumptions, advantages, and intrinsic limitations (see Sec. \ref{subsec:SM_limitations}). The strategy based on the direct search for new particles is usually formulated within explicit BSM scenarios; this approach is complicated by the large number of candidate models and the broad parameter space that each of them typically entails. A complementary strategy utilizes indirect evidence by performing precision measurements of SM observables to constrain the low-energy effects of heavy degrees of freedom, thereby gaining insight into the potential structure of ultraviolet physics (residing at scales far beyond the electroweak scale). In this sense, the setup is "model-independent", providing the most general parametrization of Nature based on observations at low energies. In this context, new physics is described through interpretive frameworks.

%BSM searches in HH
Accordingly, in the specific case of Higgs boson pair production, two categories of new signatures are typically considered in experimental searches for BSM physics, as outlined in Sec. \ref{subsec:BSM_production}. In the first scenario, a new, relatively light degree of freedom is expected to be exchanged and decay resonantly into a Higgs boson pair, allowing for direct observation of the new physics. The other type of signature corresponds to non-resonant Higgs boson pair production, where deviations from the SM predictions are parameterized in terms of operators and Wilson coefficients within an Effective Field Theory framework.

%BSM effects
In the field of indirect interpretations, the phenomenology of non-resonant Higgs boson pair production (covered in Sec. \ref{subsec:BSM_production}) implies that new physics could manifest as modifications to the interactions of the Higgs and the fields involved in its production, altering both the total rate of the process and its kinematics, leading to experimental signatures that may differ substantially from SM predictions. This dual sensitivity makes HH searches informative even in the absence of observation, but it is also what makes their interpretation challenging: to effectively constrain new physics, it is required to predict both the rate and the shape of the signal for every hypothesis under testing.

% effective coupling formalism
Among the various HH production mechanisms, gluon fusion plays a central role as the dominant channel at LHC energies. As the process is governed by the interference between diagrams involving the Higgs boson self-coupling and the Yukawa coupling, possible deviations from the SM can significantly alter this production process. At the phenomenological level, these modifications can be described by a reduced set of effective couplings that capture the leading effects in gluon fusion — $\kappa_{\lambda}$, $\kappa_t$, $c_2$, $c_g$ and $c_{2g}$ — as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:BSM_diagrams}. Therefore, each of these couplings parameters has a characteristic effect on the amplitude of the process, altering both the rate and the distribution of key observables, such as the invariant mass $m_{HH}$ .

These effective couplings can therefore be understood as a low-energy manifestation of an EFT. The manner in which they interact with one another — or remain independent — is determined by the formalism adopted to construct the theory, which directly affects the interpretation of experimental results and the energy range within which the description remains valid.

% Use and limitations of signal modeling
This formulation results in a continuous, multidimensional parameter space, which is both a blessing and a curse for experimentalists as it allows for the extraction of a multitude of results within a single analysis but also heavily increases the burden of modeling various signal topologies. Accordingly, the statistical interpretation of the results becomes unfeasible unless the signal can be modeled. This phenomenological richness necessitates a formulation capable of coherently predicting the behavior of process at any point in EFT space to save the computational budget required to simulate a large number of signal samples for different parameter combinations.

% matrix inversion
In response to this challenge, experimentalists have explored various methods. Among them, the so-called "matrix inversion method" has been the focus of extensive study by both ATLAS and CMS, as it has been employed in several analysis \cite{ATLAS:2024pov,CMS_combination}. This method elegantly simplifies the problem by allowing the signal to be modeled for a wide variety of parameter combinations by simulating only a small number of signal samples that can be linearly combined to reconstruct the signal at any point in EFT space. However, this approach presents inherent challenges, especially in high-dimensional cases such as those attempted to be addressed. Therefore, although the method is not newly developed, its systematic application to high-dimensional spaces and its coherent integration into broad EFT frameworks remains an open task and requires optimized development to correctly describe higher-dimensional spaces.

The aim of this chapter is to further refine the method to expand the coverage beyond what was shown in previous ATLAS and CMS results \cite{ATLAS:2025tkn,CMS_combination}, which constitutes the main original contribution of this thesis to the BSM interpretation of HH searches. The chapter is organized as follows: the first section presents a detailed discussion of the two effective descriptions used in the HH context: the Higgs EFT (HEFT) and the Standar Model EFT (SMEFT). \section{EFT frameworks for \texorpdfstring{$gg\to HH$}{gg → HH} production}
\label{subsec:EFTs}
% On the other hand, any known UV model (residing at scales well beyond

When studying new physics that has not yet been covered, rather than dealing with the vast amount of possible extensions to the SM, the idea behind the EFT approach is to assume that a specifc structure of any BSM physics can be integrated into the SM, resulting in effective couplings that are accessible at currently tested energies. In this manner, a substantial theory space can be subject to experimental constraints by establishing limits on the effective couplings that embody the low-energy effects of new physics as observed in collider data.

There is no single way to formulate an EFT description. In the context of this thesis, the central issue in formulating the EFT is the nature of the observed scalar and its role in EWSB. According to the SM, the physical Higgs boson and the three Goldsonte bosons — absorbed by th $W$ and $Z$ bosons — form a single $SU(2)_L$ doublet, as seen in equation \ref{eq:????}. However, the SM theory assumes the Higgs to be the fourth component of the doublet, whereas the only experimentally established facts are the existence of the three Goldstone bosons and a light neutral scalar with mass $m_H \simeq 125$~GeV and couplings compatible with the SM. The determination of whether this scalar is indeed the fourth component of the same doublet, or if it is instead a light, SM-like electroweak single, remains a hypothesis rather than a measurement. From these two possibilities arise the two internally consistent, gauge-invariant effective descriptions most commonly used in HH searches: HEFT, which adopts the more agnostic hypothesis where the physical Higgs boson is treated as an electroweak singlet; and SMEFT, which maintains the Higgs doublet structure, leading to a more restrictive hypothesis.

The EFT formalism for Higgs boson pair production has already been briefly introduced in Section \ref{subsec:BSM_production}, along with the set of five effective couplings $\kappa_{\lambda}$, $\kappa_t$, $c_2$, $c_g$ and $c_{2g}$ that govern HH production via gluon fusion at leading order (see Fig~\ref{fig:BSM_diagrams}), and whose contribution can significantly alter the total cross section and the kinematic characteristics of HH events. Therefore, while HEFT treats these parameters as free, in SMEFT they are correlated by the structure of the doublet. The following section aims to provide a detailed description of the two EFTs used, specifying the higher-dimensional operators that supplement the SM Lagrangian in each of the formalisms and the characteristics that emerge from them.

The generality of these EFT frameworks and the expanding coverage of BSM scenarios, as experimental HH analysis evolves, introduce a high dimensionality in the associated parameter space to be addressed. In this context, the cross section and kinematic distributions exhibit non-trivial variations due to interference between topologies. Therefore, covering the full space by predicting the signal at an arbitrary point in such space, as required for a statistical interpretation, cannot be accomplished through direct simulation. This motivates the development of modeling techniques, which will be discussed in the remainder of this chapter.

\subsection{Higgs EFT}
\label{subsec:HEFT}
% https://arxiv.org/pdf/1212.3305
%\paragraph{A Higgs boson outside the doublet}
Higgs EFT provides the most general effective description, as it does not assume that the physical Higgs is part of the $SU(2)_L$ linear doublet, as postulated in the SM. The HEFT approach treats the Higgs as a generic scalar singlet, independent of the Goldstone bosons that give mass to the $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons. Under this assumption, the SM Higgs particle becomes compatible with numerous new physics scenarios, such as composite Higgs \cite{Kaplan:1983fs,Agashe:2004rs}, pseudo-Goldstone \cite{Kaplan:1983sm,Contino:2010mh}, or dilaton models \cite{Goldberger:2008zz,Chacko:2012sy}. These models feature a strongly interacting sector at the TeV scale that generates a light scalar resonance in the low-energy spectrum, while they may correct the strength of SM couplings at lower energies, which appears promising in the absence of new resonances in LHC data.

This approach, which does not refer to a specific model, describes the three longitudinal degrees of freedom associated with the electroweak gauge bosons at low energy through the dimensionless matrix $U(x)$, which encodes the spontaneous breaking of electroweak symmetry. The fact that $U(x)$ is dimensionless enables the construction of an effective theory in which gauge symmetry is non-linearly realized. This results in Higgs couplings to fermions and gauge bosons, as well as vertices involving one or two Higgs bosons, that are not correlated in the same way as in the linear regime of the SM or SMEFT. As a consequence, the resulting Lagrangian contains interaction terms of arbitrary canonical dimension, and its expansion has to be organized by a different criterion.

% conteo chiral - https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP09(2018)057
% Therefore, the EFT expansion is not organized according to the canonical dimension of the operators, as is the case in the SM and SMEFT, where the contribution of new operators is suppressed by powers of $1/\Lambda$. Instead, in HEFT, the effective expansion is organized in terms of chiral dimensions, such that operators are classified according to the loop order at which they can first be generated chiral dimension one is assigned to derivatives, fermion bilinears and weak couplings, while gauge and scalar fields carry chiral dimension zero~\cite{Buchalla:2018yce}. Since $U(x)$ is dimensionless and the Higgs fields cost nothing in this counting, arbitrary polynomial dependencies on $h/v$ are admitted at a given chiral order, where $h$ is the Higgs field and $v$ the electroweak vacuum expectation value, and every power of $h$ therefore enters on the same footing. This allows vertices such as $ggH$, $ggHH$, or $t\bar{t}HH$ — which in SMEFT are correlated with one another — to be governed by independent parameters in HEFT.


% https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP09(2018)057
The required criterion is provided by chiral counting, equivalent to an expansion in loop orders $L$, implemented by assigning chiral dimensions $d_\chi\equiv 2L+2$ to fields and weak couplings~\cite{Buchalla:2013rka,Buchalla:2013eza}:
%
\begin{equation}
    d_\chi(A_\mu,\varphi,h) = 0\;, \qquad
    d_\chi(\partial,\bar{\psi}\psi,g,y) = 1\;,
\label{eq:chiral_dim}
\end{equation}
%
where $A_\mu$ denotes a generic gauge field, $\varphi$ the Goldstone bosons, $h$ the physical Higgs boson, and $g$ and $y$ any of the SM gauge couplings and Yukawa couplings respectively. Derivatives, fermion bilinears and weak couplings therefore carry chiral dimension one, while gauge and scalar fields — the Higgs boson among them — carry chiral dimension zero. Operators are thus classified according to the loop order at which they can first be generated, and the Lagrangian takes the form of a series
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{d_\chi} = \mathcal{L}_{(d_\chi=2)}
    + \sum_{L=1}^{\infty}\sum_i \left(\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\right)^{L} c_i^{(L)}\,O_i^{(L)}\;,
\label{eq:heft_expansion}
\end{equation}
%
whose successive terms are denoted $\mathcal{L}_2$, $\mathcal{L}_4$ and $\mathcal{L}_6$ according to their chiral dimension, and the expansion parameter is $f^2/\Lambda^2\simeq 1/16\pi^2$, where $f$ is the scale at which the effective expansion is valid and $\Lambda\simeq4\pi f$ is the scale of the new resonances~\cite{Heinrich:2022huh}. In this framework it is important to note that the chiral order of the Lagrangian does not necessarily coincide with the perturbative order of a given process. With this power counting in place, the explicit leading-order Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_2$ can be constructed from the SM field content and symmetries.

\subsubsection{The leading-order chiral Lagrangian}
Following the notation in~\cite{Buchalla:2018yce}, the most general Lagrangian of chiral dimension two that captures the structure of the non-linear electroweak EFT can be written as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_2 =\;& -\frac{1}{2}\langle G_{\mu\nu}G^{\mu\nu}\rangle
    - \frac{1}{2}\langle W_{\mu\nu}W^{\mu\nu}\rangle
    - \frac{1}{4}B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}
    + \sum_{\psi=q_L,l_L,u_R,d_R,e_R}\bar{\psi}\,i\slashed{D}\,\psi \\[2pt]
    &+ \frac{v^2}{4}\,\langle D_\mu U^\dagger D^\mu U\rangle\left(1+F_U(h)\right)
    + \frac{1}{2}\partial_\mu h\,\partial^\mu h - V(h) \\[2pt]
    &- v\left[\bar{q}_L\left(Y_u + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Y_u^{(n)}
      \left(\frac{h}{v}\right)^{n}\right)U P_+ q_R
    + \bar{q}_L\left(Y_d + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Y_d^{(n)}
      \left(\frac{h}{v}\right)^{n}\right)U P_- q_R \right. \\[2pt]
    &\qquad\;\; \left. + \;\bar{l}_L\left(Y_e + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Y_e^{(n)}
      \left(\frac{h}{v}\right)^{n}\right)U P_- l_R + \text{h.c.}\right].
\end{split}
\label{eq:heft_L2}
\end{equation}
%
The first line is the unbroken SM, while the remainder describes the Higgs sector. Here $U=\exp\left(2i\varphi^a T^a/v\right)$ encodes the electroweak Goldstone fields $\varphi^a$, with $T^a$ the generators of $SU(2)$, $P_\pm = 1/2 \pm T_3$, and $\langle A \rangle$ denotes the trace of the matrix $A$. The covariant derivative acting on $U$ is $D_\mu U = \partial_\mu U + i g W_\mu U - i g' B_\mu U T_3$. Besides the usual Yukawa matrices $Y_{u,d,e}$, different flavour couplings $Y^{(n)}_{u,d,e}$ can arise at every order in the Higgs field. The Higgs boson itself enters exclusively through the functions
\begin{equation}
    F_U(h) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_{U,n}\left(\frac{h}{v}\right)^{n}, \qquad
    V(h) = v^4 \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} f_{V,n}\left(\frac{h}{v}\right)^{n},
\label{eq:heft_FU}
\end{equation}
whose coefficients are free parameters of the theory. Hence, the SM is recovered for $f_{U,1}=2$, $f_{U,2}=1$, $f_{V,2}=f_{V,3}=m_h^2/2v^2$, $f_{V,4}=m_h^2/8v^2$ and $Y_f^{(1)}=Y_f$, with all remaining coefficients vanishing;  for generic values, the Lagrangian describes the SM with arbitrary modifications of the Higgs couplings.

The structure of Equations \ref{eq:heft_L2} and \ref{eq:heft_FU} makes the role of the chiral counting explicit. Because the Higgs field carries zero chiral dimension, the infinite series $F_U(h)$ and $V(h)$ cost nothing in the counting: every power of $h$ enters at the same order, and the coefficient of each is an independent parameter. This is what allows vertices such as $ggH$ and $ggHH$, or $t\bar{t}H$ and $t\bar{t}HH$ — each pair generated in SMEFT by one and the same dimension-six operator, and therefore correlated — to be governed by independent parameters in this formulation.

The formalism built on this counting is known in the literature as the non-linear chiral EW Lagrangian \cite{Feruglio:1992wf,Burgess:1999ha,Grinstein:2007iv,Contino:2010mh,Alonso:2012px,Buchalla:2013rka}, while in the context of Higgs boson pair searches it is commonly referred to as HEFT\footnote{The name is not universal: some authors reserve HEFT for the heavy-top limit of the Higgs-gluon interactions and use \textit{electroweak chiral Lagrangian} for the non-linear EFT described here~\cite{Buchalla:2018yce}}

\subsubsection{The phenomenological Lagrangian for \texorpdfstring{\ggHH}{gg → HH}}
Therefore, this LO Lagrangian represents the most general effective description of EWSB with a light scalar singlet and non-linearly realized gauge symmetry, and provides the starting point for constructing the phenomenological Lagrangian used in HH production. When projected onto the interactions relevant for gluon-fusion Higgs pair production, this formal structure is reduced to a set of independent effective couplings. These parameters capture all possible LO deviations in the top–Higgs sector and in the effective Higgs–gluon interactions, and correspond precisely to the HEFT-based parametrization used in experimental and phenomenological studies of Higgs pair production. It should be noted that the Higgs–gluon contact terms are absent from $\mathcal{L}_2$ and first arise at one loop, i.e. in $\mathcal{L}_4$; since $gg\to HH$ is itself loop-induced, both contribute at the same order. Thus, restricted to the terms relevant for $gg\to HH$ production, $\mathcal{L}_2+\mathcal{L}_4$ reduces to the following effective Lagrangian:%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{HEFT}}^{\HH}
    =-m_\PQt\left(c_\PQt\frac{h}{v}+c_{2}\frac{h^2}{v^2}\right)\PAQt\PQt-c_{hhh}\frac{m_h^2}{2v}h^3+\frac{\alpha_s}{8\pi}\left(c_{\Pg}\frac{h}{v}+c_{2\Pg}\frac{h^2}{v^2}\right)G^a_{\mu\nu}G^{a,\mu\nu}.
    \label{eq:heftL}
\end{equation}
%
where the first three couplings $c_\PQt$, $c_{2}$ and $c_{hhh}$ originate from $\mathcal{L}_2$, and correspond to the coupling between Higgs boson and top quarks, two Higgs boson and top quarks, and the effective Higgs self-coupling; while the Higgs-gluon couplings $c_{\Pg}$ and $c_{2\Pg}$ come from $\mathcal{L}_4$ and correspond to the coupling between Higgs boson and gluons and two Higgs bosons and gluons; a loop factor has been extracted explicitly from the definition of the latter two. A point in HEFT parameter space is thus fixed by:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{c}^{\text{HEFT}} = \{c_{hhh},\,c_\PQt,\,c_2,\,c_\Pg,\,c_{2\Pg}\}\;,
    \label{eq:HEFT_param}
\end{equation}
%
In the SM, $c_{hhh} = c_\PQt = 1$ and $c_2 = c_\Pg = c_{2\Pg} = 0$, while in general all five may take values of $\mathcal{O}(1)$. Although the couplings themselves belong to different orders of the chiral expansion, all diagrams contributing to $gg\to HH$ are of the same chiral dimension $d_\chi = 4$, i.e. of one-loop order, since the process is loop-induced given that the $\mathcal{L}_2$ couplings enter through loops while the $\mathcal{L}_4$ ones contribute already at tree level. It is commonly to refer to the $c_{hhh}$ and $c_\PQt$ couplings in terms of the kappa framework\footnote{The kappa framework is a subset of HEFT where only the couplings of the SM are modified by a multiplicative factor, with no new couplings introduced.}\cite{LHCHiggsCrossSectionWorkingGroup:2013rie,deFlorian:2016spz}, with $\kappa_\lambda=c_{hhh}=\lambda/\lambda^{\text{SM}}$ and $\kappa_t=c_\PQt=y_t/y_t^\text{SM}$, where $\lambda^{\text{SM}}=m_h^2 /(2 v^2)\approx0.13$ and $y^{\text{SM}}_t=\sqrt{2}\,m_t/v\approx1$ are the SM prediction for the Higgs trilinear self-coupling and the Yukawa coupling to the top quark. These HEFT couplings and their corresponding leading order diagrams for $gg\to HH$ can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:BSM_diagrams}. The diagrams illustrate the interplay between the LO anomalous couplings (dots) and the next-to-leading order terms (squares) at tree level.


\subsubsection{NLO QCD corrections}
The next logical step is to extend the calculation to next to leading order in the loop expansion, which is to two-loop order or chiral dimension six. In full generality this would require including two-loop electroweak corrections as well as local terms from $\mathcal{L}_6$, which introduce additional couplings parameterizing subleading new physics effects. Such effects lie beyond experimental sensitivity for the foreseeable future, given that the determination of the five leading couplings of Equation \ref{eq:heftL} is itself a substantial challenge. However, QCD corrections are known to be significant for $gg\to HH$ production. This motivates the extension to the next order in the non-linear EFT but restricting the NLO corrections to QCD effects, which within the systematics of the chiral expansion amounts to retaining those chiral-dimension-six contributions that come with a relative factor of QCD coupling $g_s^2$. This restriction is consistent without introducing further couplings, since the effective Lagrangian in equation \ref{eq:heftL} is renormalizable with respect to QCD.

Given that the LO amplitude for $gg\to HH$ scales as $g_s^2$, the NLO virtual corrections comprise all the diagrams at two-loop order carrying un factor total de $g_s^4$, which appear as two-loop, one-loop and tree-level topologies, supplemented by real-emission diagrams at $\mathcal{O}(g_s^3)$. Furthermore, full dependence on the top quark mass is maintained consistently, which is essential to avoid distortions in the shape of the $m_{HH}$ distribution introduced by the $m_t\rightarrow\infty$ approximation commonly used in single Higgs production. Other contributions are consistently ignored in this scheme since because they do not scale as $g_s^4$.

The resulting in NLO $K$-factors are typically large by a factor of two, and crucially for what follows, not uniform across the parameter space nor across the $m_{HH}$ spectrum, so that BSM effects cannot be accounted for by rescaling a leading-order prediction. The explicit NLO Lagrangian at chiral dimension six is not reproduced here: within the QCD-restricted scheme no couplings beyond the five of Equation \ref{eq:heftL} are generated, and that five-parameter description therefore remains complete at NLO. In contrast, higher-order corrections do affect the number and numerical value of the combinations of independent couplings in terms of which the cross section can be expanded — twenty-three at NLO, against the fifteen already introduced in
Equation \ref{eq:RHH} — which is the form exploited for the signal modeling developed in
Section \ref{subsec:physical_model}.

\noindent Taken together, the introduced elements provide a comprehensive definition of the HEFT framework within the context of Higgs boson pair searches. The formal construction of the LO chiral Lagrangian in Equation \ref{eq:heft_L2} justifies the treatmen of the Higgs couplings as free parameters, while its projection onto the relevant interaction for gluon fusion production yields the five independent couplings of Equation \ref{eq:heftL}. This phenomenological HEFT constitutes the most general parameterization available when no assumptions are made about how the observed scalar is embedded in the EWSB sector.

\subsection{Standard Model EFT}
\label{subsec:SMEFT}
%
The SMEFT approach postis that the physical Higgs boson is retained as the fourth component of the $SU(2)_L$ doublet $\phi$, with the same quantum numbers as in the SM, and that the electroweak symmetry is realized linearly exactly as in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}. The Goldstone bosons and the physical scalar are therefore not independent objects but components of a single field, and every operator built from the field $\phi$ will necessarily affect them together. In this regard, SMEFT makes a stronger assumption than HEFT, which holds as long as the new physics is decoupled, i.e., when the heavy states of new physics obtain their mass from a mechanism unrelated to the EWSB, and therefore their effects disappear as their masses increase. In this sense, SMEFT adopts a more constrained description of BSM effects in the Higgs sector, valid only for a subset of UV theories in which the low-energy limit can be obtained by decoupling the heavy physics and thus preserving the linear structure of the SM.

In this scheme, the effective expansion is organized according to the canonical dimensions of the operators, as introduced in \textcolor{red}{Equation \ref{eq:eft_lagrangian}}, where each additional dimension is suppressed by a further power of the new physics scale $\Lambda$, so that at LO corrections:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SMEFT}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}
    + \sum_i \frac{C_i^{(6)}}{\Lambda^2}\,\mathcal{O}_i^{\dim 6}
    + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda^{3}}\right)\;,
\label{eq:smeft_expansion}
\end{equation}
%
with $\mathcal{O}_i$ operators of canonical dimension larger than four built out of the SM fields, and $C_i$ the corresponding Wilson coefficients~\cite{Heinrich:2022huh}. Dimension-five operators violate lepton number and are irrelevant for Higgs phenomenology, so the leading corrections to Higgs observables arise at dimension six. A complete and non-redundant set of dimension-six operators is provided by the Warsaw basis~\cite{Grzadkowski:2010es}, of which only a small subset contributes to $gg\to HH$ production.

In contrast to the chiral expansion of Equation \ref{eq:heft_expansion}, where the Higgs field carries zero chiral dimension and could appear in arbitrary powers at given order, in this formulation, the Higgs field — as a component of the doublet — always appears through the fixed combination $\phi^\dagger\phi$, meaning that a single operator generates the vertices involving one and two Higgs bosons at once. Therefore, while HEFT treats the couplings as independent parameters, in SMEFT they are related, which makes the description much more restrictive but at the same time more predictive, since it connects HH production to single-Higgs and electroweak precision observables within a single set of parameters.

\subsubsection{The dimension-six Lagrangian for \texorpdfstring{\ggHH}{gg → HH}}
%https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.04747 : PHENOMENOLOGICAL RESULTS - echarle un ojo
In contrast to the non-linear case, where the complete LO Lagrangian can be expressed explicitly in Equation \ref{eq:heft_L2}, the full set of dimension-six operators is far too extensive to work with directly: in the full generality of the flavor sector it contains 2499 real parameters, a large subset of which could in principle contribute to $gg\to HH$~\cite{Heinrich:2023rsd}. In order to obtain a manageable subset, it is imposed a flavor symmetry $U(2)_q\times U(2)_u\times U(3)_d$ in the quark sector, which forbids chirality-flipping bilinears involving light quarks and sets all fermion masses and Yukawa couplings to zero with the exception of the top quark. This reflects the prominent role expected of the top quark in many BSM scenarios. Therefore, the number of relevant operators are considerably reduced by excluding those that generate only electroweak loops, as their impact is significantly smaller in this channel.

Even within this reduced set, not all operators contribute at the same order. While SMEFT is organized by canonical dimension, the perturbative structure introduces an additional hierarchy linked to loop suppression factors. Thus, operators can be classified into two categories: those that can already be generated at tree level in the matching to the UV theory, and those that are necessarily loop-generated and therefore carry an implicit factor $L=(16\pi^2)^{-1}$. In this expansion, contributions without loop suppression are termed as \textit{leading}, while those that include additional $L=(16\pi^2)^{-1}$  factors are classified as \textit{subleading}~\cite{Heinrich:2023rsd}.

The dimension-six SMEFT Lagrangian is typically given in the so-called Warsaw basis~\cite{Grzadkowski:2010es}, so that the leading contribution to $gg\to HH$ consists of five operators~\cite{Heinrich:2022huh}:
%
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SMEFT}}^{\HH,\,\text{lead}} =\;&
      \frac{c_{\PH\Box}}{\Lambda^2}(\phi^\dagger\phi)\Box(\phi^\dagger\phi)
    + \frac{c_{\PH D}}{\Lambda^2}(\phi^\dagger D_\mu\phi)^*(\phi^\dagger D^\mu\phi)
    + \frac{c_\PH}{\Lambda^2}(\phi^\dagger\phi)^3 \\
    &+ \frac{c_{\PQt\PH}}{\Lambda^2}\left(\phi^\dagger\phi\,\bar{Q}_L\tilde{\phi}\PQt_R
      + \text{h.c.}\right)
    + \frac{c_{\PH G}}{\Lambda^2}\phi^\dagger\phi\, G^a_{\mu\nu}G^{\mu\nu,a}\;,
\end{split}
\label{eq:smeft_lead}
\end{equation}
%
where $\tilde{\phi}=i\sigma_2\phi^*$ is the charge conjugate of the Higgs doublet. Here, the first three coefficients modify the Higgs kinetic term and the scalar potential, and hence the trilinear self-coupling; $c_{\PQt\PH}$ modifies the top Yukawa interaction; and $c_{\PH G}$ generates a contact interaction between the Higgs doublet and two gluons.

While the five operators in Eq.~\ref{eq:smeft_lead} capture the leading SMEFT effects in weakly coupled UV completions where loop-generated structures are suppressed, in strongly coupled scenarios it is possible to relax this assumption to allow for additional contributions. Therefore, loop-generated operators can make sizeable contributions in more general BSM scenarios, such as those with strong dynamics or heavy fermionic partners. This is particularly the case for the chromomagnetic dipole and the four-top interactions, which appear at the next order in the loop hierarchy and can be equally relevant in gluon fusion di-Higgs production in the aforementioned scenarios, populating a well-defined subset of the Warsaw basis. Therefore, their inclusion provide a more comprehensive and robust parameterization of the SMEFT effets relevant for Higgs pair production. Furthermore, these operators mix with the leading terms under RGE evolution, so their impact can be amplified at low scales if they are present in the UV. To account for these possibilities, and following ~\cite{Heinrich:2023rsd}, these contributions enter at subleading order according to the previous classification:
%
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \Delta\mathcal{L}_{\text{SMEFT}}^{\HH,\,\text{sublead}} =\;&
      \frac{c_{\PQt G}}{\Lambda^2}\left(\bar{Q}_L\sigma^{\mu\nu}T^a G^a_{\mu\nu}
      \tilde{\phi}\PQt_R + \text{h.c.}\right) \\
    &+ \frac{c_{Q\PQt}^{(1)}}{\Lambda^2}\bar{Q}_L\gamma^\mu Q_L
      \bar{\PQt}_R\gamma_\mu\PQt_R
     + \frac{c_{Q\PQt}^{(8)}}{\Lambda^2}\bar{Q}_L\gamma^\mu T^a Q_L
      \bar{\PQt}_R\gamma_\mu T^a\PQt_R \\
    &+ \frac{c_{QQ}^{(1)}}{\Lambda^2}\bar{Q}_L\gamma^\mu Q_L\bar{Q}_L\gamma_\mu Q_L
     + \frac{c_{QQ}^{(8)}}{\Lambda^2}\bar{Q}_L\gamma^\mu T^a Q_L
      \bar{Q}_L\gamma_\mu T^a Q_L \\
    &+ \frac{c_{\PQt\PQt}}{\Lambda^2}\bar{\PQt}_R\gamma^\mu\PQt_R
      \bar{\PQt}_R\gamma_\mu\PQt_R\;,
\end{split}
\label{eq:smeft_sublead}
\end{equation}
%
where $\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac{i}{2}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]$ and the Warsaw operator $\mathcal{O}^{(3)3333}_{qq}$ has been traded for $\mathcal{O}^{(8)}_{QQ}$, the two being related by a linear redefinition of their Wilson coefficients~\cite{Heinrich:2023rsd}. Equations \ref{eq:smeft_lead} and \ref{eq:smeft_sublead} together constitute the eleven-operator description adopted in this thesis work. It should be stressed that the leading/subleading classification rests on the assumption of a renormalizable and weakly coupled UV sector, is not invariant under field redefinitions and is therefore basis dependent, and that the subleading set retained here does not constitute a complete subleading order in a systematic power counting~\cite{Heinrich:2023rsd}.


\subsubsection{The effective parameter space}
So far, in the formulation presented via Equations \ref{eq:smeft_lead} and \ref{eq:smeft_sublead}, eleven operators appear that provide a complete description of the SMEFT contributions relevant to Higgs pair production. However, it should be noted that not all Wilson coefficients remain independent once the theory is expressed in terms of physical fields. The transition from the gauge basis to the mass basis following the EWSB introduces non-trivial relations among operators, resulting in some of the presented coefficients being combinable into effective parameters that control the physical interactions probed in $gg\to HH$.

Accordingly, after EWSB, the field redefinition required to restore the Higgs kinetic term to its canonical form combines the two kinetic coefficients into the single parameter $c_{\PH kin}=c_{\PH\Box}-\tfrac{1}{4}c_{\PH D}$. Similarly, a simplification can be carried out with respect to the four-fermion sector, taking into account that the contributions of $c_{QQ}^{(1)}$ and $c_{\PQt\PQt}$ to the amplitude factorize in the same way and can only be probed through the combination $c_{QQ\PQt\PQt}=c_{QQ}^{(1)}+c_{\PQt\PQt}$, as shown in~\cite{Heinrich:2023rsd}. Therefore, after eliminating redundancies associated with field redefinitions and EWSB, the former relations are obtained, reducing the number of independent parameters relevant for $gg\to HH$ from eleven to nine. In this way, the physical observables are exclusively determined by a specific subset of the original Wilson coefficients, those that genuinely control the physical deformations of the amplitude. Consequently, a given point in the SMEFT parameter space is fixed by
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{c}^{\text{SMEFT}} = \{c_\PH,\,c_{\PH kin},\,c_{\PQt\PH},\,c_{\PH G},\,
    c_{\PQt G},\,c_{Q\PQt}^{(1)},\,c_{Q\PQt}^{(8)},\,c_{QQ\PQt\PQt},\,c_{QQ}^{(8)}\}\;,
\label{eq:smeft_params}
\end{equation}
%
with the SM recovered when all of them vanish.

\subsubsection{Truncation of the EFT expansion}
%
After identifying the nine independent SMEFT effective parameters, the next logical step is to determine the order of the EFT expansion at which the contributions should be considered. Unlike HEFT, where the choice to be made concerned the loop order retained in the chiral expansion, in the SMEFT case the corresponding scheme choice concerns the order in $1/\Lambda$ at which the series is truncated. Hence, given that the amplitude in SMEFT is a series in $1/\Lambda^2$, the squared matrix element contains both a linear term in the dimension-six coefficients — arising from the interference with the SM — and a quadratic term. The latter is formally of the same order in $1/\Lambda$ as the interference between the SM and dimension-eight operators, which is not included, and retaining it is a scheme choice rather than a consistent order in the expansion. However, this is the standard choice in di-Higgs studies, since the linear interference is often suppressed and the quadratic terms dominate the sensitivity over much of the parameter space. This coice is known as the \textit{quadratic} truncation, where only the SM, linear and quadratic contributions for the leading operators are retained, while the subleading ones enter only linearly. The impact of this choice, and the conditions under which the truncated series remains a valid description, are discussed in Section \ref{subsec:EFT_validity}.




\vspace{10pt}
% cierre


\noindent In summary, it has been demonstrated that SMEFT provides a description of HH that is considerably more constrained than HEFT and therefore more powerful where its assumptions hold. Its effect is described by the set of nine Wilson coefficients of Equation \ref{eq:smeft_params}, which parameterize the low-energy imprint of any decoupling extension of the SM and can be matched onto specific UV-complete models, allowing results expressed as bounds on them to be reinterpreted far beyond the process from which they were extracted. Nevertheless, due to the loss of generality that comes with imposing the doublet structure, this formalism excludes the non-decoupling scenarios that motivated HEFT in the first place. The two frameworks are therefore complementary rather than alternative, and the relation between them — together with the conditions under which one reduces to the other — is the subject of the following section.



\subsection{Translating between HEFT and SMEFT}
\label{subsec:HEFT_SMEFT}
%
The two descriptions presented above are not independent alternatives, but rather complementary. In this sense, the differences in their respective approaches are reflected in the distinct ways each description encodes correlations among Higgs interactions. Therefore, while both frameworks aim to parameterize deviations from the SM, the choice between them determines which type of UV scenario can be consistently captured and, consequently, the physical significance of experimental results expressed in terms of those parameters. It is thus essential to understand not only the applicability of each formalism separately — which will be covered in the Section \ref{subsec:EFT_validity} — but also the relationship between the two and the extent to which that relationship can be trusted. This is why it is particularly informative to translate between the two descriptions.

The qualitative statement of these relationships is based on the fact that HEFT makes no assumptions about how the observed scalar is embedded in the EWSB sector; therefore, any SMEFT Lagrangian can be rewritten in terms of the HEFT formalism. In this manner, SMEFT can be regarded as a subset of HEFT that emulates the SM structure. The most direct way to make this connection explicit is to construct the dictionary between the two sets of parameters, yielding a set of concrete relations between couplings that shows exactly which direction in HEFT space are accessible to the linear realization and which lie outside its reach.

To obtain the translation, the Higgs doublet in Eq. \ref{eq:smeft_lead} is expanded around its vacuum expectation value, and applying the field redefinition
%
\begin{equation}
    h \;\rightarrow\; h + v^2\frac{c_{\PH kin}}{\Lambda^2}
    \left(h + \frac{h^2}{v} + \frac{h^3}{3v^2}\right)\;,
\label{eq:higgs_redef}
\end{equation}
%
which eliminates the modification that $c_{\PH\Box}$ and $c_{\PH D}$ induce in the field normalization, the Higgs term is restored to its canonical form $(\partial_\mu h)^2/2$ up to $\mathcal{O}(\Lambda^{-4})$, with $c_{\PH kin}=c_{\PH\Box}-\tfrac{1}{4}c_{\PH D}$ as defined in Section~\ref{subsec:SMEFT}. Comparing the coefficients of the corresponding terms in the two Lagrangian then yields the leading-order dictionary collected in Table \ref{tab:heft_smeft}.
%
\begin{table}

        $c_\PQt$   & $1 + \dfrac{v^2}{\Lambda^2}\,c_{\PH kin}
                      - \dfrac{v^2}{\Lambda^2}\dfrac{v}{\sqrt{2}\,m_\PQt}\,c_{\PQt\PH}$ \\[8pt]

The dictionary in Table \ref{tab:heft_smeft} covers only four of the nine coefficients relevant in SMEFT as included in Eq \ref{eq:smeft_params}: the remaining five coefficients — the chromomagnetic dipole and the four combinations of the four-top operators — have no equivalent in the HEFT parameterization truncated to five couplings. Furthermore, the $gg\to HH$ process is not equally sensitive to these five additional directions.  The cross section is considerably affected by $c_{\PQt G}$ and, above all, by
$c_{Q\PQt}^{(1)}$ and $c_{Q\PQt}^{(8)}$, whereas $c_{QQ\PQt\PQt}$ and $c_{QQ}^{(8)}$ contribute only marginally, to the extent that Higgs pair production is not a suitable process for constraining them~\cite{Heinrich:2023rsd}.

Several conclusions can be drawn from Table \ref{tab:heft_smeft}. The first notable point is that the five HEFT couplings are expressed in terms of only four SMEFT coefficients — $c_\PH$, $c_{\PH kin}$, $c_{\PQt\PH}$, and $c_{\PH G}$ — such that the linear realization necessarily imposes one exact relation among them. That relation is immediately apparent in the last two rows, where both gluonic couplings are derived from the single coefficient $c_{\PH G}$, establishing their relationship as follows:
%
\begin{equation}
    c_{2\Pg} = \frac{1}{2}\,c_\Pg\;.
\label{eq:smeft_gluon_relation}
\end{equation}
%
so that in HEFT these two parameters are completely independent, whereas in SMEFT, at leading order, their ratio is fixed, so the image of the SMEFT parameter space is four-dimensional at LO. The second feature that can be gleaned from the correlations shown in the Table is that a variation in a single Wilson coefficient does not correspond to a variation in a single HEFT coupling, but rather to a displacement along a fixed direction involving several of them at once. This has practical implications for phenomenological fits, since a bound on an individual Wilson coefficient constrains a combination of anomalous couplings rather than any one of them in isolation.

This can be seen in the diagram of Fig. \ref{fig:smeft_vertices}, which shows a clear visual representation of the correspondence between the five $gg\to HH$ interaction vertices in Fig. \ref{fig:BSM_diagrams} and each leading Wilson coefficient. Therefore, the figure illustrates the structure more directly: the two coefficients acting in the top sector $c_{\PQt\PH}$ and $c_{\PH kin}$, both contribute to the $\PQt\PAQt\PH$ and $\PQt\PAQt\PH\PH$ vertices at once, so that the contact interaction — which in HEFT is independently parameterized by $c_2$ — cannot be varied without simultaneously changing the Yukawa coupling; the trilinear vertex receives contributions from
$c_\PH$ and from $c_{\PH kin}$, which is therefore the only coefficient spanning three columns; and the final row reveals that $c_{\PH G}$ affects only the two gluonic vertices, which is the source of the relation depicted in Eq. \ref{eq:smeft_gluon_relation}.  Read column by column instead, it becomes apparent that no column is reached by a coefficient of its own. Every vertex shares its parametrization with at least one other, which is precisely what distinguishes the linear description from the non-linear one.

It is crucial to note that a translation performed at the Lagrange level must be applied with particular care, as the two counting schemes are based on different assumptions. Therefore, while the HEFT coefficients do not exhibit explicit dependence on the $\Lambda$ scale, the validity of the SMEFT expansion is contingent upon the scale being sufficiently high compared to the relevant energies of the process. As a result, a point in the HEFT parameter space that satisfies all current experimental constraints may correspond to values of the Wilson coefficients for which the SMEFT expansion is no longer valid. In this sense, Table \ref{tab:heft_smeft} should be interpreted as a dictionary mapping between parameterizations and not as a license to move freely between them. The scope of applicability of each of these parameterizations will be discussed in more detail in the followin section \ref{subsec:EFT_validity}.

At this stage, the selection of a framework becomes phenomenologically relevant insofar as HEFT and SMEFT describe genuinely different physical hypotheses. Higgs boson pair production is one of the few processes with the actual ability to distinguish between these two, since the couplings involving two Higgs bosons — $c_2$ and $c_{2\Pg}$ — do not contribute to the single-Higgs observables at leading order. In HEFT, however, they remain independent parameters, so Higgs pair production is the only process that can test them directly. Conversely, in SMEFT, these parameters are determined by the coefficients that also fix the $\PH\PQt\PAQt$ and $\PH\Pg\Pg$ vertices, which are strongly constrained by single-Higgs data; therefore, any constraints obtained from other processes are automatically propagated to them. Consequently, the same measured distribution corresponds to different regions of parameter space and supports different physical conclusions, depending on which description is adopted. This reveals the complementarity of both descriptions, which capture different physical hypotheses and must be evaluated in parallel when interpreting results from double-Higgs production.

\subsection{Range of validity of the effective descriptions}
\label{subsec:EFT_validity}
%
Once the translation between the two frameworks has been established, and given that the same HH distribution leads to different interpretations in each framework, it is necessary to establish the domain of applicability for each EFT within which the translation between parameterizations is meaningful. An effective description is only predictive within the energy range for which its expansion converges, and that range is defined differently for each of the frameworks introduced.

In SMEFT, the validity of the description is determined by the physical scale that governs the effective expansion. Thus, the series in $1/\Lambda$ is meaningful only as long as
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{c_i\,E^2}{\Lambda^2} \ll 1\;,
\label{eq:eft_validity}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the characteristic energy of the process.

In HEFT, on the other hand, the expansion parameter is the loop factor $f^2/\Lambda^2\simeq1/16\pi^2$ from Equation \ref{eq:heft_expansion}, so there is no physical scale against which to compare the energies of the process; rather, its validity depends on the deviations in the Higgs couplings constituting the dominant effect of new physics in the electroweak regime. While this is an unproven assumption, it is well-supported by the current experimental situation, since the couplings of the Higgs boson are still known with a precision far below that achieved in the electroweak gauge sector (see Section \ref{subsec:H_phenomenology}) and, therefore, sizeable deviations in the Higgs sector alone cannot be ruled out. Additionally, both prescriptions are bounded by unitarity constraints, which restrict the maximum size of the effective couplings and delimit the energy range in which the theory can be used consistently.

From this perspective, in HEFT the initial hypothesis does not translate into any parameters that need to be set, whereas in SMEFT, taking validity into account leads to operational decisions. In this context, the Higgs boson pair production process is particularly delicate. First, the relevant scale of the process is the invariant mass of the Higgs pair, so the criterion in equation \ref{eq:eft_validity} reads as $c_i\,\mHH^2/\Lambda^2\ll1$; that is, the validity criterion is evaluated in the regime of high values of $m_{HH}$. However, the high-$\mHH$ tail is precisely where non-resonant searches derive much of their sensitivity to anomalous couplings; thus, keeping the theory within its domain of applicability conflicts directly with the need to explore the most informative kinematic region. On the other hand, as discussed throughout this chapter, the $gg\to HH$ process is dominated by delicate cancellations between the box and triangle topologies, such that small differences in the treatment of the Wilson coefficients can have relatively large effects, particularly in differential distributions.

As shown in Equation \ref{eq:eft_validity}, SMEFT predictions are directly linked to the scale parameter $\Lambda$ and not solely on the Wilson coefficients. This means that, for a specific value of $c_i$, the shape of the distribution $m_{HH}$ varies with $\Lambda$, and the SM shape is always recovered in the limit of large $\Lambda$, since the deformation of the SM parameter space becomes arbitrarily small. Consequently, the interpretability of results regarding the sensitivity of an analysis to a given set of Wilson coefficients requires specifying the assumed value of $\Lambda$. This fragility of the SMEFT prediction is further accentuated when considering the truncation of the series, discussed in Section \ref{subsec:SMEFT}. Thus, if the EFT is within its domain of validity, the different truncation options should produce consistent results; otherwise, negative cross sections may arise in large regions of parameter space beyond the reach of the expansion. This serve to underscore the cautionary note made in Section \ref{subsec:HEFT_SMEFT} regarding the dictionary in Table \ref{tab:heft_smeft}. Given the substantial differences between the two formalisms, a point in the HEFT parameter space may be consistent with all the constraints and, even so, result in values of the Wilson coefficients for which Equation \ref{eq:eft_validity} fails.

The aforementioned considerations directly impact experimental practice. The usual experimental response to Equation \ref{eq:eft_validity} is to restrict the region of phase space considered in the interpretation, by clipping events above a certain threshold for $m_{HH}$. This procedure, commonly referred to as \textit{clipping}, ensures that the region used satisfies the validity criterion for the assumed $\Lambda$, but at the cost of reduced sensitivity.

The discussion above on the validity of effective descriptions in the production of Higgs boson pairs is conclude as follows: once the framework, the truncation scheme, the scale $\Lambda$, ant the clipping threshold are fixed, the physical model is fully specified. The next challenge relates to the complexity of the parameter space that must be explored even under these well-defined assumptions, since effective theories give rise to highly multidimensional structures, the consequences of which are examined below.

\subsection{Multidimensionality problem}
\label{subsec:multidimensionality}
%
The preceding sections have established the framework necessary to characterize the effects of new physics on the $gg\to HH$ production using two effective descriptions, each derived from a different hypothesis regarding the nature of the observed scalar. The application of these formalisms in the interpretation of experimental results in BSM searches is of considerable value, as they provide a parameterization of the deviations from the SM that does not presuppose any specific model. Therefore, any comprehensive UV model can be projected onto a point in the parameter spaces defined for each of the formalisms: five independent couplings in HEFT, Equation \ref{eq:HEFT_param}; and nine Wilson coefficients for SMEFT, Equation \ref{eq:smeft_params}. As a result, the limits expressed in terms of couplings or Wilson coefficients can be reinterpreted in terms of any extension of the Standard Model, making them essential tools for interpreting experimental searches.

However, this generality poses certain difficulties at the experimental level, since the formalisms described do not offer a finite set of direct hypotheses to test, but rather provide a continuum of predictions for which limits must be established. This, of course, has experimental implications that must be considered.

The primary challenge arises from the high dimensionality inherent in these descriptions. Working with a large set of coefficients that may be correlated — and that depend on specific assumptions regarding validity, truncation, and reference scales — makes a complete exploration of the parameter space unfeasible and causes sensitivity in each direction to depend heavily on the others. Furthermore, the inherent structure of the $gg\to HH$ process contributes to the existing challenges by amplifying them, due to the fact that different combinations of parameters can yield similar distortions in the observable distributions, leading to degeneracies and ill-conditioned regions. Furthermore, when extending the interpretation to other production mechanisms, the parameter space widens even further, since, for example, vector boson fusion introduces its own couplings to the electroweak sector, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:BSM_production}. A meaningful exclusion requires evaluating the compatibility of the data with the predicted signal at every point in such a space, and the number of points needed to cover it grows exponentially with its dimension.

On the other hand, as demonstrated throughout this chapter, the amplitude of $gg\to HH$ is influenced by topologies that interfere non-trivially, and the resulting $m_{HH}$ distributions differ both in normalization and in shape. Depending on the region of parameter space, the spectrum may take on a wide variety of forms: from a pronounced dip at the invariant mass where the leading-order amplitude nearly cancels, to a strong enhancement in the low-$m_{HH}$ region driven by a large value of the trilinear coupling, or the characteristic double-peak structure already illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:mHH_kl}. These deviations correspond to distinct effective signals, with different selection efficiencies and different responses of the multivariate discriminants on which the analysis relies. Therefore, to scan this space, it is not sufficient to predict the rate at a single point; rather, a full differential prediction, propagated throughout the entire analysis chain, is necessary.

To address the internal complexity of the EFT space, specific strategies are required. A common response to this situation has been to replace the continuum of predictions with a discrete set of representative points. This is exemplified by cluster analyses of the parameter space, partitioning it into regions whose members share a characteristic $m_{HH}$ shape and selecting a benchmark point for each of them~\cite{Carvalho:2015ttv,Carvalho:2016rys}. This approach has been widely used and, and while practical, it faces limitations given that a discrete set of points yields a discrete set of results: it does not produce exclusion contours, cannot be profiled over, and does not allow the combination of channels or production modes in a common parameter space.

This final point is of the utmost importance when proposing solutions to address the problem. The likelihood fit required for statistical interpretation in an experimental search is constructed using inference tools that require the signal to be expressed as a continuous function of the parameters to be constrained. Therefore, the solution cannot rely on a set of simulated points. Instead, a prediction of the full differential signal is required for any point in the EFT parameter space, which should be obtained at an acceptable computational cost that does not depend on the density of the scan. Overcoming this practical challenge of interpreting $gg\to HH$ in order to provide a reliable differential prediction at any point in EFT space is the purpose of the signal modeling techniques introduced in the next Section \ref{sec:Modeling}.


\section{HH signal modeling techniques}
\label{sec:Modeling}
%
As previously outlined, the two effective descriptions introduced in Section \ref{subsec:EFTs} do not provide a finite list of hypotheses to be tested, but rather a continuum of them: a five-dimensional space of independent couplings in HEFT, Equation \ref{eq:HEFT_param}, and a nine-dimensional space of Wilson coefficients in SMEFT, Equation \ref{eq:smeft_params}, on top of which the truncation scheme, the reference scale $\Lambda$ and the clipping threshold discussed in Section \ref{subsec:EFT_validity} must still be fixed. It has been demonstrated that the experimental interpretation of $gg\to HH$ searches in terms of EFT frameworks is highly influenced by the dimensionality of the parameter space.

This places effective descriptions in a challenging position from an experimental standpoint: to derive limits, the likelihood fit requires knowing the expected signal yield in every analysis category and every bin of the final discriminant as a continuous function of the EFT parameters to be constrained. This cannot be obtained by simulating each point independently: each sample must be generated, passed through the full detector simulation, reconstructed, and processed through the entire analysis chain up to the evaluation of the multivariate discriminants. Repeating this for a scan whose density grows exponentially with the dimension of the parameter space is not feasible.

%An initial solution to this problem could involve reweighting at the generator level, where the matrix element is reevaluated event by event at each target point. However, this method requires the matrix elements to be available and affordable to evaluate. for every event at every point of a dense scan. This approach is exact and widely used in tree-level HH processes, but in $gg\to HH$, which is loop‑induced already at LO, the cost of recomputing the full $m_\PQt$-dependent amplitude for each event renders it prohibitive.


Furthermore, the interference structure of the $gg\to HH$ process causes the signal shape, selection efficiency, and the response of the discriminants to change qualitatively across the EFT space. As a result, what must be provided at each point in EFT space is not a rate, but a complete differential prediction. This precludes approximations based on simple rescaling, so the problem of multidimensionality becomes a modeling problem: how to provide a reliable differential prediction throughout the EFT space without resorting to exhaustive scans.



Despite these difficulties, the effective frameworks share a structural property that makes the problem tractable. The HEFT and SMEFT Lagrangians, presented in equations \ref{eq:heftL}, \ref{eq:smeft_lead}, and \ref{eq:smeft_sublead}, the parameters of the theory — the HEFT couplings and the SMEFT Wilson coefficients — appear as constant coefficients multiplying a fixed set of local operators. Each Feynman diagram contributing to $gg\to HH$ is therefore associated with a definite monomial in those parameters, multiplied by a form factor that depends only on the kinematics of the process and on SM quantities such as $m_\PQt$, $\alpha_s$, and the collision energy. The amplitude is thus a linear combination of a finite
set of parameter-independent form factors, and its square — hence the cross section — a polynomial in the parameters with a finite number of terms, each of them the product of a known monomial and a coefficient that carries all the dynamics but none of the parameter dependence. This is not an approximation but an identity: the parametrization can be shown to be exact at leading order~\cite{Carvalho:2016rys}, with the number of independent terms growing with the perturbative order~\cite{Buchalla:2018yce}. It is, in fact, the structure already encountered at the level of the total rate in Equation \ref{eq:RHH}, where the
fifteen leading-order combinations $A_1,\dots,A_{15}$ appear. Crucially, the separation between the parameter-dependent and the parameter-independent parts takes place at the level of the matrix element, and therefore holds in every region of phase space independently: the same finite set of combinations describes any differential distribution, provided its coefficients are determined bin by bin. This analytical parametrization of the differential cross section is the common basis on which the modeling techniques used in HH searches are built.

The modeling techniques based on this analytical parametrization of the cross section differ in the stage along the analysis chain at which the factorization is exploited. The most direct use is to determine the coefficients once and for all, by fitting the parametrization to a set of simulated samples, and then to employ the resulting polynomial to assign a weight to every event of a single reference sample: the ratio between the polynomial evaluated at the target point and the same polynomial evaluated at the reference point, in the bin where the event falls. This technique, known as \textit{event-level reweighting} (ELR), allows one sample to reproduce the prediction at any point of the parameter space, and the reweighted sample can then be used to build any observable of interest, and not only those used to derive the parametrization. Its cost, however, is that of a full pass over the event sample for every point evaluated, since the weights are assigned event by event. Taking it a step further, the \textit{matrix inversion method} exploits the factorization one stage further down the analysis chain: since the cross section at any point is a linear combination of a finite number of terms, the prediction can be expressed as a linear combination $\sigma(\mathbf{c}) = \sum_i w_i(\mathbf{c})\,\sigma_i$ over a reduced set of basis points, whose weights are obtained by inverting the matrix that relates them. The combination is performed directly over the final distributions rather than over individual events, so that the analysis chain is traversed only once per basis point and the cost of scanning no longer depends on the number of points evaluated.

%There are several modeling techniques based on this analytical parameterization of the cross section, and their main difference lies in the stage along the analysis chain at which the factorization is exploited. \subsection{The underlying physical model}
\label{subsec:physical_model}
%
As introduced above, the factorization of the cross section into a parameter-dependent part and a parameter-independent part carrying the kinematics of the event is the physical basis on which all modeling techniques rest. This factorization implies that the cross section is an exact polynomial function of the couplings with a finite and known number of terms, determined by the content of the Lagrangian and the perturbative order of the calculation. Furthermore, since it occurs at the level of the matrix element, it holds in every region of phase space independently.

The starting point for expressing the factorization is the effective Lagrangian of Equation \ref{eq:heftL}. In this case, the five couplings that define the HEFT Lagrangian act as constant factors associated with the vertices of the different topologies of the $gg\to HH$ process, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_diagrams}. In essence, at leading order, each topology contributes to the total amplitude as a product of the couplings of its vertices — independent of the kinematics — and a function describing the dynamics of that topology — independent of the couplings. It becomes evident that, under this structure, the amplitude of the process takes the form:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M} = \ckt^{2}\,\mathcal{M}_{\dbox}
    + \ckt\,\ckl\,\mathcal{M}_{\dtri}
    + \cctwo\,\mathcal{M}_{2}
    + \ckl\,\ccg\,\mathcal{M}_{\Pg}
    + \cctwog\,\mathcal{M}_{2\Pg}\;,
\label{eq:amplitude_decomposition}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{M}_{\dbox}$ and $\mathcal{M}_{\dtri}$ denote the box and triangle contributions already present in the Standard Model, $\mathcal{M}_{2}$ denotes the contribution from the contact vertex $\ PQt\PAQt\HH$, and $\mathcal{M}_{\Pg}$ and $\mathcal{M}_{2\Pg}$ denote those of the topologies generated by the effective Higgs-gluon vertices. Thus, in Equation \ref{eq:amplitude_decomposition}, each of the $\mathcal{M}_i$ terms encapsulates the dynamic part associated with each of the $gg\to HH$ topologies, which is identical for any point in EFT space and depends solely on the parton Mandelstam variables $\hat{s}$ and $\hat{t}$ — equivalently, on $m_{HH}$ and the scattering angle $\cos\theta^{*}$ \cite{Carvalho:2017vnu} — and on the masses and coupling constants of the Standard Model.\\

By integrating the square of the amplitude, $\overline{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|^{2}}$, over phase space and over the parton distribution functions, the monomials can be factored out from the integral because they do not depend on the integration variables. This preserves the polynomial structure, and the cross section takes the form:
%
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}) = \mathrm{Poly}\left(\mathbf{c},\,\mathbf{A}\right)
    = \sum_{i=1}^{N_A} A_i\, f_i(\mathbf{c})\;,
\label{eq:poly_general}
\end{equation}
where the $f_i$ are the $N_A$ monomials in the EFT parameters, which are known analytically, while the $A_i$ denote the coefficients that capture the dynamics of the process.\\

% Recuento HEFT
The number of independent terms is therefore determined by combinatorics: $n$ structures will generate $n(n+1)/2$ independent terms. Thus, at leading order, the five structures generate $N_A=15$ independent terms — five squares and ten interference terms — which preciesly determines the parameterization at leading order.

However, as outlined in Section \ref{subsec:HEFT}, the $gg\to HH$ process involves significant QCD corrections, necessitating their consideration. When extending the calculation to NLO, virtual and real-emission corrections give rise to topologies that introduce new amplitude structures in Equation \ref{eq:amplitude_decomposition}, with prefactors $\kappa_{\PQt}c_{\Pg}$ and $c_{\Pg}^{2}$,
which do not exist independently at the LO. Since at this order only the interference between the LO and NLO amplitudes is retained, the additional terms introduce eight new independent combinations, all of which involve the Higgs-gluon coupling, thereby increasing the total number of terms from fifteen to $N_A = 23$. Although the functional form of Equation \ref{eq:poly_general} is preserved, it is important to note that these NLO corrections modify the values of the coefficients $A_i$ associated with the monomials $f_i$, since, as indicated in Section \ref{subsec:HEFT}, the $K$-factors are not uniform either in parameter space or along the $m_{HH}$ spectrum, which has direct consequences for modeling techniques.\\

% Recuento SMEFT
The same reasoning can be applied to SMEFT, since the Wilson coefficients linearly multiply the six-dimensional operators in the Lagrangian, so that the amplitude is once again linear in them and Equation \ref{eq:poly_general} retains its form. In the case of the SMEFT framework, the square of the modulus now takes the usual form of a constant term $A_0$, corresponding to the SM prediction, a set of linear terms arising from the interference between the SM and the six-dimensional operators, and a set of quadratic terms in the latter. According from the nine parameters in Equation \ref{eq:smeft_params}, a general quadratic form would require $1+9+9\cdot 10/2=55$ coefficients. However, the number of independent terms in SMEFT is also contingent upon the adopted truncation scheme, as elaborated in Sections \ref{subsec:SMEFT} and \ref{subsec:EFT_validity}. The quadratic truncation used in this work retains the linear and quadratic contributions of the four leading operators but discards the term $|\mathcal{M}^{\text{sublead}}_{\dim 6}|^{2}$, thereby ensuring that the five subleading coefficients appear only linearly. The resulting count is therefore $N_A = 40$ coefficients, $A_0,\dots,A_{39}$.

It is noteworthy that, while in HEFT the physical model is fully determined once the perturbative order is established, in SMEFT the selection of truncation scheme is what shapes the model. Therefore, different truncations yield different polynomials, and consequently, different signal models that are not easily comparable. This reinforces the observation made in Section \ref{subsec:EFT_validity},which states that any result expressed in terms of Wilson coefficients must be accompanied by a specification of the scheme under which it was obtained.\\

% Generalización diferencial
So far, the discussion has been presented in terms of the total cross section. Throughout the preceding discussion, however, it has been emphasized that the factorization of Equation \ref{eq:poly_general} holds at the differential. Consequently, the same functional form and the same set of monomials describe any differential distribution, with the sole difference being that the coefficients are no longer global numbers but instead depend on the region of phase space under consideration, which in practice corresponds to a bin of the chosen observable:
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\xi_j}\,\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c})
    = \mathrm{Poly}\left(\mathbf{c},\,\mathbf{A}^{j}\right)
    = \sum_{i=1}^{N_A} A^{j}_i\, f_i(\mathbf{c})\;,
\label{eq:poly_differential}
\end{equation}
%
where $\xi$ denotes a variable in phase space, and the index $j$ denotes the corresponding bin on which $A_i$ depends. This differential generalization is what makes it possible to predict not only the production rate but also the kinematics of the $HH$ system at an arbitrary point in parameter space.\\

This directly applies to experimental searches, where what is needed is the distribution of the final observable — typically the output of a multivariate discriminator — following event reconstruction and selection. They depend on the region of phase space to which they refer, as well as on the conditions under which the calculation was performed: the center-of-mass energy, the parton distribution functions, and the scales used. Hence, when transferring the parameterization to the analysis level, the set of coefficients incorporates the acceptance and efficiency of the selection; therefore, a set of coefficients cannot be transferred directly from one context to another.\\

After establishing the physical model that encodes the dependence on the EFT parameters, the goal of various modeling methods is to determine how to handle the finite set of coefficients $A_i$ that capture the dynamics of the process, in order to make predictions about any point in EFT space. Two complementary strategies are available, and the choice between them is precisely what distinguishes the two techniques described below, which have already been introduced earlier. Event-level reweighting, discussed in detail in Section \ref{subsec:ELR}, is based on fixing the coefficients from a set of known predictions, so that Equation \ref{eq:poly_differential} becomes an analytical formula that can be evaluated at any point of the parameter space and used to assign a weight to each event in a reference sample. The matrix inversion method, on the other hand, avoids determining the coefficients altogether, exploiting the fact that Equation \ref{eq:poly_differential} is linear in them. Under this approach, a set of $N_A$ predictions at basis points whose monomial vectors are linearly independent allows the coefficients to be eliminated from the problem and the prediction at any point in space to be expressed as a linear combination of the basis predictions, with weights that are known analytic functions of $\mathbf{c}$, as will be detailed in Section \ref{subsec:matrix_inversion}. Finally, it should be noted that although the dependence on the EFT parameters is exact in the presented model, the various modeling techniques introduce uncertainty into the predictions, which will also be the subject of study in the following sections.



\subsection{Event-level reweighting}
\label{subsec:ELR}
% Descripción del método - intro e hilado con sección anterior
The event-level reweighting technique is the most direct application of the model described in the previous section. This approach involves fixing the coefficients $A_i$ so that the differential prediction can be analytically evaluated at any point in the parameter space. Thus, instead of simulating a sample at the point of interest, the ELR approach involves starting with an existing sample generated at a reference point — typically the SM — and assigning each event a weight that reproduces the prediction. As both the reference point and the target point are described by the same set of coefficients, the weight is simply the ratio of two evaluations of Equation \ref{eq:poly_differential} once these are known.\\

Formally, let $\mathbf{c}$ be the target point and $\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}}$ be the point at which the initial reference sample was generated, after applying Equation \ref{eq:poly_differential} to both points, the differential prediction at the target point is obtained as:
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\xi_j}\,\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c})
    = \frac{\mathrm{Poly}\left(\mathbf{c},\,\mathbf{A}^{j}\right)}
           {\mathrm{Poly}\left(\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}},\,\mathbf{A}^{j}\right)}\;
      \frac{d}{d\xi_j}\,\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}})\;,
\label{eq:elr_master}
\end{equation}
%
Thus, once the coefficients $A_i$ are known, any point in the HEFT or SMEFT parameter space can be determined. The factor relating the two predictions will therefore depend solely on the parameters of the reference point and the target point and on the region $j$ under consideration, and is defined as:
%
\begin{equation}
    w_j(\mathbf{c})
    = \frac{\mathrm{Poly}\left(\mathbf{c},\,\mathbf{A}^{j}\right)}
           {\mathrm{Poly}\left(\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}},\,\mathbf{A}^{j}\right)}
    = \frac{\sum_{i} A^{j}_i\, f_i(\mathbf{c})}
           {\sum_{i} A^{j}_i\, f_i(\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}})}\;,
\label{eq:elr_weight}
\end{equation}
%
where $w_j$ is the weight assigned to every event in the reference sample contained within region $j$, and the total weight by which the event contributes to the prediction at the target EFT point is:
%
\begin{equation}
    w(e;\,\mathbf{c}) = w_{\text{ref}}(e)\;w_{j(e)}(\mathbf{c})\;,
\label{eq:elr_event_weight}
\end{equation}
%
where $w_{\text{ref}}(e)$ is the MC generator weight of each event $e$ in the reference sample, such that their sum reproduces $\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}})$; and $j(e)$ denotes the region in which each event falls.\\

While the choice of reference point is, in principle, arbitrary, it is determined by the availability and quality of the sample in practice. To achieve optimal modeling, factors such as greater statistical power, good regional coverage, and the absence of empty bins should be considered. Furthermore, since the normalization of the prediction is provided entirely by the reference sample, the detector response and its reconstruction are preserved throughout the method. This allows the factorization to survive the analysis pipeline, resulting in savings in the simulation budget, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:physical_model}.

On the other hand, once the quotient in Equation \ref{eq:elr_weight} has been determined, it can be used to construct the distribution of any observable, since it is an attribute of the complete event. Overall, the ELR maps the entire dependence on the EFT parameters, from the generation of events to a scalar that can be calculated event by event. The only prerequisite is that the coefficients $A_i$ must have been previously determined, which requires a dedicated procedure for that purpose, and this is what determines the actual scope of the method.\\

\subsubsection{Extraction of the parameterization coefficients}
\label{subsec:ELR_coefficients}
%
Once the method is introduced and as demonstrated above, the results yielded by the ELR are contingent on the ability to determine the coefficients $A_i$. To do so, since Equation \ref{eq:poly_differential} is linear in them, it suffices to evaluate it at a set of points in parameter space for which the prediction is known, provided that the number of such points is equal to or greater than the number of terms $N_A$. In practice, the system is highly overdetermined, and the coefficients are obtained through a fitting process performed independently in each region of phase space. The process is determined by three choices:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the set of variables $\xi$ on which the parameterization is defined, which determines the kinematic information captured by the coefficients,
    \item the binning of these variables, which strikes a balance between resolution and statistical accuracy,
    \item the fitting algorithm, which determines the stability of the coefficients and any potential bias that may arise.
\end{enumerate}
%
In addition, the set of samples used for the fit is designed to cover a broader range of phase space that standard benchmark points, so that the parameterization is valid over the widest possible domain.

The result of this procedure is the set of coefficients and their covariance matrix, which must be propagated to the final prediction in the form of additional uncertainty. Finally, once the coefficients are fixed, they are applied to the reference events using Equation \ref{eq:elr_event_weight}, thereby returning the desired prediction.\\

The specific method for determining the coefficients $A_i$ has evolved over time to the most recent formulation used in this work. In the original formulation of the method \cite{Carvalho:2017vnu}, the coefficients were extracted at LO and differentially in $\xi=\{\mHH,\, |\cos\theta^*|\}$\footnote{a well-motivated choice at LO because $m_{HH}$ is the quantity most sensitive to BSM effects and is uncorrelated with $\cos\theta^*$, and both exhaust the kinematics of a $2\to2$ process, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:physical_model}}. However, this prescription, which has been used for years as a standard procedure \cite{2510.07527}, has certain shortcomings that have become increasingly significant as searches become more sensitivity. First, the completeness of the pair $\{\mHH,\,|\cos\theta^*|\}$ is a valid statement only at the LO. When extending the calculation to NLO QCD, effects appear in the differential distributions induced by initial state radiation (ISR), for example, and the event kinematics are no longer determined by two variables. Furthermore, the distribution of $\cos\theta^*$ turns out to be practically flat throughout the EFT space, so it provides very little information about shape, and the parameterization effectively behaves as one-dimensional in $m_{HH}$, which strongly influences the response to other variables. Furthermore, the range of $m_{HH}$ used in early versions of the method has proven to be limited and its granularity modest, resulting in the invariant high-mass tail — which dominates the sensitivity of the \textit{boosted} categories — being poorly described.

This has led to the expansion of the set of variables through a three-dimensional parameterization with $\xi=\{\mHH,\,|\cos\theta^*|,\,p_T^{\HH}\}$ \cite{Cadamuro:2025car}, where the inclusion of $p_T^{\HH}$ is based on two compelling reasons. First, while $\{\mHH,\,|\cos\theta^*|\}$ fully define the $gg\to HH$ production at LO, $\,p_T^{\HH}\}$ accounts for jet radiation entering the process at NLO. Furthermore, this quantity directly encodes the cuts that the analyses apply to the reconstructed objects, enabling the description of nonlinear effects introduced by the cuts in the differential distributions. The most recent line of research \cite{HHReweighting} maintains the three-dimensional parameterization and also addresses the problem of the $m_{HH}$ domain, such that in the latest formulation of the ELR — and the one used in this work — the granularity is increased by paying special attention to the high-mass tail. In this extension of the method, it is taken into account that subdividing the high-mass region using three-dimensional binning leaves numerous empty bins; thus, three-dimensional binning is used only below $1$~TeV, while one-dimensional binning is retained above that threshold. TABLE BIN EDGES CONSIDERED

It should be noted, however, that while the three-dimensional parameterization is the optimal choice when it comes to incorporating NLO effects, reproducing other quantities such as $p_T^H$, and gaining sensitivity to selection effects on them; the one-dimensional parameterization — disregarding $\cos\theta^*$a, which, as mentioned, provides barely any information — provides the best description of the $m_{HH}$ distribution itself. The choice between the two therefore depends on the context of application.

The first two choices on the previous list are covered. The third point is also noteworthy in terms of the quality of the result, as a fit that weights the samples by their statistical precision automatically penalizes the less densely populated regions, which are often the ones containing the most characteristic information, and thereby introducing a bias. The selection of the algorithm is therefore key to balancing the stability of the coefficients with the absence of such bias, and its effect is particularly evident in the NLO QCD coefficients, whose determination proves to be the most unstable and is associated with the greatest uncertainties.

%HEFT + SMEFT
\subsubsection{Choice of the reference: the multi-reference strategy}
\label{subsec:multireference}
%
The method is fully specified once the coefficients have been determined. The only remaining decision for its application is which reference events to weight in \ref{eq:elr_event_weight}. As noted earlier, there are factors to consider when selecting the reference sample to ensure reliable predictions. The SM is the intuitive choice, as it is available in any analysis; however, it is not optimal in the regions of greatest interest, such as the invariant high-mass tail. Therefore, reweighting toward EFT points that enrich this region involves applying large weights to very few events, resulting in statistical dilution and instability in the prediction. Furthermore, since the coefficients are fitted at the generator level, they do not account for the efficiency of the cuts, which can distort the distributions very differently in the SM and a strongly BSM kinematic point.

The solution to this problem involves using a \textit{multi-reference} strategy, in which rather than using a single sample, a set of samples is used as a starting point. These samples are chosen for their statistical stability in different regions of phase space and for covering the kinematic diversity allowed by EFT space. In this strategy, each sample is reweighted independently, and the resulting predictions are combined region by region using a weighted average based on their statistical uncertainty. This procedure overcomes the limitations inherent of starting with a single signal topology, making ELR a more robust method for prediction.

\subsubsection{Validation at generator and analysis level}

Overall, the discussion throughout this section has demonstrated that the ELR modeling method provides a differential prediction whose dependence on the EFT parameters is exact and can be evaluated at any point in EFT space, whether in five-dimensional HEFT or nine-dimensional SMEFT. The results presented indicate that this tool therefore allows for reliable predictions based on a limited simulation budget and with the advantage of constructing any observable a posteriori, including the reconstructed quantities on which experimental searches rely. Furthermore, the procedure does not merely provide a central prediction but also the uncertainty associated with the modeling itself, allowing it to be incorporated into the statistical fitting. Although the method has been extensively used in numerous analyses, its most recent formulations have considerably expanded its scope by considering its extension to NLO QCD, three-dimensional parameterization, the multi-reference strategy, and coverage of the invariant high-mass tail, as well as its generalization to the SMEFT formalism with subleading operators. All these improvements have transformed a procedure originally restricted to LO and the $m_{HH}$ distribution into a description that can be used in the regions and formalisms required by current searches.

However, while ELR eliminates the need to simulate a sample for each point in the parameter space, it does not eliminate the cost of processing the resulting prediction: obtaining the final distribution at a given point requires iterating through the reference samples, evaluating the weight event by event, and reconstructing the histogram throughout the entire analysis chain. Moreover, this process must be repeated for each point in the scan and for each systematic variation. In a dense scan — especially within likelihood fit, where the signal must be evaluated continuously across the parameters — the cost can become prohibitive in certain searches. This consideration enables the use of ELR as a tool capable of establishing a natural reference against which to evaluate the convergence of any other modeling procedure, such as the matrix inversion method described below. Furthermore, the availability of an analytical and differential description plays a decisive role in this work, as it allows for the generation of $m_{HH}$ templates at arbitrary points without the need to simulate or process any events, and thus to evaluate the quality of a prediction at a negligible computational cost. This feature is essential for addressing the central problem of this chapter, which is the development and extension of the matrix inversion method. The ELR thus serves as the foundation upon which the optimization developed in Section \ref{sec:full_model} is built, once this modeling method is introduced in the following section.

\subsection{Matrix inversion method}
\label{subsec:matrix_inversion}
% Descripcon general
The following modeling method, called the Matrix Inversion Method, is based on the same physical model as in the previous Section \ref{subsec:physical_model}, but exploits it in a direction opposite to that of event-level reweighting. While ELR sought to determine the coefficients of the parameterization $A_i$, the matrix method seeks to eliminate dependence on them. To develop this method, it is useful to rewrite Equation \ref{eq:poly_differential} by explicitly separating the monomial vector $\vec{f}(\mathbf{c}) = (f_1(\mathbf{c}),\ dots,f_{N_A}(\mathbf{c}))^\top$ and the coefficient vector $\vec{a} = (A_1,\dots,A_{N_A})^\top$. In this way, the known analytical function of the EFT parameters is contained in $\vec{f}(\mathbf{c})$, while the information to be determined is contained in the vector $\vec{a}$. Accordingly, the prediction of the cross section can be written as:
%
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}) = \vec{f}^{\;\top}(\mathbf{c})\cdot\vec{a}\;,
\label{eq:master_model}
\end{equation}
%
Its bilinear nature — linear in the monomials and linear in the coefficients — allows for two complementary interpretations. While the ELR in Section \ref{subsec:ELR} relies on the explicit determination of $\vec{a}$, the matrix inversion method eliminates $\vec{a}$ from the problem. For this purpose, the method requires predictions at a set of $N_A$ basis points ${\mathbf{c}1,\dots,\mathbf{c}{N_A}}$, that is, $N_A$ EFT scenarios for which the cross section or differential distribution is known. Evaluating Equation \ref{eq:master_model} at each of these yields a linear system in $\vec{a}$:
%
\begin{equation}
  \vec{\sigma}_{\text{basis}} \equiv
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}_1) \\ \vdots \\ \sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}_{N_A})
  \end{pmatrix}
  =
  \underbrace{
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \vec{f}^{\;\top}(\mathbf{c}_1) \\ \vdots \\ \vec{f}^{\;\top}(\mathbf{c}_{N_A})
  \end{pmatrix}}_{F}
  \cdot\;\vec{a}\;,
\label{eq:matrix_system}
\end{equation}
%
where the matrix $F$ groups the monomial vectors evaluated at the basis points by row. If these vectors are linearly independent, then $F$ is invertible, and the system can be solved: $\vec{a} = F^{-1},\vec{\sigma}_{\text{basis}}$. Rather than using this solution to recover the coefficients, however, it suffices to substitute it back into Equation \ref{eq:master_model}, thereby eliminating $\vec{a}$ entirely from the problem:
%
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c})
    = \underbrace{\vec{f}^{\;\top}(\mathbf{c})\,F^{-1}}_{\textstyle \vec{w}^{\;\top}(\mathbf{c})}
      \cdot\;\vec{\sigma}_{\text{basis}}
    = \sum_{k=1}^{N_A} w_k(\mathbf{c})\,\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}_k)\;.
\label{eq:matrix_master}
\end{equation}
In this manner, the prediction at any point in parameter space is expressed as a linear combination of the predictions at the $N_A$ basis points, with weights $\vec{w}(\mathbf{c}) = \left(F^{-1}\right)^{\top}\vec{f}(\mathbf{c})$ that are known analytic functions of the coordinates of the target point and those of the basis, completely eliminating the dependence on the coefficients $A_i$ and thus requiring no fit.\\

Since the matrix $F$ is constructed exclusively from the couplings of the basis points, and the monomial vector is universal, nothing in the previous derivation depends on the region of phase space under consideration. Therefore, the argument can be systematically repeated for each bin $j$ simply by substituting $\vec{a}\to\vec{a}^{,(j)}$ in Equation \ref{eq:master_model} and the total cross sections with the corresponding differential predictions in Equation \ref{eq:matrix_system}. The matrix inversion is consistent, as are the weights:
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\xi_j}\,\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c})
    = \sum_{k=1}^{N_A} w_k(\mathbf{c})\;
      \frac{d}{d\xi_j}\,\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}_k)\;.
\label{eq:matrix_differential}
\end{equation}
%
where it should be noted that the weights $w_k(\mathbf{c})$ are not indexed by $j$, meaning that the same linear combination can reproduce any differential distribution. In addition, the previously established and reiterated argument regarding the preservation of the factorization throughout the analysis chain is maintained; the index $j$ can refer equally to a bin of a generated variable or to a bin of the final observable of the search following the complete selection of the analysis.\\

%%%%%------------AÑADIRÉ EL STATS AWARE?????---------------
%It should also be noted that, if more than $N_A$ basis points are available, the system in Equation \ref{eq:matrix_system} becomes overdetermined, and $F$ is no longer strictly invertible. The solution then involves a least-squares fit, in which the statistical information from the basis predictions can be explicitly incorporated through their covariance matrix. This extension of the method, which allows for the use of broader bases than the minimum required, is discussed in Section \ref{sec:full_model}.

\subsubsection{Practical advantages and scope of application}
% ventajas y ámbito de uso
The practical advantage is immediate: the combination is performed directly on the final histograms of the analysis rather than event by event, so that the analysis chain is traversed only once per basis point. Consequently, generating a prediction at an arbitrary point in parameter space is reduced to a weighted sum of $N_A$ previously calculated histograms, with weights evaluated analytically. In contrast to ELR, the scanning cost is no longer dependent on the number of evaluated points, leading to a substantial reduction in computational expense. Its appeal in the experimental context is therefore evident, since the method allows for a complete EFT interpretation based solely on a small number of fully processed samples, with the added advantage that it can be implemented directly within the statistical fitting tool, as the EFT parameters can be treated as continuous parameters of the likelihood fit. This decoupling of the EFT interpretation from the generation and processing of the samples, while moving it to the final stage of the analysis, offers a clear operational advantage.

Because of this, ATLAS and CMS have used this method extensively to interpret their searches for Higgs boson pair production \cite{CMS_combination, ATLAS:2024pov, ATLAS:2025tkn}, and is implemented in the standard statistical modeling tools used by the scientific community. The application of the method is not restricted to the $gg\to HH$ process; rather, the same construction applies to other production modes, such as $qqHH$, $VHH$, and $t\bar{t}HH$, as well as to the production of three Higgs bosons, provided that the cross section allows for the factorization described in Section \ref{subsec:physical_model}.

To date, the EFT interpretations using this modeling strategy have been carried out in parameter spaces of reduced dimensionality, where the number of basis points required to solve the system is minimal. One example is the case of one-dimensional scanning in $\kappa_{\lambda}$, where, by fixing the remaining couplings to their SM values, the amplitude of Equation \ref{eq:amplitude_decomposition} reduces to the box and triangle structures; thus, $N_A=3$ independent predictions are sufficient to determine the model. The same number of basis points is required when exploring the plane $(\kappa_{\lambda},\kt)$, which consists of the same amplitude structures. Adding the contact vertex $c_2$, however, introduces a third structure, bringing the the total to six. It is within this three-dimensional restriction of HEFT space that the most recent experimental interpretations are situated. In these cases of low problem dimensionality, the required number of predictions is typically on par with the number of BSM scenarios generated by a standard analysis. The basis selection is then made heuristically: the kinematically most disparate scenarios are selected by direct inspection of their $m_{HH}$ spectra, and the choice is validated a posteriori by comparing the method’s prediction with independently simulated data points — an approach that has worked reasonably well thanks to the low dimensionality of the cases.

However, extending the coverage to the full HEFT space of five couplings, or to that of nine Wilson coefficients in SMEFT, causes the number of basis points to increase to the twenty-three required by the NLO parameterization of Equation \ref{eq:heft_diff} and the forty required by Equation \ref{eq:smeft_diff}, respectively. The choice of basis, therefore, is no longer subject to inspection, meaning that the quality of the prediction is contingent on the appropriate handling of the basis selection.\\

\subsubsection{Basis choice}
% Caveats: elección de la base

A poorly chosen basis can lead to flawed method performance due to various potential issues. If not properly managed, these issues can compound, resulting in significant problems. First, the stability of the method is determined by the condition number of $F$ in Equation \ref{eq:matrix_system}, that is, the degree to which the monomial vectors approach degeneracy.  The points of a basis that generate nearly parallel monomial vectors produce an inverse matrix with very large entries and alternating signs, a behavior that is fully transmitted to the weights $w_k(\mathbf{c})$ in Equation \ref{eq:matrix_differential} and, with them, to the prediction.

The consequences of a deficient basis can be roughly quantified. Assuming statistically independent basis points, the error propagation in Equation \ref{eq:matrix_differential} is
%
$$
    \left[\Delta\left(\frac{d\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c})}{d\xi_j}\right)\right]^{2}
    = \sum_{k=1}^{N_A} w_k^{2}(\mathbf{c})\,
      \left[\Delta\left(\frac{d\sigma_{\ggHH}(\mathbf{c}_k)}{d\xi_j}\right)\right]^{2}\;,
$$
%
so that the quantity $\sum_k w_k^{2}(\mathbf{c})$ acts as an amplification factor for the statistical uncertainties of the samples that make up the basis. Since the weights are functions of the objective point, this factor can grow without limit. Consequently, in a poorly conditioned basis, a small cross section may end up being modeled as the difference between two much larger quantities, which can lead to disproportionate uncertainties precisely in regions that may prove to be decisive for the sensitivity of the analysis.

On the other hand, an insufficiently independent basis can introduce a bias in the shape of the predicted distribution that is not accounted for by any assigned uncertainty, and which therefore propagates unchecked into the statistical fit. The combination of these two effects can lead to non-physical predictions or bins with negative values that are meaningless and can cause standard signal extraction tools to fail.

It should also be noted that none of these properties are a global characteristic of the basis; rather, they are local to the point being predicted. For instance, the same basis may perform flawlessly in the SM environment and catastrophically in a given BSM direction, or it may correctly reproduce the overall performance while failing in the least populated bins of the distribution. It follows that validation limited to the inclusive effective section or to a handful of reference scenarios can be completely misleading, and the only way to obtain a meaningful evaluation is to perform it differentially across the entire domain to be covered.

Furthermore, each basis point requires a separate full simulation, which represents a considerable cost in the case of twenty-three or forty complete productions. After careful consideration, it has been determined that the choice of basis in a five- or nine-dimensional space cannot be addressed using the heuristic procedure applied to lower-dimensional spaces. This is due to the fact that there is no clear intuition that allows for the anticipation of the constraints on a set of twenty-three or forty points, and a systematic exploration of alternatives would require an unmanageable volume of simulations.\\

\subsubsection{Basis optimization: the role of event-level reweighting}
% ELR como tool para abordar el problema de forma sistematica
Therefore, extending the method to high-dimensional systems — such as the case of complete EFT descriptions — requires a systematic approach to the problem of basis selection. It is necessary to define a search strategy capable of exploring the entire coupling domain under consideration, as well as to establish a performance metric that objectively quantifies the quality of a basis in that space. The objective is therefore to identify a favorable configuration in a search space whose dimensionality is comparable to that of problems typically addressed using machine learning techniques.

However, evaluating the objective function to converge in a space of this dimensionality requires on the order of $10^5$ to $10^6$ evaluations of the objective function, which is unfeasible if one needs to simulate and process each sample of the test basis points and each objective sample. As previously mentioned, the ELR is a key factor in this matter. As outlined in Section \ref{subsec:ELR}, the ELR provides an analytical and differential description of the cross section, valid for any point in HEFT or SMEFT space, enabling the generation of templates for distributions of interest, such as $m_{HH}$, without the need for simulating or processing a single event, and at a negligible cost. Additionally, generating "toys" from the analytical distribution allows for reproducing the effect of a finite simulation statistic, thereby characterizing the error amplification previously discussed, so that the evaluation is not limited to shape agreement but incorporates the statistical precision that the basis would have in actual use. This provides a continuous test set across the entire coupling space, enabling precise measurement of the performance of any basis. Furthermore, it eliminates the need to simulate each basis point separately: the basis coordinates become completely free and optimizable.\\

With these elements in place the search for the basis can be approached systematically within the framework of hyperparameter optimization, analogous to that of a machine learning problem, relying on a parameterization of the basis as a vector of hyperparameters, a bounded objective function that aggregates the discussed quality criteria — shape agreement with the true prediction, statistical accuracy of the resulting combination, and the absence of bins with negative content — and a global search algorithm. Under this approach, the choice that was traditionally guided by inspection becomes a reproducible procedure applicable to complete EFT models. The construction of a complete HEFT and SMEFT model for the $gg\to HH$ production constitutes the main contribution of this chapter and is the objective of Section \ref{sec:full_model}, where validity of the method will also be examined for the optimized bases.

\subsection{Finding a good basis}
\label{subsec:finding_basis}
As has been established, the only remaining ingredient needed to reliably apply the matrix inversion method in complete EFT spaces is the basis, i.e., the set of BSM scenarios from which any other point in the parameter space is modeled. The requirements that must be met have already been stated: they must adequately cover the parameter space and capture the relevant features of the signal, remain sufficiently far from degeneracy, and not compromise the statistical accuracy of the resulting prediction; and the consequences of failing to meet them were discussed in detail in Section \ref{subsec:matrix_inversion}. The remaining task is to translate these requirements into an operational procedure, which will be the focus of this section.

A procedure of this nature requires four elements. First, a controlled environment is necessary to generate signal shapes virtually instantaneously, which is essential for iterative strategies and that additionally allows for the reproduction of the finite sample size effect that would be observed in real applications. Second, a quantifiable criterion that measures how faithfully a given basis reproduces the prediction at an arbitrary point in the coupling space and that simultaneously penalizes any degradation in statistical precision, so that it can be evaluated repeatedly throughout the optimization. Third, a parameterization of the basis itself that reduces the search to a manageable dimension and eliminates trivial degeneracies that the minimizer would otherwise explore without gaining any information in return. And finally, a global search algorithm capable of traversing the resulting landscape. The remainder of this section develops these four elements in that same order, leading to optimized bases for the full HEFT space and for its SMEFT analog.\\

\subsubsection{Toy based evaluation}
\label{subsec:toy_evaluation}
First, the question arises of how to methodically assess the performance of a given basis so that iterative optimization can be carried out. To avhieve this objective, it is essential to have a rapid mechanism capable of generating signal forms. This mechanism is the parameterization of the cross section in Equations \ref{eq:heft_diff} and \ref{eq:smeft_diff}, evaluated using the coefficients $A_i$ obtained through event level reweighting of Section \ref{subsec:ELR} \cite{HHReweighting}, which provides the differential cross section of $gg\to HH$ in $m_{HH}$ at any point in the HEFT or SMEFT spaces without the need to simulate or process a single event. This distribution is taken as the reference truth.

This description, however, produces a perfect signal shape, which would skew the evaluation, since in real-world applications, predictions are derived from simulated samples with finite statistics, and it is precisely this limitation that the linear combination amplifies. Therefore, to reproduce the finite statistics available in a real-world scenario, the analytical distribution is used as a probability density function from which simulations are run, and it is the resulting histograms — rather than the exact curve — that are fed into the matrix inversion method. The number of toys thrown is not arbitrary, but chosen to match the size of the LHE samples against which the method is validated later in this Chapter, so that the toy environment reproduces the statistical precision the method would actually encounter and both exercises can be read on a common scale. Figure \ref{fig:statvar} illustrates the procedure in the HEFT case: to showcase the dependence of the distribution on the number of thrown toys, the top two panels and the bottom-left one show that, for a small number of toys, the distribution is markedly noisy and the statistical uncertainty is large, whereas as the number of toys increases, the distribution converges toward the underlying exact analytical prediction. The remaining panel shows this prediction for the SM and for some representative BSM scenarios, where couplings not explicitly indicated are set to their SM values, and demonstrates the diversity of morphologies that the basis must be capable of reproducing.

By generating these synthetic distributions, a controlled environment is established in which the predictions obtained from different sets of basis points can be consistently evaluated. The next issue to address is how to define a quantitative criterion that would translate these comparisons into a single measure of performance.\\

\subsubsection{Quality metrics and objective function}
\label{subsec:objective}
%
Therefore, once the method for generating signal shapes has been established, the optimization requires an objective function that quantifies the ability of the method to model the signal across the entire parameter space under consideration. Ideally, the template resulting from the matrix combination should match the one obtained by direct simulation — or, in this case, the toy distribution — and should also exhibit a statistical uncertainty that does not appreciably exceed that of the templates provided as input. In addition to these two quality criteria, a third criterion of a practical nature should be considered: the absence of negative event counts, which, even though they are covered by statistical uncertainty, lack physical meaning and cause standard signal extraction tools to fail. To quantify the three criteria in a single optimizable function, the histogram of a signal magnitude, such as $m_{HH}$, obtained from the toy models, $H_t$, is compared with that obtained using the matrix inversion method, $H_m$, through the following terms:
%
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{Wasserstein distance} or earth movers distance~\cite{ramdas2015wassersteinsampletestingrelated,2020SciPy-NMeth} $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ quantifies the "cost" of transforming one distribution into the other. It is a measure commonly used in machine learning problems because it always has a well-defined gradient, and is stable with respect to small variations. It also offers a specific advantage for comparing kinematic histograms: it is not a bin-to-bin measure, but a genuine distance, i.e., a global shift in the spectrum of $m_{HH}$ toward slightly lower masses does not result in the dramatic differences that bin-to-bin comparison would reveal in the regions of steepest gradient, while the effect through the experimental lens of resolution and signal acceptance is actually small. Being a transport distance, it carries the units of the observable, and the finite size of the templates leaves a residual distance that decreases as the inverse square root of the number of events.\footnote{For the template sizes used here this residual amounts to a \textcolor{red}{few GeV}, comparable to the distances found where the method performs best.} Its absolute value is therefore interpretable only at fixed statistics, and all comparisons that follow use a common template size.
    \item A \textbf{statistics error} by comparing the per bin error $\delta_i^{m}$ of $H_{m}$ with the corresponding bin content $b_i^{m}$ and $\delta_i^{m}$ with the per bin error $\delta_i^{t}$ of $H_{t}$:\\ $f_{stat}(H_{t},H_{m})=1/N_\text{bins}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{i=N_\text{bins}}(0.5\cdot\delta_i^\text{m}/b_i^{m} +0.5\cdot\delta_i^{m}/\delta_i^{t})$
    \item The number of \textbf{negative bins} $N_{\rm neg}$ in $H_m$, which is counted directly and used to penalize basis choices that lead to negative bins in the resulting signal template that make no physical sense.
\end{itemize}
%

Ideally, when the modeling works properly, $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ should be small, on the order of the statistical residual of the templates. Conversely, a $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ of tens of GeV indicates a macroscopic distortion. For $f_{stat}(H_{t},H_{m})$, however, the reference is not zero but rather $0.5$ when the combination does not degrade the precision of the templates, whereas values close to one indicate that the error per bin has been substantially amplified. And, trivially, $N_{neg}$ is expected to be exactly zero. Thus, the first two metrics quantify different modes of failure, while the third simply indicates the presence of a qualitative failure.

Given these ingredients, the objective function is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scorefunc}
    s(H_{t},H_{m})=\frac{1}{1+a\cdot d_W(H_{t},H_{m}) + b\cdot f_{stat}(H_{t},H_{m}) + c\cdot N_{neg}}
\end{equation}
With parameters $a,\,b,\,c$ determining the weight of each respective objective, and where the default values $a=0.6,\,b=0.3$, and $c=2$ have been adopted because they yielded the best results after testing different configurations. When constructed in this manner, the score is bounded between $0$ and $1$, and decreases continuously toward zero as any of the three failure modes occurs. The upper bound, however, is not attained even in the ideal case of perfect agreement in shape and absence of negative bins, since reaching it would require the three terms of the denominator to vanish simultaneously, and neither of the first two does. The second term of $f_{stat}(H_{t},H_{m})$ compares the uncertainty of the modeled template with that of the target, so that the absence of statistical dilution corresponds to $\delta_i^{m}=\delta_i^{t}$, and the metric therefore settles at $f_{stat}(H_{t},H_{m})\simeq0.5$ instead of vanishing; likewise, $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ retains a residual value set by the finite size of the templates being compared, as discussed below. The score of an ideal basis is therefore of the order of a few tenths, and the absolute value it takes is not meaningful in itself: it depends on the weights $a,\,b,\,c$, on the binning and on the composition of the test set, and it is only to be read as a relative measure between competing bases.\\

\subsubsection{Basis parameterization}
%
After defining the objective function, the next problem to be addressed is formulating the basis search as a hyperparameter optimization problem. The aim is to optimize the basis:
%
\begin{equation}
 B=\left[ \begin{bmatrix}  c_1^{1},  & c_1^{2},  & \cdots & c_1^{nc}, \end{bmatrix},\, \begin{bmatrix}  c_2^{1},  & c_2^{2},  & \cdots & c_2^{nc},  \end{bmatrix},\, ...\, \begin{bmatrix}  c_{nb}^{1},  & c_{nb}^{2},  & \cdots & c_{nb}^{nc},  \end{bmatrix}\right]
 \label{eq:basicbasis}
\end{equation}
%
for a problem with $n_c$ couplings — five in HEFT and nine in SMEFT — and $n_b$ basis points, such that it maximizes $s(H_t,H_m)$ over a wide range of test cases.

In general, this involves $n_b\times n_c$ hyperparameters, that that is, $115$ in HEFT and $360$ in SMEFT, and also suffers from severe degeneracy: there are $n_b!$ equivalent bases that differ only in the order of their points, which the minimizer must explore without gaining any information in return.

Therefore, to reduce the number of hyperparameters and resolve the degeneracy problem, a minimal form for the basis is postulated, inspired by the structure of the coefficients $A_i$ themselves. This formulation associates each basis point with one coefficient from Equations \ref{eq:heft_diff} and \ref{eq:smeft_diff}, activating only the couplings involved in the associated monomial and setting the remaining ones to zero. Thus, in HEFT, the point associated with $A_1$, whose monomial is $\kappa_t^4$, takes the form $[0,A_1,0,0,0]$ in the parameterization $[\kappa_\lambda,\kappa_t,c_2,c_g,c_{2g}]$; equivalently, for $A_2$, with monomial $\kappa_\lambda \kappa_t^3$, the associated form would be $[A_{2a},A_{2b},0,0,0]$; and so on up to $A_{23}$. \subsubsection{Algorithmic optimization}
%
Once the optimization formulation has been properly defined, the most appropriate solution for a problem of this complexity is sought. In this case, the particle swarm optimization~\cite{488968,Tani:2020dyi} (PSO) algorithm is used, which is possibly the best option for a problem of this complexity. PSO is a genetic algorithm in which a set of particles “flies” through the hyperparameter space with a given direction and momentum. In each iteration, each particle evaluates the objective function based on the hyperparameter coordinates it currently occupies. From there, it experiences an attractive force toward the position of its personal best score and toward the coordinates of a global best—that is, the hyperparameter coordinates corresponding to the best score the entire swarm has detected so far. Communication of this global best is not instantaneous; rather, in each iteration, each particle updates its knowledge by exchanging information with a specific number of particles in the swarm, its informants. The position for the next iteration is then updated based on both attractions to the two best locations and its momentum; the latter prevents the particles from becoming easily trapped, while the dual attraction to the personal best and the global best mitigates the risk of convergence toward a local extreme.

Bayesian optimization~\cite{BayesPaper1,BayesBook,Tani:2022izz} (BO) was also investigated as a potential solution to the problem; however, the PSO was found to be the more reliable choice as BOs surrogate function never seemed to really learn the underlying score landscape.

Finally, the configuration adopted for PSO uses XXX particles in HEFT and XXXX in SMEFT case, with the number of informants set to X. Here, the performance of each basis is evaluated by summing the scores from the toy-based test points in both 2D scan planes, with the parameter ranges listed in Table \ref{tab:ranges}, and a set of XXXX random points scattered throughout the EFT parameter space.\\

The convergence performance achieved by the PSO in both cases is shown in Figure ~\ref{fig:convergence}, which displays the best personal score attained by each particle up to a given iteration, along with its median and mean across the swarm, such that as the iterations progress, the PSO particles find progressively better solutions. It is noteworthy that, toward the end, the median of the distributions lies above its mean, indicating a distribution skewed toward low scores, with most of the swarm concentrated near the optimum and a residual tail of particles that continue to explore far from it — a behavior typical of a healthy swarm. The color gradient, from purple to yellow, represents the progression of the optimization from early to late iterations. Different configurations of the score parameters $a,\,b,\,c$ of the objective function were tested and were found to yield similarly strong basis sets. It should be emphasized, however, that the absolute value of the score is not meaningful in itself, since it depends on the weights $a,\,b,\,c$ used, on the binning, and on the composition of the test set, and it is only useful as a relative measure.

Several properties can be observed in the resulting basis sets. First, a considerable fraction of the benchmarks lie very close to the domain boundaries shown in Table \ref{tab:ranges}. This is expected behavior rather than a pathology, stemming from the fact that $F$ is better conditioned when the monomial vectors are as different from one another as possible. Therefore, the optimizer tends to choose extreme configurations to achieve this maximum separation. This also clarifies that the optimized bases are restricted to the domain in which they were sought, and that expanding the domain would require repeating the procedure rather than extrapolating the present result. Secondly, none of the benchmarks correspond to a scenario that an analysis would test on its own. Instead, they are deliberately spread across configurations whose individual cross sections differ by orders of magnitude, which is precisely what allows the linear combination to reach any intermediate point.

In relation to this last feature, the kinematic coverage of the basis sets is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:basisoverview}, where the range of the different $m_{HH}$ shapes spanned by the two optimized basis sets can be seen. For visualization purposes, only a few representative benchmarks are shown explicitly, while the range of kinematics across all benchmarks is shaded. It becomes evident that the two sets encompass a broad spectrum of kinematic scenarios, extending well beyond the SM shape in both directions: from the threshold-enhanced configurations that cluster near the $m_{HH}$ turn-on—characteristic of large values of the trilinear coupling, to the hard spectra that remain populated out to the TeV region, driven by contact interactions. This spread is not an incidental feature but the very property required by the matrix inversion method, since the prediction at an arbitrary point is constructed as a linear combination of these shapes: a basis whose members were kinematically similar would be unable to reach the extremes of the parameter space except through the large, alternating weights that Section \ref{subsec:matrix_inversion} identified as the source of error amplification.\\

\subsection{Closure and validation at generator level}
\label{subsec:closure}
%
The basis sets presented in the previous section were therefore obtained by maximizing the score of Equation \ref{eq:scorefunc} on test cases generated from the same analytical parameterization that the matrix inversion method is intended to use. By construction, the method is self-consistent, meaning that a high score confirms that the optimizer has converged to a well-conditioned basis. Therefore, once the optimal bases have been obtained by applying the procedure described in the preceding section, it is necessary to evaluate their performance, which is assessed at different levels.

This section represents the initial level of evaluation, which is confined to the generator level. Detector simulation, the trigger, and analysis selection are deliberately excluded at this stage to ensure that the modeling itself can be isolated as the potential source of discrepancy between the target and the modeled template. Similarly, the evaluation is structured in two phases: an initial closure test phase, where the $m_{HH}$ distributions yielded by the matrix method are compared against toy templates; and a second phase that extends the $m_{HH}$ toy shapes based on a simplified model of the process to LHE events capable of capturing all the kinematic information of the process. In total generality, the consistency of the method is evaluated using the same metrics defined in the optimization process in Section \ref{subsec:objective}, so that the reliability of the description is interpreted in terms of the Wasserstein distance, i.e., the shape agreement, and the relative statistics error.\\

\subsubsection{Toy-based closure}
\ref{subsec:toy_evaluation}
The method is evaluated against toy templates in the closure test, with the evaluation performed across a dense set of coupling configurations that covers the ranges in Table \ref{tab:ranges}. This scenario is the same one that served as the evaluation environment during optimization, as described in Section \ref{subsec:toy_evaluation}, where the target distribution $H_t$ is obtained directly by sampling the analytical prediction from Equation \ref{eq:heft_diff} using a finite number of toy models capable of replicating the sample size available in a real-world application; and $H_m$ is constructed by linearly combining, with the matrix inversion weights, the templates of the basis points generated in the same manner.

Hence, the objective function and the basis both stem from the same parameterization, and since the inversion is an algebraic identity provided that the monomial matrix is not degenerate, the combination would reproduce the objective function exactly with infinite statistics. Consequently, any remaining discrepancy can only be attributed to the propagation of the finite size of the templates through the morphing weights, amplified to the extent that the basis is ill-conditioned according to the mechanism described in Section~\ref{subsec:matrix_inversion}.\\

Under these conditions, the closure does not evaluate the correctness of the method, as it is correct by construction; instead, it determines the extent to which a specific basis deviates from that exact equality due to finite statistics. The metrics $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ and $f_{stat}(H_{t},H_{m})$ quantify the effects inherent to the choice of bases. It is important to note that while optimization required reducing them to a single number that would allow the ranking of the bases, the diagnosis requires a different approach, as the two metrics correspond to distinct modes of failure. Therefore, they are examined separately here to measure, respectively, the agreement in form and the degradation in precision relative to that of the input templates. Subjecting a non-optimized basis to the same closure test enables direct isolation of the contribution of optimization and verification that the score from Equation \ref{eq:scorefunc} translates into an effective improvement in modeling.

\subsubsection{Extending the evaluation to LHE events}

However, the previous approach is limited to the analysis of toy models, and these models are simplified representations of the process and do not contain complete kinematic information. The subsequent logical step is therefore to replace the sampled distributions with events generated using the full matrix element calculation, which do reproduce the complete kinematics of the process.\\

The events used for validation have already been entered, since they constitute the same set of samples used to determine the $A_i$ coefficients of the parameterization, as described in Section \ref{subsec:ELR_coefficients}. A total of 5000 samples, each containing XXX events, corresponding to random combinations of coupling parameters drawn from Gaussian distributions centered around the SM values, ensuring that the generated events capture a wide variety of kinematic configurations. In addition, the coupling ranges spanned by these samples are wider than those of Table \ref{tab:ranges} used in the basis optimization, so that the entire domain over which the basis is designed to operate is contained within them.

\textcolor{red}{
The generation process is performed at the matrix element level using \texttt{POWHEG-BOX-V2} \cite{powheg1,powheg2,powheg3}, for the nonlinear implementation of $gg\to HH$ up to NLO in QCD with full dependence on the top quark mass \cite{HEFTpowheg}, and the events are used without being subjected to a parton shower, since the matrix inversion method operates on distributions at the matrix element level. Full details of the generator configuration can be found in \cite{HHReweighting}.}

Under these conditions, the evaluation is organized by reserving 1000 of the 5000 samples for modeling the basis points using ELR, while the remaining 4000 define the test set against which the prediction is evaluated, such that they are drawn from different sets. It should be noted, however, that since the coefficients $A_i$ on which the generation of toys — and thus the determination of the basis coordinates — is based, and the basis templates also come from a subset of these LHE events, validation on LHE should not be understood as independent of the parameterization; that is, it does not provide a prediction from a different source, but rather the complete kinematics of the process and a statistic whose structure is that of a truly generated sample, which is exactly what the toy environment lacks.\\

\subsubsection{Implications for realistic analysis}
\label{subsec:uncertainties}
%
The above characterization establishes the behavior of the method, but the metrics must be translated into a concrete prescription for its use in an analysis. Based on the observed behavior, it can be concluded that the method is capable of reproducing the $m_{HH}$ spectrum of a kinematically complete LHE description with the same level of accuracy as it satisfied the closure condition, with the coupling configurations in which it fails representing a very small fraction of the explored space. Their presence, however, requires adding an additional uncertainty to the prediction specifically intended to account for these rare discrepancies.

The quantity that emerges from the discussion as a logical basis for setting this uncertainty is $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ itself, since it is precisely this quantity that quantifies the discrepancy in a way that statistical uncertainty does not capture in the problematic quadrant. It is therefore proposed to assign to every signal distribution obtained using this method a bin-by-bin uncertainty \textcolor{red}{ranging from $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})/2$ to $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ percent of the content of the bin, independent of the statistical uncertainty of the prediction itself and added to it.} Two features of this prescription are worth making explicit. It reads the numerical value of a quantity carrying the units of $m_{HH}$ as a percentage of the bin content, a correspondence that is an empirical calibration for the spectrum and range considered here rather than a dimensional identity. And the distance it is built from contains, besides any genuine mismodeling, the residual term discussed in Section \ref{subsec:objective}, so that it overestimates the bias and the resulting band errs on the conservative side.\\

Therefore, the foundation for this uncertainty is entirely empirical, and the outcomes of its application is depicted at four representative test points, encompassing the full range, in Figure \ref{fig:heftexamples}. The figure presents a comparison of the $m_{HH}$ spectrum of the LHE sample with that predicted by the method; the proposed band is also superimposed to illustrate what each of the $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ values in the distribution represents in terms of the shape of the distribution, as well as to delimit the range over which the assigned uncertainty does cover the discrepancy.

The first point shown (top left) corresponds to $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})\approx2$, which, as can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:performance}, is the typical case and the one that describes the vast majority of test points: both distributions are indistinguishable to the naked eye, and the assigned band is correspondingly narrow. The next two (top right and bottom left), around values of $10$ and $30$, illustrate progressively more unfavorable situations where the discrepancy begins to become apparent, although in both cases it still remains within the proposed band. It is only in the last case, above $60$ (bottom right), that the disagreement becomes clearly visible and is no longer accounted for by the assigned uncertainty. The prescription therefore covers the discrepancy throughout the range that contains the overwhelming majority of the parameter space, and ceases to do so only in the extreme configurations that populate the far tail of Figure \ref{fig:performance}. These correspond to highly non-linear coupling combinations, intrinsically difficult to model, which lie outside the regions that experimental scans typically explore.

Figure \ref{fig:heftexamples} therefore demonstrates that the prescription is innocuous in cases where the method is already operating correctly, since it does not amplify the uncertainty in the majority of the parameter space, while it becomes important precisely in the cases that are poorly described by the method, where the statistical uncertainty alone would present a poorly modeled spectrum as well known. This is what justifies linking the assignment of the uncertainty to $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$, with the caveat made explicit above that the band is calibrated to absorb the discrepancy of the regime in which the method degrades gradually, and not that of the extreme tail.

Although the Wasserstein distance measured against the LHE spectrum itself — which is available by construction in a validation — has been used here, it remains to be specified how to obtain the value of $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ that determines the band in a specific application. To this end, the toy environment plays a crucial role, since its evaluation does not necessitate the generation or processing a single event; thus, $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ can be computed densely over complete planes of the coupling space, and it is then sufficient to read the corresponding value, so that the method can be applied in any analysis.\\

This concludes the evaluation at the generator level. The closure has established that the optimization fulfills its purpose and that the resulting basis provides uniform performance across the coupling space, in contrast to the localized degradation exhibited by a non-optimized basis. Furthermore, the extension to LHE events has demonstrated that the method reproduces a spectrum derived from a full matrix element calculation equally well. Moreover, the few configurations in which the model fails without this being reflected in the statistical uncertainty receive a systematic uncertainty derived from $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$, which can be evaluated at any point without simulation cost and which absorbs the discrepancy everywhere except in the extreme tail of the distribution, where the residual cases are both rare and confined to coupling configurations that experimental scans do not explore.

These findings, however, are based on distributions that lack any selection criteria, and therefore do not take into account effects such as acceptance, efficiency, or resolution. This is not the case in a real analysis, where the observed spectrum is the one that survives the trigger, the reconstruction, and a set of cuts that depend on the kinematics of the events — and which therefore act differently on each point in the coupling space. It is therefore necessary to determine whether the agreement demonstrated here at the generator level holds at the end of the chain, which is the objective of the following Section \ref{subsec:realistic}.\\


\subsection{Performance in a realistic analysis}
\label{subsec:realistic}
%%%%% enlace
The generator-level validation in the preceding section has demonstrated the validity of the method in the toys environment for which it was optimized, as well as for LHE events, which include the complete calculation of the matrix element. However, validation of the inversion matrix method cannot be considered complete until it is evaluated in terms of realistic analyses, in which criteria are applied that act on the kinematics of the events and are therefore dependent on the point in coupling space. Given that each coupling configuration generates a distinct spectrum — and therefore different acceptance and efficiency — it is essential to ensure that the agreement demonstrated prior to selection remains consistent a posteriori. This distinction makes validation under realistic conditions the final and decisive step in the evaluation chain, since it is the only environment in which a modeling error can manifest as a bias in the predicted performance of the analysis and, ultimately, in the limits derived from it.

\textcolor{red}{Tendré que incluir bbtt y motivarlo todo un poco mejor cuando sepa lo que incluyo} The scenario chosen for this purpose is the recent analysis by the CMS Collaboration targeting the $gg\to HH$ production in the $4b$ final state \cite{XXX}, which covers both the resolved regime, with four separately reconstructed $b$ jets; and the boosted regime, in which the decay products of each Higgs boson are collected in a single large-radius jet. The publication of the analysis provides event trees with generator-level information for four different values of $\kappa_\lambda = 0, 1, 2.45$, and $5$ in HEPData \cite{XXX}, allowing the reproduction of the effect of the complete analysis chain. In this context, the focus of this validation is on the boosted regime, since it represents the most unfavorable case the method may encounter: the reduced phase space and the more restrictive selection result in limited statistics, leading to significant variations in acceptance and efficiency across the coupling space that are particularly sensitive to extreme coupling values. If the method is capable of maintaining its accuracy under these challenging conditions, it is reasonable to assert its validity in less demanding regions.\\

% procedimiento
The procedure followed to perform the validation is described below. First, it is necessary to construct the templates that will serve as input to the matrix. To accomplish this, the event-level reweighting described in Section \ref{subsec:multireference} is used on the data provided by the analysis to construct the points of the optimized basis shown in Table \ref{tab:bestheft}. This is performed independently for each topology; that is, the result after ELR is a set of basis for each topology. This enables the prediction of both the shape of the distributions and their normalization after the various selections for any configuration of the couplings. This therefore allows us to model the expected signals at the points $\kappa_\lambda$ and compare them with the direct simulation of those signals.

The evaluation will be organized into three stages of increasing rigor. First, a comparison is made of the reconstructed kinematic distributions in both topologies to verify that the method can reliably reproduce the shape of the spectrum once it has been distorted by the selection process. Next, the focus will shift to how the method behaves under normalization, which is assessed through its dependence on the couplings of the product of acceptance and efficiency and the effective cross section following selection. Finally, the method is tested on two-dimensional coupling planes. In this scenario, the ELR serves as the reference, since the number of points to be evaluated is not compatible with full simulation. In all cases, agreement is quantified using the same metrics employed thus far, $d_W(H_{t},H_{m})$ and $f_{stat}(H_{t},H_{m})$, which allows the results to be interpreted in continuity with those at the generator level and compared with the systematic uncertainty specified in Section \ref {subsec:uncertainties}.\\

\subsubsection{Differential distributions after selection}
%
The initial validation stage compares the distributions of events that survive the selection process for each signal region to ensure the robustness of the method.  A comparison is made between three overlapping predictions: the direct simulation of the point, obtained directly from the event trees of the analysis, serves as the true reference; the prediction returned by the multi-reference ELR for that same point, which is the intermediate step from which the base templates are constructed; and the prediction from the matrix inversion method itself, obtained as a linear combination of those templates. The inclusion of the prediction returned by ELR in this context is not unfounded; rather, it responds to the practical need to discern the origin of potential sources of error in the matrix inversion method. A discrepancy shared by both ELR and the matrix method points to a limitation of the reweighting, whereas a discrepancy affecting only the latter can be directly attributed to the linear combination — and thus to the basis — which is the ultimately objective of this validation process.

It should be noted that the four simulated samples, which serve as a reference, also constitute the set of references that feeds the ELR from which the basis templates are derived. In this sense, the test does not constitute a validation against a completely independent source of information, but should be understood solely as the definitive test of the validity of the model subjected to the complete analysis chain.\\

The variable chosen in this case to perform the comparison is the transverse momentum of the Higgs boson pair, $p_T^{HH}$. This choice is intended to demonstrate the independence of the method from the target variable, since $m_{HH}$ has already been thoroughly examined and is also the variable upon which the optimization itself is based. In this regard, it is informative to use an observable other than the one that guided the construction of the method, since the accuracy of its description is not guaranteed by construction but rather depends on whether the factorization discussed in Section \ref{subsec:physical_model} is preserved throughout the entire chain.\\

sl2: Modeling HH signals in BSM scenarios / The need for HH signal modeling

Ways to model / to save the simulation budget
 • Generator based reweighing (common in madGraph used at the moment i.e in qqHH, VHH, ttHH and HHH)
 • Event level reweighting based on generator level variables such as used since many years in ggHH

Morphing: mapping of parameters using of set of values : Emulate a sample (e.g. ggF), with a new value of a parameter (e.g. ����, ��t) by reweighing other samples (interpolation/extrapolation) When we want to set constraints on parameters, being them deviations of the Higgs couplings on the kappa framework or Wilson coefficients in the EFT framework, we need to be able to continuously model the signal for a large variety of parameter combinations → simulating a large number of signal samples for different parameter combinations, but that is computationally very expensive and thus not feasible in practice. And even if we were able to do that, employing for example event-level reweighting techniques to model the signal for a large variety of parameter combinations would still be computationally expensive on the long flows of experimental analyses and thus not feasible in practice. The statistical interpretation of the results, which is typically done by performing a likelihood fit to the data, also becomes challenging in this context one of the methods used by experimentalists to handle this challenge: Matrix inversion method. It elegantly simplifies the problem, allowing to model the signal for a large variety of parameter combinations by simulating only a small number of signal samples and then using the matrix method to interpolate between them

 To model the signal for arbitrary coupling values, we need a theoretical description of how the HH cross section depends on these couplings --> Morphing techniques: Rely on the factorization of the HH cross section


 sl3: Physical model

Physical model behind reweighting and morphing
Basic idea: the HH cross section (or any differential observable) can be factorized into a coupling‑dependent part and a coupling‑independent part.
Through some algebra, the coupling‑independent part can be eliminated, and the cross section at any point in parameter space can be written as a linear combination of a small set of input samples.
