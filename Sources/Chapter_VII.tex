\chapter{Results and combination with the Run 2 dataset}
\label{ch:bbtt_results}
%
With the prediction of the analysis fully determined, the next step is to convert its comparison with the data into a measurement. However, the low cross section of the signal being sought means that this comparison is not straightforward at first glance. The excess that would result from the production of Higgs boson pairs is negligible when compared to the uncertainty associated with the known background. Under these conditions, a comparison between an observed number and a predicted number is not meaningful. Instead, the question is how much signal the data can accommodate once the background is allowed to be adjusted within the limits of its uncertainties.

Accordingly, the statistical model treats each source of systematic uncertainty as an additional degree of freedom in the fit, which can readjust normalizations and reshape distributions within the limits allowed by each uncertainty. However, deviating from the nominal value comes at a cost, so it is done only to the extent that the data require it. Therefore, in the observable fitted, the low-score bins — populated almost exclusively by background — serve as the anchoring points for the fit, while the high-score bins are the only ones that respond appreciably to the presence of a signal. Given that the fifty signal regions share processes and calibrations, the readjustment must be consistent across all of them at the same time, ensuring that the fit is performed simultaneously on the entire set.

Given the relative signal size, it follows that the expected sensitivity of the analysis is not sufficient to observe the production of Higgs boson pairs at the rate predicted by the Standard Model based on the 172\,fb$^{-1}$ of data collected during the analyzed Run 3 periods. Therefore, the result is not a direct measurement of the signal, but rather an estimate of how much signal might have gone undetected, along with the resulting constraint on the couplings that govern the process under study. The extent to which this margin has narrowed compared to previous analyses, as well as the degree to which the enhanced conditions of Run 3 — new triggers and improved identification algorithms such as those described in Chapter \ref{ch:taggers} — have contributed to more than simply accumulate data, also provides an additional interpretation of the results.

For this reason, the analysis does not stop at the data from Run 3, but rather seeks to accumulate as much statistical information as possible in this channel. It therefore includes the combination of this measurement with the corresponding one from Run 2, which requires determining which uncertainties are common to both analyses and which are not. It is also important to place the result in the context of searches for Higgs pair production in other final states, since the $bb\tau\tau$ search is just one of several that target the same process, each with its own balance between the decay fraction and the background to be suppressed. Likewise, a comparison with results from the ATLAS experiment provides a broader picture of where this measurement stands and what actually limits it.

The present chapter is structured as follows. The construction of the statistical model that meets the aforementioned requirements is the subject of Section \ref{sec:bbtt_statistics}, which builds on the general framework introduced in Section \ref{sec:stat_framework} and is limited to the decisions specific to this study. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_results} first presents the distributions of the discriminant after adjustment and then the results derived from it, examining the factors that limit the measure. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_combination} combines the obtained result with the published measure on the Run 2 dataset without reprocessing. Finally, Section \ref{sec:bbtt_comparison} compares the result with the other searches in the same process, \textcolor{red}{and Section \ref{sec:bbtt_summary} concludes the chapter.}
