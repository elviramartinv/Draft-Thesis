\chapter{Results and combination with the Run 2 dataset}
\label{ch:bbtt_results}
%
With the expected signal and backgrounds distributions fully specified, the next step is to convert its comparison with the data into a measurement. However, the low cross section of the signal being sought means that this comparison cannot be made directly. The excess that would result from the production of Higgs boson pairs is small when compared to the uncertainty on the backgrounds that accompany it. Under these conditions, a comparison between an observed number and a predicted number is not meaningful. Instead, the question is how much signal the data can accommodate once the background is allowed to be adjusted within the limits of its uncertainties.

Accordingly, the statistical model treats each source of systematic uncertainty as an additional degree of freedom in the fit, which can readjust normalizations and reshape distributions within the limits allowed by each uncertainty. However, deviating from the nominal value comes at a cost, so it is done only to the extent that the data require it. Therefore, in the observable fitted, the low-score bins — populated almost exclusively by background — serve as the anchoring points for the fit, while the high-score bins are the only ones that respond appreciably to the presence of a signal. Given that the fifty signal regions share processes and calibrations, the readjustment must be consistent across all of them, so the fit is performed simultaneously on the entire set.

Given the relative signal size and the analyzed 172\,fb$^{-1}$ of data collected during the considered Run 3 periods, it follows that the expected sensitivity of the analysis is not sufficient to observe the production of Higgs boson pairs at the rate predicted by the standard model (SM). Therefore, the result is not a direct measurement of the signal, but rather an upper limit on how much signal might have gone undetected, along with the resulting constraint on the couplings that govern the process under study. These results also show how much this margin has narrowed with respect to previous analyses, and to what extent the Run 3 conditions — new triggers and improved identification algorithms such as those of Chapter \ref{ch:taggers} — have done more than merely accumulate data.

For this reason the analysis does not stop at the Run-3 data, but exploits all the data available in this final state. This measurement is combined with the published Run-2 measurement in the same final state, which requires determining which uncertainties are common to both analyses and which are not. It is also worth placing the result in the context of searches for Higgs pair production in other final states, since the $bb\tau\tau$ search is just one of several that target the same process, each with its own balance between the branching fraction and the background to be suppressed. Likewise, a comparison with results from the ATLAS experiment provides a broader picture of where this measurement stands and what actually limits it.\\

The present chapter is structured as follows. The construction of the statistical model that meets the aforementioned requirements is the subject of Section \ref{sec:bbtt_statistics}, which builds on the general framework introduced in Section \ref{sec:stat_framework} and is limited to the decisions specific to this study. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_results} first presents the post-fie distributions of the discriminant and then the results derived from it, examining the factors that limit the measurement. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_combination} combines the obtained result with the published measure on the Run 2 dataset at the level of the statistical model and without reprocessing those data. Finally, Section \ref{sec:bbtt_comparison} compares the result with the other searches for the same process, \textcolor{red}{and Section \ref{sec:bbtt_summary} concludes the chapter.}

\section{The statistical model of the search}
\label{sec:bbtt_statistics}
%
The comparison between the prediction and the data is performed using a binned maximum likelihood fit, carried out simultaneously across the fifty signal regions of the analysis. In each region the observable is the output of the classifier assigned to it, using the adaptive binning described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction}, so that the total likelihood is the product of the contributions from all bins in all regions. The formalism upon which this is built was introduced in Section \ref{sec:stat_framework}, so the following is limited to the decisions specific to this search. The implementation is based on \textsc{Combine} \cite{CMS-combine}, the statistical analysis and combination tool used at CMS.

The fit is determined by three decisions. The first is the measurement itself, i.e., which parameters are left free and how the dependence of the signal on them is modeled, which is the subject of Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_likelihood}. The second concerns how the systematic uncertainties discussed in the previous chapter enter the fit. This includes the scheme by which they are correlated across periods, categories, and channels, as well as how the templates are handled before reaching the fit, and is covered in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_nuisances}. Finally, Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_teststat} addresses the third aspect, which consists of describing how each result is constructed from the fit.

\subsection{The fit and its parameters of interest}
\label{subsec:bbtt_likelihood}
% 
% de qué está hecha la predicción en cada bin y de dónde viene cada parte
In each bin of each signal region, the expected number of events is the sum of the contributions from all the processes that populate that region. As previously noted, the simulation provides the signal in its two production modes and the backgrounds from $t\bar{t}$, Drell--Yan, single top quark and $tW$, $W$+jets, diboson and triboson production, and single Higgs boson production, which in this search constitutes an irreducible background. The contribution from jets misidentified as $\tau_h$ is extracted from the data using the method described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_ff}. This distinction has consequences that must be considered in the fit, since the object calibrations enter as nuisance parameters of their own for the simulated processes, whereas their effect on the estimate obtained from the data was quantified during its derivation and was absorbed into the associated envelopes.

% qué está fijo y qué es libre
Each contribution enters the fit with the normalization and shape assigned to it by the prediction built in the previous chapter, and can only deviate from them through the nuisance parameters associated with their uncertainties. None is left free, so the only parameter that the fit determines without any constraint is the parameter of interest, which is defined based on the result to be interpreted. In this regard, two alternatives are considered: one for measuring the strength with which the signal appears in the data — which constitutes the natural parameter when the goal is to measure or set a limit on the production rate — and another for constraining the parameters that govern the process — where the coupling modifiers themselves constitute the parameter of interest.\\


%ya que el ajuste no dispone de ningún grado de libertad con el que abosrber un fondo mal modelado más alla de lo que su propia incertidumrbe consiente. 

The signal strength, $\mu$, is defined as the factor by which the cross section predicted by the SM must be multiplied to reproduce the observed cross section. In the fit, $\mu$ multiplies the expected number of signal events in each bin of each signal region, thereby modifying the height of the signal distribution but not its shape. Its value is continuous and is determined by the fit, with $\mu = 1$ and $\mu = 0$ merely marking the SM hypothesis and the absence of signal, respectively.


This parameter is used to derive two measurements, one that is inclusive of both production modes and another that is exclusive to vector boson fusion. This split reflects the marked asymmetry between the two modes, as the cross section for gluon fusion is nearly twenty times larger than that for VBF. Consequently, the inclusive measurement is barely sensitive to the VBF contribution, which is tied to the dominant mode and, therefore, it must be measured separately so that the data can constrain it.

The inclusive measurement uses a single $\mu$ for both production modes. By scaling both templates by the same factor, the ratio between them is fixed at the value predicted by the SM, so that the only thing the fit determines is the overall scale of their sum. The exclusive measurement replaces that parameter with another that multiplies only the templates for vector boson fusion. It is the dedicated VBF category of Section \ref{sec:bbtt_flow} that provides the necessary sensitivity by isolating a region where this production mode accounts for a much larger fraction of the total than it does in the rest of the analysis.\\

In the second alternative, a coupling modifier replaces $\mu$ as the parameter of interest and is fitted directly to the data, while the other couplings are held at their SM values; this yields confidence intervals for $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_{2V}$\footnote{The same procedure applies to $\kappa_t$ and $\kappa_V$, for which this search does not improve upon the constraints provided by single-Higgs-boson production measurements. Therefore, they are not reported here.}. This treatment is possible because the parametrization described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples} allows the signal prediction at any point in coupling space to be expressed as a linear combination of the simulated samples, and it does so bin by bin rather than just for the total cross section, so that each signal template is a known analytic function of the couplings. Unlike $\mu$, a coupling does not merely scale the height of the signal distribution. A change in $\kappa_\lambda$ shifts the invariant mass spectrum of the $HH$ system and thereby modifies the distribution of the observable that is fitted.

The same parametrization can also be used the other way around. The couplings are held fixed at a point in coupling space, and the parameter of interest is again $\mu$, which provides the limit on the cross section as a function of the coupling. By scanning over a grid in two of them, the exclusion contours in the plane they define can be determined.\\

Once the parameter of interest has been defined in each case, the model is fully determined except for its remaining parameters, which carry the uncertainties and on which the burden of covering any residual difference between the prediction and the data falls entirely. Their definition, the scheme by which they are correlated and the treatment their templates receive before entering the fit are addressed in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_nuisances}.

\subsection{The nuisance parameters of the fit}
\label{subsec:bbtt_nuisances}
%
The sources of uncertainty are incorporated into the fit as nuisance parameters, each of which carries a constraint term that penalizes its deviation from the nominal value and acts on the prediction either by modifying only the normalization of the affected process or by additionally changing its shape, according to the mechanism described in Section \ref{sec:stat_framework}. The treatment of each of the sources detailed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics} depends on its origin, which determines the three decisions that remain to be made, that is, which of them share parameters across regions; what modifications the shifted templates undergo before entering the fit; and how the limited statistical precision of the prediction itself is incorporated.\\


% correlación
Each source of uncertainty affects the prediction in more than one signal region, and it is therefore necessary to determine whether its effect on one region is tied to its effect in the others, since the model may either use a single parameter that shifts them all at once or assign independent ones. Accordingly, a source is correlated when it has a common origin across all affected regions — as is the case with theoretical predictions or calibrations derived jointly for the entire dataset — and is uncorrelated when it is measured independently in each period, or when it stems from a finite number of events — in which case fluctuations in one region bear no relation to those in another. The fifty signal regions differ both in category and in data-taking period, and what distinguishes them as far as these uncertainties are concerned are the conditions under which the data were recorded and the calibrations derived for them. The division is therefore made between periods, with a single parameter shared by the ten categories of a given period and independent parameters from one period to the next.

Once the correlation scheme has been fixed, the two shifted templates for each source — constructed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics} by shifting the corresponding correction in both directions — undergo some corrections before the fit to ensure that all variations behave consistently under interpolation.

When the two variations of a source fall on the same side of the nominal template in a bin, the sign of the nuisance parameter becomes meaningless, since the content moves in the same direction regardless of the sign of the shift; thus, they are replaced by a symmetric pair constructed from the larger of the two differences. On the other hand, the same requirement for adaptive binning as in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction} applies here to the estimate of \textit{fakes} obtained from the data. If a bin is empty, it is assigned a minimum value, since a process with zero yield does not allow for relative variations and leaves its uncertainties without a possible treatment. The pile-up reweighting receives a separate treatment, in which its two variations are replaced by the nominal template rescaled by the corresponding ratio of integrals, corrected so that they lie on opposite sides of the nominal value and neither deviates from it by less than $2\,\%$. This substitution eliminates shape information, so it applies only to background processes, i.e., the signal is excluded.\textcolor{red}{por qué se hace esto?...}\\

Finally, the statistical limitation of the prediction itself is addressed with the Barlow and Beeston treatment described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}. Its implementation distinguishes between two regimes based on the effective number of events in the bin, defined as $(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$ to account for the fact that the simulated events are assigned different weights. When this number exceeds ten events, the total background fluctuation is described by a single parameter per bin, shared by all the processes that populate it. When it does not reach that number, the Gaussian approximation no longer applies, and an independent parameter is assigned to each process, with a Poisson constraint. \subsection{Extraction of the results}
\label{subsec:bbtt_teststat}
%
With the model complete, the results are extracted from the profile likelihood ratio introduced in Section \ref{sec:stat_framework}, evaluated in the asymptotic approximation \cite{Cowan:2010js}. From this statistic the three results already announced are constructed, each of them twice. First in its expected version, obtained in the same signal regions but replacing the observed data by an artificial dataset that reproduces the background so that no observed data enter and the result measures only the sensitivity of the analysis. And then in its observed version, once the signal region has been unblinded. The expected version was the only figure of merit available while the analysis was being built, so the decisions of the previous chapter were taken against it, whereas the observed one constitutes the result of the measurement.

%A partir del estadístico se construyen tres clases de resultados, ya nombrados anteriormente: la intensidad con la que la señal aparece en los datos junto con la significancia que puede atribuírsele, el límite superior sobre la sección eficaz de producción cuando esa significancia no basta para declarar una observación, y los intervalos de confianza sobre los acoplamientos. 

% signal strength
The signal strength is the value that maximizes the likelihood, and its uncertainty is obtained from the likelihood profile on either side of that maximum, allowing all nuisance parameters to be readjusted at each value of the signal strength. The significance quantifies the compatibility of the data with the background-only hypothesis, by comparing the likelihood at the maximum with that obtained when the signal strength is set to zero.

% upper limits
The upper limit is determined at the $95\,\%$ confidence level using the modified frequentist criterion described in Section \ref{sec:stat_framework}, and is computed for both the inclusive and VBF-exclusive measurements. By repeating the same procedure for a sequence of coupling values, with the coupling fixed at each value, the limit as a function of that caoupling is also obtained.

% couplings
Confidence intervals for the couplings are derived from the value that this same statistic takes when the coupling is set at each point in a scan. By construction, it is zero at the value that best fits the data and increases as the value moves away from it, so that the interval is bounded by the two values at which this increase reaches the threshold corresponding to the desired confidence level. The same criterion, applied to a two-dimensional scan, delimits the excluded regions in the plane spanned by two couplings.

All of these results are calculated not only for the entire dataset, but also separately for each data-taking period, for each channel, and for each category group, which allows the breakdown shown in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_results} and reveals the source of the sensitivity.\textcolor{red}{algo de la bondad del ajuste??? no, no?}\\\\

The model constructed in this way is fitted to the data in a single operation, which simultaneously yields the signal strength value and the values of all the nuisance parameters. The fit therefore returns not only a measurement but also a revised prediction — the result of applying the shifts assigned to each process — and comparing that prediction with the data reveals whether the fit required deviating from the nominal parameter values. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_results} begins with this comparison, before extracting from that same fit the results sought by the analysis.


\section{Results from the Run 3 dataset}
\label{sec:bbtt_results}
%
The number of signal events produced in the analyzed dataset is the product of the integrated luminosity, the production cross section, and the branching fraction to the final state under consideration, $N = \mathcal{L}\,\sigma_{HH}\,\mathcal{B}(HH\to b\bar{b}\tau\tau)$. Using the values given in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples} for the cross sections of the two production modes and a branching fraction of $7.3\,\%$, the $172$ fb$^{-1}$ collected during Run 3 contain about 450 signal events. Of these, the acceptance of the selection, together with the trigger, reconstruction, and identification efficiencies, leave only a small fraction, which is spread across the fifty signal regions and accompanied by a background several orders of magnitude larger. \textcolor{red}{cutflow} This is the magnitude of the excess whose extraction is the fit described in the previous section, and which constitutes the subject of the results presented below.

\subsection{Post-fit distributions}
%
The post-fit distributions are obtained by applying to each process the values that the fit has assigned to the nuisance parameters, so that they represent the prediction as the model leaves it once it has been confronted with the data. Figure \ref{fig:postfit_2024} shows them for the 2024 data-taking period, which has the highest integrated luminosity and is presented as a representative example, since the other perdios exhibit similar behavior. The ten signal regions are plotted along a single axis, grouped by category and, within each category, by channel, and in all of them the bins are ordered according to the discriminant score. The two signal contributions are superimposed, scaled to the signal strength that best fits the data.

The composition of the background is the one anticipated in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_controlplots}. The contribution from jets misidentified as $\tau_h$ dominates in all regions, followed by the production of top quark pairs and Drell--Yan, while the single Higgs boson production and all other processes constitute minor contributions. Within each region, the yield decreases sharply as one moves toward higher scores. This is the behavior imposed by the adaptive binning described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction}, which distributes the signal uniformly, so that the last bins in each group are both the least populated and the most sensitive. The signal, even when scaled to the fitted strength, remains several orders of magnitude below the background in all cases.\\

In contrast to the comparison of Section \ref{sec:bbtt_controlplots}, which was made before the fit and in which a discrepancy would have indicated a problem with the modeling, here the agreement is largely guaranteed by construction, since the fit used the nuisance parameters to achieve it. However, this capacity for absorption is not unlimited, since the parameters can only shift within the limits allowed by their constraints. Therefore, a discrepancy greater than that margin would still be visible, which justifies examining the post-fit distributions before interpreting any results. The simultaneous nature of the fit also requires checking each region separately, since a discrepancy localized in one region may be offset by the rest and not manifest itself in any of the final numbers.

The figure confirms that the ratio of the observed data to the prediction is consistent with unity along the entire axis, with the largest fluctuations concentrated in the least populated bins, and that none of the ten regions deviates from the expected behavior. The uncertainty band is also significantly narrower than that of the pre-fit distributions, because the bins populated almost exclusively by background contain enough events for the fit to determine several of the nuisance parameters with greater precision than the uncertainty initially assigned to them. This behavior — and in particularly whether the fit has required appreciable shifts in the parameters from their nominal values — is examined in detail in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_impacts}.

\subsection{Signal strength and significance}
\label{subsec:bbtt_mu}
%
The simultaneous fit to the fifty signal regions yields a best estimate of the signal strength of $\mu = 2.6^{\,+2.1}_{\,-1.7}$, where the uncertainty defines the $68\,\%$ confidence interval. The central value lies above the standard model prediction, $\mu = 1$, by less than one standard deviation, so the result is consistent with it. The result is also consistent with the absence of signal, $\mu = 0$, from which it lies about one and a half times the lower uncertainty away. Figure \ref{fig:likelihood_scan_r} shows the profile of the likelihood ratio as a function of the signal strength, from which both the central value and the interval are obtained.

The significance quantifies the degree to which the data are consistent with the background-only hypothesis, and is obtained by comparing the likelihood at its maximum with the one obtained when $\mu$ is set to zero, as described in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_teststat}. Its observed value is $1.5$ standard deviations, while the expected value — calculated assuming the signal to be present with the strength predicted by the SM — is $0.7$ standard deviations and indicates what the analysis could have hoped to detect. Therefore, no significant excess above the background-only hypothesis is observed, nor is there any evidence of Higgs boson pair production under the SM hypothesis.\\

It is obtained for the two measurements defined in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_likelihood} — the inclusive one, covering both production modes, and the one exclusive to vector boson fusion — and is computed both for the full combination and for each category separately. For inclusive production, the combination of the ten categories excludes cross sections greater than $6.6$ times that predicted by the standard model, compared to an expected value of $3.9$, so that the observed limig is less restrictive than expected one, consistent with the excess described in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_mu}. \section{Combination with the Run 2 dataset}
\label{sec:bbtt_combination}
%
The search for nonresonant production of Higgs boson pairs in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state using the Run 2 dataset \cite{CMS-HIG-20-010} analyzed the $138$ fb$^{-1}$ of data collected between 2016 and 2018 at a center-of-mass energy of $13$ TeV. It therefore constitutes a set of events disjoint from those collected during Run 3, recorded during different periods and at different energies, which makes them statistically independent and allows for the combination of both datasets.

The combination presented here is performed at the \textit{datacard} level, which means that each analysis contributes its own binned distributions, with its own binning, templates, and nuisance parameters, and the joint likelihood results from the product of the contributions of all the regions of both. Under this approach the data are not reprocessed. Consequently, the events from Run 2 do not benefit from the triggers, identification algorithms, or discriminant developed for Run 3, and its contribution remains fixed at the sensitivity that analysis originally achieved.

However, as already noted, the strategy followed in Run 3 largely derives from the Run 2 search, so that both analyses share a similar structure. The combination, although it requires no reprocessing, is therefore not immediate, as two analyses that share methods and calibrations also share some of their sources of uncertainty.

Therefore, the combination must determine which of the nuisance parameters of the two analyses describe the same source of uncertainty and must consequently be identified with one another. Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comb_procedure} addresses this decision and how the joint likelihood is constructed, and Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comb_results} presents the results obtained from it, compared in each case with those provided from each dataset separately.


\subsection{The combined statistical model}
\label{subsec:bbtt_comb_procedure}
%
The combined likelihood is the union of all the binned distributions from the two analyses. In Run 3 these are the fifty outputs of the discriminant described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction}, one per signal region. In Run 2, these are the outputs of its own classifier, evaluated across the eight orthogonal categories defined by that search and broken down by channel and by year. The result is a single fit over all of them in which each region retains its own histogram and what is shared is the set of parameters. It is performed in one step, using the same parameters of interest defined in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_likelihood}, which scale the signal from both sets relative to the SM prediction corresponding to the energy of each, $13$ TeV in Run 2 and $13.6$ TeV in Run 3.\\


To ensure consistency in the combination, it is necessary to establish how the nuisance parameters of one analysis relate to those of the other. The criterion is the same as the one that governed the correlations within Run 3, i.e., the origin of the uncertainty. The theoretical uncertainties described in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_theory_unc} stem from the same calculations in both searches, so each of them is described by a single parameter common to the two. This group includes those associated with the Higgs boson branching fractions, both in their effect on the normalization and on the shape; the choice of the set of parton distributions; the value of $\alpha_s$; the renormalization and factorization scales; and, notably, the one that combines the latter with the ambiguity in the top quark mass scheme for the signal produced by gluon fusion, which is the largest of the theoretical uncertainties affecting the signal. In contrast, experimental uncertainties are handled as independent, as is appropriate for datasets with differing detector conditions and calibration procedures.\\

A second consideration is that the two datasets were recorded at different energies, so that the same value for the couplings does not yield the same prediction in one dataset as in the other, since both the signal and the background cross sections differ between $13$ and $13.6$ TeV. The signal parametrization described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples} is thus constructed separately for each dataset, based on its own simulated samples and the corresponding cross sections, but using the same matrix inversion method and following the recommendations of the LHC Higgs Working Group. In this way, each point in coupling space is assigned, in each dataset, the prediction corresponding to its own energy.

Furthermore, this dependence is not limited to the signal but also includes part of the background. The Higgs boson branching fractions and the single Higgs production rates also depend on the coupling modifiers — though with different functional dependencies — and their variation is likewise included in the model. If this were not done, a scan in $\kappa_\lambda$ would shift the signal while leaving unchanged a background that in reality moves with it.\\

One final clarification is in order regarding the Run 2 result shown in this combination. Since the publication of that search, some of its theoretical uncertainties have been updated, including through the introduction of an additional uncertainty on the heavy-flavor associated single Higgs production. The \textit{datacards} used here therefore incorporate these updates, so the result presented separately for Run 2 may differ slightly from that published in Ref.~\cite{CMS-HIG-20-010}.

With the likelihood function constructed in this way, the fit and the extraction of the results follow the procedure described in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_teststat}, with the only difference being the set of regions over which they are performed.

\section{Comparison with other HH searches}
\label{sec:bbtt_comparison}
%
To ensure a fair comparison of different searches, it is necessary to specify the terms on which they are compared. While the quantities each of them reports are presented in this chapter — although not all searches cite all three — the measurement common to all of them is the upper limit on the production rate expressed in units of the SM prediction, which in all cases is reported as both the observed value and the expected value, along with the confidence intervals on the coupling modifiers. Of the two values, the expected value quantifies the sensitivity achieved by the analysis and is therefore the one on which the comparison rests, while the observed value additionally reflects the particular event yields recorded in each dataset. 

The sensitivity achieved in each case depends on a number of factors, including the integrated luminosity being analyzed, what the phase space of the final state allows from the outset, as described in Section \ref{subsec:HH_BR}, and the strategy with which that room has been exploited. Therefore, it is essential to consider these factors when evaluating the results of the comparison.

Given that the integrated luminosity on which the sensitivity depends may differ from one analysis to another, each value is quoted together with the luminosity it corresponds to, and, where the comparison calls for it, rescaled to a common luminosity. The composition of the dataset may also vary between searches, since not all of them cover the same data-taking periods or the same center-of-mass energy, so that a difference may simply reflect that one analysis includes data that the other has not yet incorporated.

Looking at other final states shows how this sensitivity is distributed across channels and what each one contributes to the whole. Within CMS, the $4b$ and $bb\gamma\gamma$ final states have been analyzed using Run 3 data, which are contemporaneous with the result obtained here, and are therefore the ones against which this result is placed. By contrast, keeping the final state fixed and changing the experiment brings the strategy into view, and this is addressed by the search published by the ATLAS experiment for the $bb\tau\tau$ decay channel.

On this basis the two comparisons that follow are built taking from each publication the observed and expected limits, as well as the confidence intervals on the couplings modifiers and the luminosity to which they correspond.

\subsection{Comparison with the ATLAS search in \texorpdfstring{$bb\tau\tau$}{bbtautau}}
\label{subsec:bbtt_comp_atlas}
%
Agotado lo que el programa de CMS permite comparar, la referencia exterior la proporciona la búsqueda que el experimento ATLAS ha publicado recientemente en este mismo estado final \cite{ATLAS-HH-bbtautau-Run3}. Al mantenerse fijo el canal se situa la estrategia seguida aquí frente a hasta dónde puede llegarse con un conjunto de datos de tamaño parecido, aunque no exactamente igual, lo cual es clave para contextualizar la comparación. ATLAS analiza los $140$ fb$^{-1}$ de Run 2 y $56$ fb$^{-1}$ de Run 3, puesto que no se incluye el periodo 2024\footnote{pequeñas diferencias en luminosidad se deben a lo que graba cada detector por separado de la luminosidad total entregada por LHC?}. Además, en la comparación cabe notar que ATLAS reanaliza por completo los datos de Run 2 con su última estrategia desarrollada, mientras que aquí intervienen con la que tenían en su publicación original, según se describió en la Sección \ref{subsec:bbtt_comb_procedure}.\\

% estrategias
Ambas búsquedas, ATLAS y CMS, comparten estrategias de análisis: se distingue entre los canales según el modo de desintegración del par de leptones $\tau$, se reconstruyen los dos candidatos a bosón de Higgs a partir de jets etiquetados como provenientes de quarks $b$ y de los productos visibles de los leptones $\tau$, y se extrae la señal a partir de un ajuste simultáneo a la distribución de la salida de un clasificador. 

Sin embargo, son varias las diferencias que separan ambos análisis, tanto en la forma de llegar a las regiones de señal, que ATLAS obtiene en un mismo paso que la extracción de la señal a partir de una única red basada en la arquitectura de transformador introducida en la Sección \ref{subsec:PNet}, como en la granularidad del ajuste resultante, que comprende doce regiones frente a las cincuenta empleadas aquí. Por otro lado, el criterio con que se construyen las regiones se apoya en la separación del espectro de masa invariante del sistema $HH$ en un tramo bajo y otro alto,  y en el tramo bajo el observable que se ajusta está construido sobre una configuración anómala con $\kappa_\lambda = 5$, cuya cinemática más blanda es la que puebla esa región. El reparto del espacio de fases y el discriminante quedan así organizados en torno a la variable de la que depende la sensibilidad a los acoplamientos, mientras que aquí las categorías se definen por la topología y la calidad del etiquetado, y es la red la que debe explotar internamente esa dependencia.

Estas no son, en cualquier caso, las únicas diferencias. ATLAS incorpora al ajuste cuatro regiones de control dedicadas a sus fondos dominantes, reconstruye la masa del sistema $\tau\tau$ mediante un método de verosimilitud en lugar de una red de regresión, aplica a los jets etiquetados correcciones dedicadas que mejoran su resolución en energía, determina las intensidades de señal de los dos modos de producción en un único ajuste con dos parámetros de interés, y valida la estrategia completa midiendo en este mismo estado final las producciones de $ZZ$ y de $ZH$. Cuál de todas ellas pesa en la diferencia de sensibilidad es lo que el desglose de incertidumbres que se presentado más adelante permite acotar.\\

% resultados de ATLAS
Evaluando los resultados que se obtienen con cada búsqueda la intensidad de señal que mejor describe los datos de ATLAS es $\mu = 2.6^{+1.4}_{-1.0}$, con una significancia observada de $2.6$ desviaciones estándar frente a $1.2$ esperadas. El valor central coincide con el obtenido con los datos del Run 3 en este trabajo, $\mu = 2.6^{+2.1}_{-1.7}$ de la Sección \ref{subsec:bbtt_mu}, mientras que al tomar en cuenta la combinación en la Sección \ref{subsec:bbtt_comb_results} el valor pasa a ser $\mu = 0.9^{+1.6}_{-1.4}$, con $0.7$ observadas frente a $0.8$ esperadas. Los dos resultados son compatibles entre sí y con la predicción del SM dentro de sus incertidumbres. Por otro lado, el límite superior observado por ATLAS es $5.3$ veces la predicción del SM, con un valor esperado de $2.4$. bajo la hipótesis de ausencia de señal y de $2.8$ bajo la de una señal como la del modelo estándar. Esta segunda referencia es la pertinente cuando se observa un exceso, porque es la que indica qué límite cabría esperar si la señal estuviera presente, y es también la que acompaña a los resultados de la Sección \ref{subsec:bbtt_limits} en la Figura \ref{fig:limits_combined}. La Figura \ref{fig:atlas_results_summary} recoge ambos conjuntos de valores desglosados por canal y por periodo.

Las restricciones sobre los acoplamientos se comparan con intervalos esperados similares de $[-1.7,\ 8.5]$ frente a $[-1.5,\ 8.4]$ para el acoplamiento trilineal y de $[0.0,\ 2.1]$ frente a $[-0.1,\ 2.2]$ para el acoplamiento entre dos bosones de Higgs y dos bosones vectoriales, por la razón ya expuesta de que su anchura la fija la forma de la predicción y no la sensibilidad sobre la tasa de producción. La diferencia está en el intervalo observado de ATLAS para el acoplamiento trilineal, que se parte en dos tramos disjuntos, $[-3.4,\ 1.6]$ y $[5.5,\ 10.1]$ porque los datos permiten excluir la zona próxima al mínimo de la sección eficaz predicha, separando así las dos regiones permitidas.

REVISAR
Las dos comparaciones recorridas en esta sección convergen en un único objeto, la combinación que ATLAS y CMS han realizado de todas sus búsquedas con los datos completos de Run 2 \cite{ATLASCMS:2026HH}, que reúne a la vez el eje de los estados finales y el de los experimentos. Esa combinación excluye tasas de producción superiores a $2.5$ veces la predicción del modelo estándar, con un valor esperado de $1.7$, y restringe el acoplamiento trilineal al intervalo $[-0.7,\ 6.1]$ y el acoplamiento entre dos bosones de Higgs y dos bosones vectoriales a $[0.7,\ 1.3]$. Ese valor esperado merece atención, porque significa que ni la suma de los dos experimentos y de todos sus canales sobre la totalidad de Run 2 esperaba alcanzar la tasa que el modelo estándar predice. La observación de este proceso no está por tanto a la distancia de un análisis sino de un programa, y el resultado presentado en este capítulo es una de sus piezas, la más sensible que el experimento ha obtenido en este estado final y una de las tres que sostienen su contribución al conjunto. Lo que la comparación añade a esa posición es un mapa de por dónde continuar, que señala el reprocesado de los datos de Run 2 con una estrategia revisada, la reducción de las incertidumbres experimentales que aquí pesan más que en el experimento vecino, y la recuperación de aceptancia de señal. De esas direcciones, y de lo que cabe esperar del conjunto completo de Run 3 y de los periodos que seguirán, se ocupa la sección final del capítulo.
