\chapter{Theoretical context}
\label{ch:theory}
\noindent The Standard Model (SM) of particle physics~\cite{Herrero:1998eq,Thomson:2013zua} is a renormalizable quantum field theory that describes the elementary particles and their fundamental interactions. Formulated in the early 1970s as a consolidation of theories and experimental discoveries from the previous decade, SM predictions have been well corroborated by numerous observations in collider experiments. The discovery of the Higgs boson is the final entry on this extensive checklist, theorized as a direct consequence of the Brout-Englert-Higgs (BEH) mechanism, which is responsible for the spontaneous electroweak symmetry breaking (EWSB).

Nevertheless, despite the excellent agreement between its predictions and the experimental measurements, a large body of evidence ranging from subnuclear to astrophysical phenomena suggests that its current formulation is incomplete. This makes a more comprehensive theory necessary to provide a complete picture. Exploring the scalar sector is a promising way to research physics Beyond the SM (BSM), where the connection between the Higgs boson self-interaction and the nature of the Higgs potential plays a fundamental role. Therefore, measuring the Higgs boson self-coupling constant, which is currently unconstrained by observation, could offer invaluable insights.

The present chapter establishes the theoretical basis of this work and motivates the study of Higgs boson pair production in this context. First, Section \ref{sec:SM} provides a concise overview of the SM, from its particle content and fundamental interactions to the BEH mechanism, the phenomenology of the Higgs boson, and the limitations that point to physics beyond it. Next, Section \ref{sec:HH_production} focuses on Higgs boson pair production, first within the SM and then in the presence of new physics, introducing the coupling modifiers and effective field theories in which its interpretation is framed. Section \ref{sec:HH_searches} then reviews the decay channels accessible at the LHC and the current status of the experimental searches, which sets the context for the contributions of this thesis. Finally, Section \ref{sec:pp_pheno} describes the phenomenology of proton-proton collisions on which the prediction of the signal and the backgrounds relies.


\section{Structure of the Standard Model of particle physics}
\label{sec:SM}
%
The SM was initially proposed by S. Glashow, A. Salam, and S. Weinberg~\cite{Glashow:1961tr,Salam:1968rm,Weinberg:1967tq} and has been demonstrated to be the most compelling theoretical framework for synthesizing fundamental constituents and their interactions. It is a renormalizable quantum field theory (QFT) based on quantum mechanics and special relativity that satisfies gauge invariance. According to this framework, elementary particles are defined as indivisible entities devoid of internal structure, and correspond to excited states of underlying quantum field, which are mathematically described by QFT.

The SM also encompasses three of the four fundamental interactions. These are the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions, each connected to different intrinsic charges of the matter particles: the electromagnetic charge, the weak isospin, and the color charge. These interactions are described by the exchange of particle mediators, which arise as a consequence of local gauge invariance. The description of the gravitational force is not included in the SM, which is a limitation of the model. However, it can be disregarded at the particle level scale since it is a force with such a weak intensity that its effect has practically no influence compared to the other forces.

The mathematical formulation of the SM is based on a Lagrangian density, $\mathcal{L}$, which condenses all the necessary information to describe the propagation and interactions of the particle fields. The Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ is defined as a scalar function of the fields $\phi_i$, their derivatives $\partial_\mu \phi_i$, and the spacetime coordinates, $x^\mu$, and its invariance under a specific set of symmetry transformations constitutes the fundamental principle on which the SM is built. According to the Noether theorem~\cite{Noether:1918zz}, each continuous global symmetry of a physical system leads to a conserved quantity. These quantities are the intrinsic charges of the matter particles associated with each interaction, and they manifest as a consequence of the symmetry of the SM Lagrangian under the continuous group $SU(3)_C \times SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$. Furthermore, as a direct consequence of local gauge symmetry, the introduction of vector fields is imperative, and their quantum excitations result in the particles that mediate the interactions: the strong interaction, invariant under $SU(3)_C$ and described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD); and the electroweak (EWK) force, unifying the $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ symmetries.


\subsection{Particle content}
%
According to the SM, the elementary particles can be classified depending on their spin. Two types of particles are distinguished: fermions with spin 1/2 and bosons with integer spin — 0 or 1. The former arise from Lorentz spinor fields and constitute all known matter. In regard to the latter, a distinction is made between gauge bosons, corresponding to spin 1, which are associated with the vector fields and responsible for mediating interactions between fermions; and the Higgs boson, with spin 0, linked to a scalar field and responsible for the mechanism by which fermions and bosons acquire their mass. The particle content and their properties are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:SM_particles}, and discussed in detail next.

\subsubsection{Matter particles: Fermions}
%
Due to the relativistic nature of the SM, quantum excitations of spinorial fields that transform in the spin-$1/2$ representation of the Lorentz group give rise to half-integer spin particles called fermions. Experimental observations have demonstrated the existence of twelve fermions: six quark fields and six lepton fields, which will be detailed below. The 12 fermions are arranged into three generations of mass, with each generation consisting of two quarks with electric charges of $Q=+2/3$ and $Q=-1/3$ and two leptons with charges of $Q=-1$ and $Q=0$. The difference in mass between the families is related to their coupling strength with scalar fields. The first family, being the one with the lowest mass, constitutes stable matter, while the particles of the other families are predominantly unstable and decay rapidly. Each fermion has a corresponding antiparticle with the same spacetime characteristics — mass and spin — but opposite intrinsic quantum numbers — charges —, meaning when a particle and its antiparticle interact, they annihilate each other.


\paragraph{Quarks}
%
Each generation contains a quark doublet composed of an up-type quark, with $Q=+2/3$ — up ($u$), charm ($c$) and top ($t$) —, and a down-type quark, with $Q=-1/3$ — down ($d$), strange ($s$) and bottom ($b$). The first-generation quarks are the lightest, with masses of $m_u = 2.16 \pm 0.07$ MeV and $m_d = 4.70 \pm 0.07$ MeV. Their counterparts in the second generation reach masses of $m_s = 92.9 \pm 0.7$ MeV and $m_c = 1.2729 \pm 0.0045$ GeV, while the third generation comprises the bottom quark, with $m_b = 4.186 \pm 0.006$ GeV, and the top quark, the heaviest known elementary particle, with $m_t = 172.60 \pm 0.27$ GeV~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026mpi}. 

Of all the particle content of the SM, only quarks are subject to all three forces. They are the only fermions that carry color charge — connected to the strong force — in addition to their electric charge. The strong interaction, as described by quantum chromodynamics, leads to the phenomena known as QCD color confinement, thereby implying that quarks cannot exist as a free states, as observed for leptons, but can only be detected as bound states, called \textit{hadrons}. Only color-neutral hadrons exist in nature, so the only possible states are mesons, composed of a quark-antiquark pair, and baryons, formed by three quarks. The top quark, however, is an exception, since its very high mass and therefore its high instability cause it to decay via electroweak interaction before it can form a bound state. At very high energies, the strong force weakens, causing quarks to behave like asymptotically free particles and allowing their interactions to be studied in proton colliders like the LHC.

\paragraph{Leptons}
The other variety of fermions are known as leptons, which, devoid of color charge, interact solely through the weak and electromagnetic forces. Each family of lepton doublets consists of a charged lepton with electric charge $Q=1$: electron ($e$), muon ($\mu$) and tau ($\tau$); and the corresponding neutrino ($\nu$) with $Q=0$.

The three charged leptons have masses that span more than three orders of magnitude, $m_e = 0.511$ MeV, $m_\mu = 105.66$ MeV and $m_\tau = 1776.93 \pm 0.09$ MeV, the first two being known with a relative precision better than $10^{-7}$~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026mpi}. As a consequence, the electron is the only stable charged lepton, while the muon has a mean lifetime of $2.197$ $\mu$s and the tau of $(290.3 \pm 0.5) \times 10^{-15}$ s. In collider experiments, muons are produced with a Lorentz boost large enough to cross the whole detector before decaying, so that they behave as stable particles, whereas the tau decays before reaching the first layer of the tracker and can only be inferred from its decay products.

Neutral leptons, neutrinos, share the same lepton flavor as their corresponding charged leptons, finding $\nu_e$,$\nu_{\mu}$ and $\nu_{\tau}$. Because they are electrically neutral, neutrinos only interact with matter through the weak force, which makes direct detection in experiments unfeasible. Their indirect signature is revealed by the missing energy in the final state. In the classical formulation of the SM, the neutrino is assumed to be massless. This assumption, however, is contradicted by the observation of neutrino oscillations, first established with atmospheric neutrinos by Super-Kamiokande in 1998~\cite{Super-Kamiokande:1998kpq} and confirmed with solar neutrinos by SNO~\cite{SNO:2002tuh}. Oscillations can only occur if neutrinos are massive and the flavor states $\nu_e$, $\nu_\mu$ and $\nu_\tau$ are superpositions of three mass states, $\nu_1$, $\nu_2$ and $\nu_3$, a mixing described by the Pontecorvo-Maki-Nakagawa-Sakata (PMNS) matrix~\cite{Pontecorvo:1957qd,Maki:1962mu} that requires extending the original formulation of the SM. Oscillation experiments measure the differences between the squared masses of these three states, which do not exceed a few $10^{-3}$ eV$^2$, while the direct measurement from tritium $\beta$ decay constrains the absolute mass scale to below $0.45$ eV, so that all three neutrino masses lie below the eV scale~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026mpi}.


\subsubsection{Bosons}

\paragraph{Force-carrying particles: Gauge Bosons}
%
Gauge bosons are spin-1 particles responsible for mediating the fundamental interactions of the SM. They are the excitations of each gauge field associated with local symmetries, so the number of bosons mediating each force depends on the dimension of the gauge group, and their properties follow from the Lagrangian of that interaction.

The symmetry gauge group $SU(3)_C$ is associated with the strong interaction and involves eight generators, each of them associated with a gauge boson, the gluon ($g$). Gluons are massless and electrically neutral particles that carry color charge and therefore interact with other colored particles, i.e., themselves and quarks. As a result of being colored particles, gluons cannot be directly observed as free particles, yet their existence is inferred from the hadronic jets produced by gluon emission, with the first experimental evidence appearing in 1979~\cite{TASSO:1979zyf,Barber:1979yr,PLUTO:1979dxn,JADE:1979rke}.


The electroweak sector unified in the symmetry $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ consists of four generators. The photon ($\gamma$), a neutral, massless particle, is associated with the electromagnetic force. The other three generators are the $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons, which are responsible for the charged and neutral currents of the weak force, respectively. The $W$ and $Z$ bosons were directly observed in 1983, and the two charged $W$ bosons, with opposite charges $Q=\pm1$, have identical masses of $80.3$ GeV, while the mass of the neutral $Z$ boson is $91.2$ GeV.

\paragraph{Scalar Boson: Higgs Boson}
%
To endow the $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons with mass, the $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ symmetry must be broken. This breaking is realized spontaneously through the Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism, which introduces a scalar field and generates the masses without spoiling the gauge invariance or the renormalizability of the SM. The quantum excitation of this field is the Higgs boson, the only fundamental scalar or spin-0 particle in the SM. It couples directly to all the massive particles, imparting mass to them, as has been confirmed experimentally, while photons, gluons and, in the SM, neutrinos remain massless. Its description and properties are intimately linked to the EWSB mechanism, detailed in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}.

% The Higgs boson is unique among the other particles because it is the only non-gauge spin-0 or scalar boson in the SM. It emerged as a result of the Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism, which was developed to explain bosons with non-zero mass, contrary to previous theoretical predictions. Likewise, the Higgs boson interacts directly with the other particles, except for photons and gluons, imparting mass to them, as has been proven experimentally. Its description and properties are intimately linked to the EWSB mechanism, detailed in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}.

\subsection{Fundamental interactions}
%
\subsubsection{Strong interaction}
%
The strong interaction is described by QCD, a gauge theory based on the non-abelian group $SU(3)_C$, where the subscript $C$ refers to color, the conserved charge associated with the symmetry. Since only quarks carry color, only the quark fields transform non-trivially under this group, and the construction of the theory starts from the Lagrangian density of a free fermion field, the Dirac Lagrangian,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dirac}} = \bar{\psi}(x)\,(i\slashed{\partial} - m)\,\psi(x),
    \label{eq:dirac_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
%
where $\psi(x)$ is the quark spinor field at the spacetime coordinates $x$, $m$ is the quark mass, and $\slashed{\partial} = \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu$ denotes the contraction of the Dirac matrices $\gamma^\mu$ with the spacetime derivatives. This Lagrangian is invariant under global $SU(3)_C$ transformations, but loses its invariance under local, in which the quark field transforms as
\begin{equation}
    \psi(x) \rightarrow e^{\left(i g_s \theta^a(x) \frac{\lambda_a}{2}\right)} \psi(x),
    \label{lagrangian_transformation}
\end{equation}
%
with $\lambda_a$ being the eight Gell-Mann matrices that generate the group, $\theta^a(x)$ arbitrary real functions of the spacetime point, and $g_s$ the strong coupling constant. To restore local gauge invariance, understood as the preservation of the invariance of the Lagrangian density \eqref{eq:dirac_lagrangian} under the transformation \eqref{lagrangian_transformation}, the derivative $\partial_\mu$ is redefined as covariant derivative
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i g_s\, \frac{\lambda_a}{2}\, G^a_\mu,
    \label{covariant_derivative}
\end{equation}
%
where the new gauge vector fields $G^a_\mu$ correspond to the eight gluon fields, the gauge bosons mediators for the strong interaction. Incorporating the vector fields ensures that the covariant derivative transforms equivalently as the quark fields and, consequently, the Lagrangian density is promoted to be invariant under local gauge transformations. In order to propagate, the gluons require a kinetic term, which is built from the gluon field-strength tensor
\begin{equation}
    G^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu G^a_\nu - \partial_\nu G^a_\mu - g_s f^{abc}\, G^b_\mu G^c_\nu,
    \label{gluon_field}
\end{equation}
%
where $f^{abc}$ symbols denote the structure constants of $SU(3)_C$. Combining these ingredients, the QCD Lagrangian reads
% \begin{equation}
%     \mathcal{L}_{\text{QCD}} = \bar{\psi}\,(i\slashed{\partial} - m)\,\psi - g_s\, \bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu \frac{\lambda_a}{2}\psi\, G^a_\mu - \frac{1}{4}\, G^a_{\mu\nu} G_a^{\mu\nu}.
%     \label{QCD_Lagrangian}
% \end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{QCD}} =
    \underbrace{\bar{\psi}\,(i\slashed{\partial} - m)\,\psi}_{\substack{\text{free quark}\\ \text{propagation}}}
    \underbrace{{}-g_s\, \bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu \frac{\lambda_a}{2}\psi\, G^a_\mu}_{\substack{\text{quark-gluon}\\ \text{interaction}}}
    \underbrace{{}-\frac{1}{4}\, G^a_{\mu\nu} G_a^{\mu\nu}}_{\substack{\text{gluon kinetic}\\ \text{term}}}.
    \label{QCD_Lagrangian}
\end{equation}
%
The first term is the Dirac Lagrangian of Equation \ref{eq:dirac_lagrangian}, which describes the free propagation of the quarks. The second term arises from the covariant derivative and describes the interaction between quarks and gluons, with a strength set by $g_s$ — usually expressed through $\alpha_s = g_s^2/4\pi$ —, while the third is the kinetic term of the gluons, which, owing to the last term of Equation \ref{gluon_field}, also contains cubic and quartic gluon self-interactions, a direct consequence of the non-abelian nature of $SU(3)_C$. The requirement of local gauge invariance therefore generates the eight gluons, which differ only in the color charge they carry, and fixes their interactions with the quarks and among themselves. In contrast, a gluon mass term, of the form $m_g^2\, G^a_\mu G_a^\mu$, would break this invariance, so that gluons are necessarily massless.

\subsubsection{Electroweak interaction}
%
The electroweak (EWK) theory provides a unified description of the electromagnetic and weak interactions, obtained by imposing local gauge invariance under the group $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$. Since the two interactions are very different in nature, they are first presented separately before showing how both emerge from a common symmetry.


% electromagnetic sector
The gauge group $U(1)_{em}$ is the abelian group associated with the electromagnetic sector, whose description is encapsulated in the theory of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED). As in the case of QCD, the Dirac Lagrangian of Equation \ref{eq:dirac_lagrangian} is invariant under global phase transformations, $\psi \rightarrow e^{iQ\theta}\psi$, but not under local ones, in which the phase $\theta(x)$ varies from point to point. Local invariance is restored by introducing a single vector field, the photon field $A_\mu$, through the covariant derivative and the corresponding field-strength tensor
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i e Q\, A_\mu, \qquad F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu,
    \label{eq:qed}
\end{equation}
%
where $e$ is the unit of electric charge and $Q$ the charge of the fermion in units of $e$. Since $U(1)_{\text{em}}$ is abelian, $F_{\mu\nu}$ contains no quadratic term, so that, unlike gluons, photons do not interact with each other. Additionally, as for the gluons, a photon mass term would break gauge invariance, so that the photon is massless. In the SM, however, the photon field $A_\mu$ is not a fundamental field of the theory, but arises as a combination of the gauge fields of the electroweak symmetry, as shown below.\medskip


% Weak
The non-abelian group $SU(2)_L$ is responsible for describing the weak interaction, which differs substantially from the other forces and therefore requires a more complex structure. It couples universally to all fermions, connects different quark flavors through the Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa (CKM) mixing matrix, and is the only interaction observed to violate charge (C), parity (P), and combined charge-parity (CP) symmetries. The violation of parity~\cite{Lee:1956qn,Wu:1957my} manifests itself in the fact that the weak interaction acts differently on fermions depending on an intrinsic property known as \textit{chirality}. This property, which is defined through the matrix $\gamma^5 = i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$, built from the Dirac matrices of Equation \ref{eq:dirac_lagrangian}. Since $(\gamma^5)^2 = 1$, its eigenvalues are $\pm1$, and any fermion field can be decomposed into two components of opposite chirality, referred to as left-handed, with eigenvalue $-1$, and right-handed, with eigenvalue $+1$, which are obtained with the projection operators $P_L = (1-\gamma^5)/2$ and $P_R = (1+\gamma^5)/2$. Only the left-handed components of the fermions — or right-handed components of the antifermions —, transform under $SU(2)_L$, as indicated by its subscript $L$. In the limit of a massless particle, chirality coincides with helicity, the normalized projection of the spin onto the direction of motion.


Accordingly, the fermion fields of each generation are arranged into a left-handed doublet, which transforms under $SU(2)_L$, and right-handed singlets, which do not — two for quarks, and only one for leptons, since the SM contains no right-handed neutrino:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_L \equiv P_L\begin{pmatrix}\psi \\ \psi'\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_L \\ \psi'_L\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
    \psi_R \equiv P_R\,\psi, \qquad
    \psi'_R \equiv P_R\,\psi',
    \label{fermionic_fields}
\end{equation}
%
where $\psi$ and $\psi'$ denote the two members of a doublet, either the neutrino and the charged lepton or the up- and down-type quarks. Leptons and quarks thus belong to separate doublets, and since no interaction of the SM connects them, a quark can never be transformed into a lepton or vice versa. The charge associated with $SU(2)_L$ is the weak isospin $T_3$, which takes the values $T_3=+1/2$ for the upper and $T_3=-1/2$ for the lower component of the doublets, and $T_3=0$ for the right-handed singlets.

As in QCD, local invariance under $SU(2)_L$ requires one gauge field for each generator of the group, so that three fields $W^i_\mu$, with $i=1,2,3$, are introduced. They enter the covariant derivative through the term $i g\, T_i\, W^i_\mu$, where $g$ is the weak coupling constant and $T_i = \sigma_i/2$, with $\sigma_i$ the Pauli matrices, are the generators of $SU(2)_L$, and their kinetic term is built from the field-strength tensor
\begin{equation}
    W^i_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu W^i_\nu - \partial_\nu W^i_\mu - g\, \epsilon^{ijk}\, W^j_\mu W^k_\nu,
    \label{eq:ew_field_strengths}
\end{equation}
%
where the Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon^{ijk}$ provides the structure constants of $SU(2)_L$. Since $SU(2)_L$ is non-abelian, its tensor contains a quadratic term, which gives rise to cubic and quartic self-interactions among the $W^i_\mu$ fields. However, $SU(2)_L$ alone cannot describe the observed interactions. Its neutral field, $W^3_\mu$, couples only to left-handed fermions and therefore cannot be identified with the photon, which couples equally to both chiralities, so that the electromagnetic interaction must be incorporated into the same symmetry.\medskip

This incorporation is achieved by introducing an additional abelian group, $U(1)_Y$, whose charge is the weak hypercharge $Y$, carried by both chiralities and related to the electric charge through the Gell-Mann-Nishijima relation, $Q = T_3 + Y/2$. The electromagnetic symmetry $U(1)_{\text{em}}$ is therefore not independent, but contained in $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$. It is noteworthy that a right-handed neutrino — and a left-handed antineutrino — would carry no electric charge, no weak isospin, no hypercharge and no color, and would thus be sterile with respect to all the interactions of the SM. Local invariance under the full group requires the covariant derivative
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i g\, T_i\, W^i_\mu + i g'\, \frac{Y}{2}\, B_\mu,
    \label{eq:ew_covariant_derivative}
\end{equation}
%
where $B_\mu$ is the gauge field of $U(1)_Y$ and $g'$ its coupling constant, and where $T_i = 0$ for the right-handed singlets, which therefore couple only to $B_\mu$. The kinetic term of $B_\mu$ is built from the tensor $B_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu B_\nu - \partial_\nu B_\mu$, which has the same abelian form as the electromagnetic tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ of Equation \ref{eq:qed}, but is built from the hypercharge field, which couples to $Y$ rather than to $Q$. The EWK Lagrangian finally reads
% \begin{equation}
%     \begin{split}
%     \mathcal{L}_{\text{EWK}} = {}& \bar{\Psi}_L\, i \slashed{D}\, \Psi_L + \bar{\psi}_R\, i \slashed{D}\, \psi_R + \bar{\psi}'_R\, i \slashed{D}\, \psi'_R \\
%     & - \frac{1}{4}\, W^i_{\mu\nu} W_i^{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{4}\, B_{\mu\nu} B^{\mu\nu},
%     \end{split}
%     \label{EW_Lagrangian}
% \end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{EWK}} =
    \underbrace{\bar{\Psi}_L\, i \slashed{D}\, \Psi_L}_{\substack{\text{left-handed}\\ \text{doublets}}}
    + \underbrace{\bar{\psi}_R\, i \slashed{D}\, \psi_R + \bar{\psi}'_R\, i \slashed{D}\, \psi'_R}_{\substack{\text{right-handed}\\ \text{singlets}}}
    \underbrace{{}- \frac{1}{4}\, W^i_{\mu\nu} W_i^{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{4}\, B_{\mu\nu} B^{\mu\nu}}_{\substack{\text{gauge}\\ \text{kinetic terms}}}.
    \label{EW_Lagrangian}
\end{equation}
%
The fermion terms contain, through the covariant derivative, both the free propagation of the fermions and their interactions with the gauge fields, while the gauge kinetic terms describe the propagation of the gauge fields and, through the quadratic term of $W^i_{\mu\nu}$, their self-interactions. However, the fields $W^i_\mu$ and $B_\mu$ do not correspond directly to the observed bosons, which arise as combinations of them. The first two $SU(2)_L$ fields combine into the charged fields $W^\pm_\mu = (W^1_\mu \mp i\,W^2_\mu)/\sqrt{2}$, associated with the $W^\pm$ bosons, while the two neutral fields, $W^3_\mu$ and $B_\mu$, mix into the fields of the $Z$ boson and the photon through a rotation by the Weinberg angle $\theta_W$, a free parameter of the SM,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix} A_\mu \\ Z_\mu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta_W & \sin\theta_W \\ -\sin\theta_W & \cos\theta_W \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} B_\mu \\ W^3_\mu \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tan\theta_W = \frac{g'}{g}.
    \label{eq:weinberg_mixing}
\end{equation}
%
Rewriting Equation \ref{EW_Lagrangian} in terms of these physical fields separates it into four contributions, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWK}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{free}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{CC}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{NC}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{kin}}$, which describe the free propagation of the fermions, their charged- and neutral-current interactions with the gauge bosons, and the kinetic terms of the gauge fields,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{free}} &= \bar{\psi}\, i\slashed{\partial}\, \psi, \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CC}} &= -\frac{g}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\bar{\psi}_L\gamma^\mu\psi'_L\, W^+_\mu + \bar{\psi}'_L\gamma^\mu\psi_L\, W^-_\mu\right), \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{NC}} &= -e\,Q\,\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi\, A_\mu - \frac{g}{\cos\theta_W}\,\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\left(T_3\, P_L - Q\sin^2\theta_W\right)\psi\, Z_\mu, \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{kin}} &= -\frac{1}{4}\, W^i_{\mu\nu} W_i^{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{4}\, B_{\mu\nu} B^{\mu\nu},
    \end{split}
    \label{eq:ew_currents}
\end{equation}
%
where a sum over all fermions is implied, and the photon coupling, $e = g\sin\theta_W = g'\cos\theta_W$, is identified as the unit of electric charge. The free term is the Dirac Lagrangian of Equation \ref{eq:dirac_lagrangian} without its mass term, for reasons discussed below. The charged-current term, mediated by the $W^\pm$ bosons, acts only on the left-handed components, since it originates from the $SU(2)_L$ part of the covariant derivative, and changes the flavor of the fermion within the doublet. The neutral-current term, mediated by the photon and the $Z$ boson, contains in its first part the photon interaction of QED that follows from Equation \ref{eq:qed}, while the $Z$ boson couples to both chiralities, but with different strengths. Finally, the kinetic term coincides with the gauge kinetic terms of Equation \ref{EW_Lagrangian} and, once written in terms of the $W^\pm$, $Z$ and photon fields, gives rise to trilinear and quadrilinear self-couplings involving the $W^\pm$ bosons, such as the $WW\gamma$ and $WWZ$ vertices.\medskip


Up to this point, none of the terms of the EWK Lagrangian of Equation \ref{EW_Lagrangian} contains a mass, so that all the particles of the theory, both the gauge bosons and the fermions, are massless. This is a direct consequence of the symmetry, since a mass term for the gauge bosons, of the form $m^2 W^i_\mu W_i^\mu$, would break gauge invariance, as in the case of the gluons and the photon. A fermion mass term, which was allowed in QCD and QED, is now forbidden as well, since $m\bar{\psi}\psi = m(\bar{\psi}_R\psi_L+\bar{\psi}_L\psi_R)$ mixes the two chiralities, which transform differently under $SU(2)_L$~\cite{Herrero:1998eq}. Local gauge invariance is therefore only preserved if all the masses vanish, in stark contradiction with the observed masses of the $W^\pm$ and $Z$ bosons and of the fermions, since only the photon and the gluons are massless. To account for this evidence while maintaining gauge invariance, the SM relies on the mechanism proposed in 1964 by R. Brout and F. Englert~\cite{Englert:1964et}, by P. Higgs~\cite{Higgs:1964ia,Higgs:1964pj}, and by G. Guralnik, C. Hagen and T. Kibble~\cite{Guralnik:1964eu}, which explains the origin of the masses through the spontaneous breaking of the electroweak symmetry.



\subsection{The Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism}
\label{subsec:BEH}
%
Given that the EWK theory as it stands fails to account for the masses of the electroweak bosons, additional mass terms must be incorporated into the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWK}}$ without breaking its gauge invariance. The origin of this additional degree of freedom, corresponding to the mass term, is a direct consequence of the so-called electroweak symmetry breaking, which occurs when the potential of a system is invariant under symmetry transformations, but its individual ground states are not. This mechanism is first described for the gauge bosons, whose masses arise from the breaking of the electroweak symmetry, and then extended to the fermions, which acquire their masses through their Yukawa interactions with the same scalar field.


\subsubsection{Electroweak symmetry breaking}
\label{subsubsec:EWSB}
In the SM, spontaneous symmetry breaking takes place through the Brout-Englert-Higgs (BEH) mechanism~\cite{Englert:1964et,Higgs:1964ia,Higgs:1964pj,Guralnik:1964eu}, which explains the process by which the masses of the gauge bosons and fermions are generated upon the breaking of the electroweak symmetry, $SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$, at low energies. According to the Goldstone theorem~\cite{Goldstone:1961eq,Goldstone:1962es}, a massless spin-0 boson, the Goldstone boson, appears for each generator of a continuous global symmetry that is spontaneously broken. However, in a theory with local gauge symmetry, gauge bosons and Goldstone bosons recombine to form massive bosons, thereby giving rise to the Higgs mechanism. The breaking of the EWK symmetry is therefore based on the introduction of a complex scalar $SU(2)_L$ doublet with hypercharge $Y_\phi=1$, known as the Higgs field,
\begin{equation}
    \phi = \begin{pmatrix} \phi^{+} \\ \phi^{0} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} \phi_{1} + i\,\phi_{2} \\ \phi_{3} + i\,\phi_{4} \end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:higgs_doublet}
\end{equation}
%
where $\phi^+$ is a charged field with $T_3=+1/2$ and $\phi^0$ a neutral field with $T_3=-1/2$. The field must be a scalar in order to satisfy spatial isotropy, since otherwise its expectation value in the vacuum would be frame-dependent, and this value must be constant to satisfy homogeneity. With the covariant derivative of Equation \ref{eq:ew_covariant_derivative}, the BEH Lagrangian reads
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{BEH}} = (D_\mu \phi)^\dagger (D^\mu \phi) - V(\phi), \qquad V(\phi) = -\mu^2\,\phi^\dagger \phi + \lambda\,(\phi^\dagger \phi)^2,
    \label{higgs_potential}
\end{equation}
%
where the scalar potential $V(\phi)$ is chosen with a form that induces spontaneous symmetry breaking, and $\mu^2$ and $\lambda$ are constants of the model. The values these parameters can take are restricted by the requirement that the potential be bounded from below, i.e., ensuring the stability of the theory, which demands $\lambda>0$. If $\mu^2<0$, the potential has a single minimum at the origin, $\phi=0$, which is symmetric under the transformations of the group, so that the symmetry is exact. Conversely, if $\mu^2>0$, the potential adopts the \textit{Mexican hat} shape illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:higgs_potential}. In this scenario, the origin becomes an unstable local maximum, while the minimum is found for the field configurations that satisfy $\phi^\dagger \phi = \mu^2/2\lambda \equiv v^2/2$, where $v$ is the vacuum expectation value (VEV).
%
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/chapter_I/higgs-champ.png}
    \caption{Shape of the Higgs potential of Equation \ref{higgs_potential} for $\mu^2>0$: the origin becomes an unstable local maximum, and the minimum, located at $|\phi| = v/\sqrt{2}$, is infinitely degenerate.}
    \label{fig:higgs_potential}
\end{figure}

Since the Lagrangian is invariant under $SU(2)_L$ and $U(1)_Y$ phase transformations, this minimum is infinitely degenerate: all the configurations $\phi_0 = (v/\sqrt{2})\,e^{i\theta}$, which form a circle around the origin, have the same energy, $V(\phi_0) = -\lambda v^4/4$, and a phase transformation turns any of them into another. This degeneracy is the origin of the symmetry breaking, since the system must select one of these states as its ground state, which, without loss of generality, can be chosen as
\begin{equation}
    \phi_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:higgs_vev}
\end{equation}
%
This choice breaks both $SU(2)_L$ and $U(1)_Y$ symmetries, while preserving the $U(1)_{em}$ symmetry, according to the scheme $SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y\rightarrow U(1)_{\text{em}}$. Therefore, three of the four generators are broken spontaneously, yielding three massless Goldstone bosons, in accordance with the Goldstone theorem. The particle spectrum is obtained by expanding the field around this ground state,
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = \exp\!\left( \frac{i\,\sigma_i\,\theta^i(x)}{2v} \right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v + H(x) \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
%
where $H(x)$ and the three $\theta^i(x)$ are four real fields that represent small perturbations. The real fields $\theta^i(x)$ correspond to the Goldstone bosons, which are not observed in nature, since the freedom to perform local $SU(2)_L$ transformations on $\phi$ can be used to remove them entirely from the Lagrangian. In this so-called unitary gauge, the Higgs field reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v + H(x) \end{pmatrix},
    \label{phi_parametrization}
\end{equation}
%
and the only remaining scalar is the real field $H(x)$, associated with a massive particle, the Higgs boson. Substituting Equation \ref{phi_parametrization} into Equation \ref{higgs_potential}, and rewriting the gauge fields in terms of the physical fields introduced above, the BEH Lagrangian takes the form
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{BEH}} = \frac{1}{2}\,\partial_\mu H\,\partial^\mu H
    + \left(m_W^2\, W^+_\mu W^{-\mu} + \frac{1}{2}\, m_Z^2\, Z_\mu Z^\mu\right)\left(1+\frac{H}{v}\right)^{2} - V(H),
    \label{eq:beh}
\end{equation}
%
where the first term is the kinetic term of the Higgs boson, the second describes its interplay with the $W^\pm$ and $Z$ bosons, and the last is the scalar potential. Expanding the factor $(1+H/v)^2$, the second term splits into three types of contributions,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{BEH}} \supset {}&
    \underbrace{m_W^2\, W^+_\mu W^{-\mu} + \frac{1}{2}\, m_Z^2\, Z_\mu Z^\mu}_{\text{masses of } W^\pm \text{ and } Z}
    + \underbrace{\frac{2m_W^2}{v}\, H\, W^+_\mu W^{-\mu} + \frac{m_Z^2}{v}\, H\, Z_\mu Z^\mu}_{HVV \text{ interactions}} \\[4pt]
    & + \underbrace{\frac{m_W^2}{v^2}\, H^2\, W^+_\mu W^{-\mu} + \frac{m_Z^2}{2v^2}\, H^2\, Z_\mu Z^\mu}_{HHVV \text{ interactions}}.
    \end{split}
    \label{eq:beh_gauge}
\end{equation}
%
The first contains the mass terms of the vector bosons: the degrees of freedom of the three Goldstone bosons have become the longitudinal polarizations of the $W^\pm$ and $Z$ bosons, which thereby acquire the masses
%
\begin{equation}
    m_Z = \frac{\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}}{2}\,v, \qquad m_W = \frac{g\,v}{2} = m_Z\cos\theta_W.
    \label{eq:boson_masses}
\end{equation}
%
while the absence of any term involving the photon field reflects the fact that $U(1)_{\text{em}}$ remains unbroken, and confirms that the photon is massless. The other two contributions describe the interactions of the Higgs boson with the vector bosons, $V=W^\pm, Z$, shown in Figure \ref{fig:higgs_vertices}, namely the trilinear $HVV$ vertices and the quartic $HHVV$ vertices, with couplings
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{V} = \frac{2}{v}\, m_{V}^{2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lambda_{2V} = \frac{1}{v^{2}}\, m_{V}^{2}.
\end{equation}
Both are therefore entirely fixed by the masses of the vector bosons and by the VEV, so that their measurement provides a direct test of the mechanism that generates those masses. The quartic coupling, in particular, involves two Higgs bosons and is thus only accessible through Higgs boson pair production, to which it contributes via vector boson fusion, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:SM_production}.

At this stage, the free parameters of the BEH mechanism are the VEV, $v$, and the mass of the Higgs boson, $m_H$. The former sets the energy scale of the electroweak symmetry breaking, and is determined from the relation between the mass of the $W^{\pm}$ boson and the Fermi constant, $G_F$, which is precisely measured from the muon lifetime:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{G_F}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{g^2}{8\,m_W^2} = \frac{1}{2v^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \left(\sqrt{2}\,G_F\right)^{-1/2} \approx 246\ \text{GeV}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Yukawa interactions}
\label{subsubsec:yukawa}
Thus far, the mechanism that generates the masses of the vector bosons and of the Higgs boson itself has been described, while the fermions have remained massless. Their masses arise from an additional term in the SM Lagrangian, the Yukawa interaction between the Higgs field and the fermions, introduced by S. Weinberg in his formulation of the electroweak theory of leptons~\cite{Weinberg:1967tq} and later extended to the quark sector, which couples the left-handed doublets to the right-handed singlets of Equation \ref{fermionic_fields} and reads
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Yukawa}} = -y_{\psi'}\, \bar{\Psi}_L\, \phi\, \psi'_R - y_{\psi}\, \bar{\Psi}_L\, \tilde{\phi}\, \psi_R + \text{h.c.},
    \label{yukawa_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
%
where $y_{\psi}$ and $y_{\psi'}$ are the Yukawa couplings and h.c. denotes the hermitian conjugate terms. The lower component of the doublet, with $T_3=-1/2$, couples to the Higgs field $\phi$, whose VEV lies in its lower component, while the upper component, with $T_3=+1/2$, couples to the conjugate field $\tilde{\phi} = i\sigma_2\phi^*$, which after EWSB takes the form
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\phi}(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} v + H(x) \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{phi_conjugated}
\end{equation}
%
Introducing Equations \ref{phi_parametrization} and \ref{phi_conjugated}, the Yukawa Lagrangian can be written more conveniently as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Yukawa}} =
    \underbrace{-\sum_f m_f\, \bar{\psi}_f \psi_f}_{\text{fermion masses}}
    \;\underbrace{-\sum_f \frac{m_f}{v}\, H\, \bar{\psi}_f \psi_f}_{Hf\bar{f}\text{ interactions}},
    \qquad m_f = \frac{y_f\, v}{\sqrt{2}},
    \label{eq:yukawa_masses}
\end{equation}
%
where the sum runs over all the fermions except the neutrinos, and $\bar{\psi}_f\psi_f = \bar{\psi}_{f,L}\psi_{f,R} + \bar{\psi}_{f,R}\psi_{f,L}$ couples the two chiralities. The first term contains the fermion masses, each proportional to the Yukawa coupling of the fermion and to the VEV, while the second describes the interaction of the fermions with the Higgs boson, with a coupling $g_{Hff} = m_f/v = y_f/\sqrt{2}$ proportional to the fermion mass. Since the SM contains no right-handed neutrinos, neutrinos do not acquire mass through this mechanism.\bigskip

\noindent In essence, the BEH mechanism resolves the inconsistency of the electroweak theory by introducing a scalar field with a non-zero VEV. Following the symmetry breaking, three of the four degrees of freedom of the scalar doublet are absorbed by the $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons as their longitudinal polarizations, providing them with mass, while the fourth gives rise to the Higgs boson. The fermions, in turn, acquire their masses through the Yukawa interactions with the same scalar field, which extend the BEH mechanism to the matter sector. These interactions are governed by the gauge and Yukawa couplings, together with the VEV, which constitute free parameters of the theory and establish, after EWSB, the masses of all the SM particles. As a result, the coupling of the Higgs boson to each particle is proportional to its mass — linearly for fermions and quadratically for vector bosons —, so that the Higgs boson decays preferentially into the heaviest particles that are kinematically accessible, as detailed in the following section.



\subsection{Phenomenology of the Higgs boson}
\label{subsec:H_phenomenology}
%
Despite the theoretical robustness of the BEH mechanism, proposed in 1964, its experimental confirmation took almost five decades. At the Large Electron-Positron collider (LEP), which operated at CERN between 1989 and 2000 in the tunnel that now hosts the LHC, direct searches excluded a SM Higgs boson lighter than $114.4$ GeV~\cite{LEPWorkingGroupforHiggsbosonsearches:2003ing}. At the same time, the precision measurements of electroweak observables performed at LEP and at the SLC collider at SLAC, sensitive to the Higgs boson through quantum corrections, favored a light Higgs boson~\cite{ALEPH:2005ab}. The search continued at the Tevatron, the proton-antiproton collider at Fermilab that operated at $\sqrt{s}=1.96$ TeV until 2011, where the CDF and D0 experiments excluded further mass ranges and observed an excess of events compatible with a Higgs boson decaying into a $b\bar{b}$ pair at masses between $115$ and $140$ GeV~\cite{CDF:2013kiv}. Finally, on July 4, 2012, the ATLAS and CMS collaborations at the LHC claimed the discovery of a new particle compatible with the Higgs boson predicted by the SM~\cite{ATLAS:2012yve,CMS:2012qbp,CMS:2013btf}, observed independently by both experiments with the data collected during Run 1 at $\sqrt{s}=7$ and $8$ TeV, mainly in the $H\to\gamma\gamma$ and $H\to ZZ\to 4\ell$ channels. The discovery confirmed the BEH mechanism as part of the SM, and the measurement of the mass of the new particle fixed the last free parameter of the theory. Its current value, $m_H = 125.13 \pm 0.11$ GeV~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026mpi}, is known with a precision of about one per mille.

Since then, the characterization of the new particle has become one of the central goals of the LHC physics program, with the aim of testing whether it behaves as predicted by the SM and of further exploring the EWSB. The increase of the center-of-mass energy to $\sqrt{s}=13$ TeV in Run 2, between 2015 and 2018, and the collection of about $140$ fb$^{-1}$ per experiment multiplied by about thirty the number of recorded Higgs bosons with respect to the discovery~\cite{CMS:2022dwd,ATLAS:2022vkf}, and Run 3, which started in 2022 at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV, continues this program with a larger dataset. The following paragraphs describe the production and decay modes of the Higgs boson and summarize the status of its characterization after fourteen years of measurements at the LHC.

Figure \ref{fig:H_xsec} illustrates the cross sections of the main production modes as a function of the center-of-mass energy of the proton-proton collisions. The cross sections of these processes are computed as perturbative expansions in the coupling constants of the strong and electroweak interactions, truncated at a given order, the leading order (LO), next-to-leading order (NLO), next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO) and so on, as described in Section \ref{sec:pp_pheno}, so that the accuracy of each prediction is specified by the order reached in each of the two interactions. At the energy of Run 3, $\sqrt{s} = 13.6$ TeV, and for $m_H = 125$ GeV, the dominant mechanism is gluon-gluon fusion (ggF), which proceeds through a loop of heavy quarks, dominated by the top quark, and has a cross section of $51.7^{+4.7\%}_{-7.4\%}$ pb, computed at N$^3$LO in QCD and NLO in the electroweak interaction~\cite{Anastasiou:2016cez,Mistlberger:2018etf,Dulat:2018rbf}. It is followed by vector boson fusion (VBF), about an order of magnitude smaller, with $4.11 \pm 2.4\%$ pb at the same accuracy~\cite{Dreyer:2016oyx,Ciccolini:2007jr}. In the VBF mechanism, two vector bosons radiated by the incoming quarks fuse into a Higgs boson, which in the final state appears alongside two high-momentum jets close to the direction of the beams — the experimental signature that characterizes the process. The next mechanism is associated production with a vector boson ($VH$, $V = W^\pm, Z$), with $1.47^{+1.9\%}_{-2.0\%}$ pb for $WH$ and $0.95^{+3.3\%}_{-3.1\%}$ pb for $ZH$, computed at NNLO in QCD and NLO in the electroweak interaction~\cite{Brein:2003wg,Brein:2012ne,Denner:2011id}. Together with VBF, these modes provide direct access to the couplings of the Higgs boson to the vector bosons. Lastly, associated production with heavy quarks benefits from the large couplings of the Higgs boson to the heaviest fermions, with cross sections of $0.593^{+3.2\%}_{-3.6\%}$ pb for $t\bar{t}H$, at NNLO+NNLL in QCD and NLO in the electroweak interaction~\cite{Catani:2022mfv,Frederix:2018nkq}, $0.57 \pm 9.3\%$ pb for $b\bar{b}H$~\cite{Bonvini:2016fgf}, and $0.106$ pb for single top quark production ($tH$), dominated by the $t$-channel, at NLO in QCD~\cite{Demartin:2015uha}. Among them, $t\bar{t}H$ is of particular interest because it enables a direct determination of the top quark Yukawa coupling, $y_t$, as opposed to the indirect one from the loop of ggF~\cite{LHCHWG:2026R5}.

\subsubsection{Decay modes}
%
At a mass of about $125$ GeV, the Higgs boson can decay into a wide variety of SM particles, which gives access to an exceptionally rich range of experimental channels. The probability of each decay is given by its branching ratio (BR), shown as a function of the Higgs boson mass in Figure \ref{fig:H_br}, and the corresponding leading-order diagrams are shown in Figure \ref{fig:H_decays}. Since the Higgs boson couples to vector bosons with a strength proportional to the square of their mass, $\lambda_V = 2m_V^2/v$, and to fermions with a strength proportional to their mass, $g_{Hff} = m_f/v$, as derived in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}, it decays preferentially into the heaviest particles that are kinematically accessible. The most probable decays are therefore $H\to b\bar{b}$, with a BR of $58\%$, and $H\to WW^*$, with $22\%$, followed by $H\to gg$ and $H\to\tau\tau$, with $8\%$ and $6\%$, respectively~\cite{deFlorian:2016spz}. Decays into massless particles, such as gluons and photons, do not occur directly, but proceed through loops of massive particles, which are in fact a key tool for investigating physics at energies higher than those directly reachable.

\subsubsection{Experimental status}
%
Since the discovery, the properties of the new particle have been measured with increasing precision, starting with those that define the particle itself, namely its mass, its width, and its spin and parity. The mass of the Higgs boson is measured in the $H\to\gamma\gamma$ and $H\to ZZ\to4\ell$ channels, in which all the decay products are reconstructed with excellent resolution. The most precise measurements, $m_H = 125.11 \pm 0.11$ GeV by ATLAS~\cite{ATLAS:2023oaq} and $m_H = 125.38 \pm 0.14$ GeV by CMS~\cite{CMS:2020xrn}, reach a relative precision of about one per mille, and their average is $m_H = 125.13 \pm 0.11$ GeV~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026mpi}. The width of the Higgs boson, in contrast, is predicted to be only $\Gamma_H \approx 4.1$ MeV in the SM, far below the experimental mass resolution of about $1$ GeV, so that it cannot be extracted from the shape of the mass peak. It has instead been determined by comparing the production of on-shell and off-shell Higgs bosons decaying into $ZZ$, which yields $\Gamma_H = 3.2^{+2.4}_{-1.7}$ MeV~\cite{CMS:2022ley}, in agreement with the SM prediction and with the result of ATLAS~\cite{ATLAS:2023dnm}. The angular distributions of the decay products, in turn, established that the new particle is a scalar with even parity, $J^P = 0^+$, the quantum numbers of the Higgs boson predicted by the SM, excluding the alternative spin and parity hypotheses at more than $99.9\%$ confidence level~\cite{CMS:2014nkk,ATLAS:2015zhl}.

The production and decay rates are compared with the SM predictions through signal strength modifiers, defined as the ratio of the measured to the predicted rate, $\mu = \sigma\cdot\mathrm{BR}/(\sigma\cdot\mathrm{BR})_{\text{SM}}$. Combining all the Run 2 measurements, the global signal strength is $\mu = 1.002 \pm 0.057$ for CMS~\cite{CMS:2022dwd} and $\mu = 1.05 \pm 0.06$ for ATLAS~\cite{ATLAS:2022vkf}, about four times more precise than at the time of the discovery, with the statistical, experimental and theoretical uncertainties now contributing at a similar level. The five main production modes, ggF, VBF, $WH$, $ZH$ and $t\bar{t}H$, have been observed individually with significances above five standard deviations, as have the decays into pairs of photons, $Z$ and $W$ bosons, $\tau$ leptons and $b$ quarks. The observations of $t\bar{t}H$ production~\cite{CMS:2018uxb,ATLAS:2018mme} and of the $H\to\tau\tau$~\cite{CMS-HIG-16-043} and $H\to b\bar{b}$~\cite{CMS:2018nsn,ATLAS:2018kot} decays, in particular, established the Yukawa interactions of the Higgs boson with the third-generation fermions. The rarest decays are only beginning to be accessible, with evidence for $H\to\mu\mu$ at three standard deviations~\cite{CMS:2020xwi}, the first indication of a coupling to a second-generation fermion, and for $H\to Z\gamma$ at $3.4$ standard deviations in the combination of ATLAS and CMS~\cite{ATLAS:2023yqk}.

These measurements are interpreted in terms of the couplings of the Higgs boson within the $\kappa$-framework~\cite{LHCHiggsCrossSectionWorkingGroup:2013rie}, in which each coupling is rescaled by a multiplicative factor with respect to its SM value, $\kappa_i = g_i/g_i^{\text{SM}}$, so that the SM corresponds to $\kappa_i = 1$. The rate of each combination of production mode $i$ and decay mode $f$ is then expressed as $\sigma_i \cdot \mathrm{BR}_f = \sigma_i^{\text{SM}}\,\mathrm{BR}_f^{\text{SM}}\,\kappa_i^2\kappa_f^2/\kappa_H^2$, where $\kappa_H^2$ accounts for the modification of the total width. The results of the CMS Collaboration within this framework are summarized in Figure \ref{fig:H_coupling}, which shows that the couplings to the $W$ and $Z$ bosons scale quadratically with their masses and those to the fermions linearly, from the top quark down to the muon, over three orders of magnitude in mass. The couplings to the vector bosons and to the third-generation fermions are determined with a precision of about $10\%$~\cite{CMS:2022dwd}, whereas the coupling to charm quarks is only constrained to $1.1 < |\kappa_c| < 5.5$ at $95\%$ confidence level~\cite{CMS:2022psv}, and those to the first-generation fermions remain beyond experimental reach.

Thus far, all the measured properties of the Higgs boson are consistent with the predictions of the SM. This agreement, however, holds within uncertainties that remain large for most measurements, of order $10\%$ for the best-measured couplings and considerably larger for the rarer processes, so that sizable deviations from the SM are still allowed, and more data, such as those of Run 3 and of the High-Luminosity LHC, are needed to test it at the level of precision at which new physics could manifest itself. The proportionality between the couplings of the Higgs boson and the fermion masses, in particular, has been established for the third generation, whereas for the second only evidence for the coupling to the muon exists, and the couplings to the first generation, tiny in the SM, are not expected to be accessible at the LHC. Several pieces of the picture are therefore still missing, such as the couplings to the lighter fermions or the CP properties of the interactions of the Higgs boson. Among them, the coupling of the Higgs boson to itself constitutes the next fundamental step in validating the EWSB mechanism, since it is the only one that directly probes the shape of the scalar potential. In the SM, the trilinear self-coupling is entirely determined by $m_H$ and $v$ through Equation \ref{higgs_pot}, $\lambda_{HHH} = m_H^2/2v^2 \approx 0.13$.



Experimentally, the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ parameter is accessible at leading order (LO) from Higgs boson pair production rates, and at next-to-leading order (NLO) from cross-section measurements of single Higgs boson production, thanks to radiative NLO electroweak corrections arising from the trilinear coupling. This underscores the key importance of searches for HH production. Furthermore, the experimental determination of HH production has profound implications for BSM theories that introduce additional effective terms into the H potential, given that the kinematic and HH production rates are highly sensitive to new physics. Deviations from SM predictions may indicate the presence of extensions in the scalar sector, ranging from models with additional doublets or singlets to frameworks such as compositeness or supersymmetry. Therefore, directly determining the self-coupling value from HH production constitutes an essential closure test of the BEH mechanism and ultimately probes SM consistency.

\subsection{Limitations of the Standard Model}
\label{subsec:SM_limitations}
The relevance of characterizing SM consistency is supported by a substantial body of theoretical considerations and experimental evidence that points to the existence of physics beyond the SM. Despite the remarkable success in theoretically describing all observed particle fields, the SM falls short in addressing fundamental unsolved problems in Nature, such as the matter-antimatter asymmetry observed in the Universe, nor the existence of a particle species compatible with the dark matter, which is observed from its gravitational effects. Moreover, the present SM formalism is capable of accounting for only three of the fundamental interactions, excluding the gravitational force. In addition to these limitations, the SM is unable to provide an explanation for the experimentally observed neutrino oscillations, which require leptonic masses and mixing that are not covered by the SM theory.

In the electroweak sector, the SM fails to explain three main theoretical elements that are closely related to the physics of the Higgs boson. Firstly, the current theory does not provide an answer to the question of the origin of the three families of fermions, which are identical in all respects except for their coupling to the Higgs boson, spanning several orders of magnitude. Secondly, the hierarchy problem arises from the value ascribed to the Higgs boson mass. Given the Planck scale ($\sim10^{19}$ GeV), the Higgs boson mass should assume high values, due to quadratically divergent radiative corrections that tend to push the mass towards values much larger than the observed $m_{H}$, suggesting the existence of a mechanism to stabilize it and avoid unnatural fine-tuning. Finally, the validity of the theory up to the Planck scale can only be assured if the scalar potential is bounded from below, thereby guaranteeing vacuum stability. In view of the observed values of $m_H$ and $m_t$, the SM is inclined towards a state of metastability in the scalar potential~\cite{EliasMiro:2011aa}, which suggests that the stability of the electroweak vacuum may be questionable at very high energies.

At such scales, the behavior of the Higgs potential is also relevant for cosmology. Its value at the minimum, $V(\phi_0) = -\lambda v^4/4$, has been dropped from Equation \ref{higgs_pot} since a constant does not affect the equations of motion, but it contributes to the vacuum energy, which acts as a cosmological constant and contributes to the curvature of spacetime~\cite{Weinberg:1988cp,Martin:2012bt}. The value predicted by the SM exceeds the vacuum energy density inferred from cosmological observations~\cite{Planck:2018vyg} by more than fifty orders of magnitude, a dilemma that requires either a proper quantum theory of gravity or a mechanism that cancels the vacuum energy density of the Higgs field. In addition, certain scenarios propose that the Higgs field could have acted as the inflaton in the early Universe~\cite{Bezrukov:2007ep,Bezrukov:2013fka}. Both vacuum stability and the viability of Higgs-driven inflation depend on the shape of the scalar potential at very high energies, which is determined by the running value of the BEH potential parameter $\lambda$. This parameter is intimately connected to the Higgs boson self-coupling, which is the primary subject of investigation in this thesis.


In this regard, the Standard Model is naturally conceived as a part of a broader theoretical framework that extends beyond it, operating below the Planck scale and providing a resolution to the aforementioned shortcomings of the SM. Consequently, the existence of BSM physics may offer solutions to the limitations of the current theory, while preserving its remarkable success in explaining the phenomena observed in collider experiments.

When searching for deviations from the SM predictions, two different approaches can be distinguished depending on the energy scale of the new physics. If BSM physics operates at energies accessible to collider experiments, the new states or particles can be directly produced and experimentally tested. In this case, new physics could manifest as a new resonance at a given value of the invariant mass $m_X$, an excess of events that contradicts the SM predictions. Conversely, if the energy scale of the new physics is beyond the current experimental reach, its effects can only be detected indirectly, through deviations from the SM predictions. These deviations may appear in the cross sections and kinematic distributions of SM processes, but also in the properties of the known particles, such as the couplings of the Higgs boson, so that precision measurements become a probe of new physics in their own right. A graphical representation of these two approaches can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_approaches}.

In indirect searches, the results can be interpreted using effective Lagrangians with higher-dimensional operators designed to capture potential BSM effects. In this context, the SM becomes an Effective Field Theory (EFT), which serves as a low-energy approximation of a more comprehensive theory. This approach does not rely on a specific BSM model, which makes it a versatile tool to interpret measurements in terms of new physics. Its generality, however, comes with several limitations: an EFT is only valid as long as the scale of the new physics lies well above the energies probed, a condition that becomes delicate in the high-energy tails of the kinematic distributions; and its expansion must be truncated at a given order, which introduces an additional theoretical uncertainty. Moreover, different BSM models can lead to the same low-energy effects, so that an EFT can reveal the existence of new physics but not identify its nature, which ultimately requires the direct observation of the new states.

\section{Higgs boson pair production}
\label{sec:HH_production}
%
Higgs boson pair production (HH) represents one of the primary goals of the LHC program in the coming decades precisely because it provides direct access to the Higgs sector. The HH production rate depends directly on the trilinear self-coupling, $\lambda_{HHH}$, which, according to Equation \ref{higgs_pot}, determines the structure of the Higgs potential. Therefore, the study of HH production is directly linked to the $\lambda_{HHH}$ parameter.

However, the trilinear self-coupling is not the only interaction that contributes to Higgs boson pair production. In addition to the Higgs self-coupling, which is often considered the primary driver of HH searches, HH production provides a means of testing other SM parameters. Depending on the production mode, the process involves the top quark Yukawa coupling $y_t$, defined in Equation \ref{eq:yukawa_masses}, and the trilinear and quartic couplings to vector bosons, $\lambda_V$ and $\lambda_{2V}$, whose vertices are shown in Figure \ref{fig:higgs_VV}, in addition to the $HHH$ vertex of Figure \ref{fig:higgs_self}. The production mechanisms of an on-shell Higgs boson pair, as detailed in this section, are characterized by different interactions from which the contribution of the trilinear coupling must be disentangled from other effects. Furthermore, since new physics could modify any of these couplings, a deviation in the rate or the kinematics of HH production cannot be attributed to $\lambda_{HHH}$ alone. The couplings are therefore allowed to vary independently, each of them rescaled by its own modifier within the $\kappa$-framework, as discussed in Section \ref{subsubsec:coupling_modifiers}.

Di-Higgs production is a very rare process at hadron colliders. In proton-proton collisions at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV, its SM cross section is dominated by gluon fusion, with about $34$ fb~\cite{H:2026ksi}, followed by vector boson fusion, with about $1.9$ fb~\cite{Grober:2026ece}, more than three orders of magnitude below that of single Higgs boson production. These and the subdominant production mechanisms, together with the precision of their theoretical predictions, are detailed in Section \ref{subsec:SM_production}. Because of this small rate, the couplings involved in HH production remain difficult to measure, and the trilinear self-coupling is only expected to be determined with a precision of about $30\%$ by the end of the High-Luminosity LHC (HL-LHC) phase, with $3$ ab$^{-1}$ of data collected by each experiment~\cite{ATLASCMS:2025HLLHC}. Nevertheless, BSM effects can already be probed with the current LHC data, described in Section \ref{sec:LHC}, since small deviations of the couplings from their SM values can lead to significant variations in the rate and the kinematics of HH production. An overview of these effects is given in Section \ref{subsec:BSM_production}.


\subsection{Di-Higgs production in the SM}
\label{subsec:SM_production}
%
In proton-proton collisions, HH production occurs via different mechanisms depending on the collision energy. The six main mechanisms as a function of collision energy are shown in Figure \ref{fig:HH_xsec}. At the LHC energies, the two dominant production mechanisms are fusion of gluons and vector bosons, discussed in detail in the following. While experimental research has predominantly focused on these two modes, advancements in experimental apparatus and techniques are gradually adding other production mechanisms to the analysis landscape, which are also briefly reviewed below.

\subsubsection{Gluon fusion $gg\rightarrow HH$}
%
Gluon-gluon fusion is the dominant HH production mode at the LHC. Gluons — massless particles — do not interact directly with the Higgs boson, and the interaction is instead mediated by a loop of heavy quarks. The loop is dominated by the top quark, owing to its large Yukawa coupling, while the contribution of the $b$ quark is of the order of $1\%$. Figure \ref{fig:HH_ggF_diagrams} shows the LO Feynman diagrams of the process. In the triangle diagram of Figure \ref{fig:HH_triangle}, an off-shell Higgs boson splits into two Higgs bosons, while in the box diagram of Figure \ref{fig:HH_box}, the two Higgs bosons are radiated from the heavy quark loop. Therefore, the amplitude of the triangle diagram is proportional to the product of the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ and the top quark Yukawa coupling $y_t$, while that of the box diagram is proportional to $y_t^2$.

Although the two diagrams have similar amplitudes, they interfere destructively, resulting in an extremely small total cross section of approximately $34$ fb. Figure \ref{fig:ggf_contributions} shows this phenomenon, depicting the differential cross section corresponding to the triangle and box diagrams, as well as the destructive interference that occurs between them. Since the process proceeds through a loop, its higher-order corrections are particularly difficult to compute with the full dependence on the top quark mass. The most precise prediction therefore combines an NNLO calculation in QCD, in which this dependence is included wherever it is known, with higher-order QCD corrections computed in the limit of an infinitely heavy top quark and with the NLO corrections from the electroweak interaction~\cite{H:2026ksi}. For $m_H = 125$ GeV and $\sqrt{s} = 13.6$ TeV, the resulting cross section is
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{ggF}} = 33.62^{+2.1\%}_{-4.9\%}\,(\text{scale})\;{}^{+5\%}_{-16\%}\,(m_t)\;\pm 2.4\%\,(\text{PDF} + \alpha_s)\ \text{fb},
\end{equation}
where the scale and PDF uncertainties, described in Section \ref{sec:pp_pheno}, are complemented by the uncertainty associated with the renormalization scheme of the top quark mass in the loop, which dominates the total.

The final state of this production mode is characterized by two additional jets, originating from the outgoing quarks, which are emitted close to the beam direction with a large invariant mass and a large separation in pseudorapidity. This distinctive signature allows the VBF signal to be identified despite a cross section about $18$ times smaller than that of gluon fusion. For $m_H = 125$ GeV and $\sqrt{s} = 13.6$ TeV, the cross section computed at N$^3$LO in QCD and NLO in the electroweak interaction~\cite{Grober:2026ece,Dreyer:2018qbw,Dreyer:2020xaj} is
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{VBF}} = 1.874^{+0.05\%}_{-0.03\%}\,(\text{scale})\;\pm 2.7\%\,(\text{PDF})\ \text{fb}.
\end{equation}
%
As in gluon fusion, the contributing diagrams interfere destructively. In this case, however, the cancellation has a deeper origin, since the amplitudes of the diagrams of Figures \ref{fig:HH_vbf_kv} and \ref{fig:HH_vbf_k2v} grow with the invariant mass of the Higgs boson pair, and this growth only cancels for the relation between $\lambda_V$ and $\lambda_{2V}$ predicted by the SM~\cite{Bishara:2016kjn}. Any deviation from this relation is therefore enhanced at high $m_{HH}$, which makes VBF production particularly sensitive to $\lambda_{2V}$, a coupling that does not enter gluon fusion, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:BSM_production}.

\subsubsection{Vector boson associated production: $qq'\rightarrow VHH$}
% 
In vector boson associated production, a quark-antiquark pair annihilates into an off-shell $W$ or $Z$ boson, which produces the Higgs boson pair together with an on-shell vector boson. As shown by the LO Feynman diagrams of Figure \ref{fig:HH_VHH_diagrams}, the process involves the same couplings as VBF production, namely the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ and the couplings $\lambda_V$ and $\lambda_{2V}$ of the Higgs boson to vector bosons. For $m_H = 125$ GeV and $\sqrt{s} = 13.6$ TeV, the cross sections computed at NNLO in QCD are $0.358^{+0.36\%}_{-0.40\%}$ fb for $W^+HH$, $0.187^{+1.3\%}_{-1.4\%}$ fb for $W^-HH$ and $0.396^{+3.4\%}_{-2.7\%}$ fb for $ZHH$, where the uncertainties correspond to the scale variations, while the PDF uncertainties range between $1.9$ and $2.5\%$~\cite{Grober:2026ece,Baglio:2012np}. The larger scale uncertainty of $ZHH$ arises from gluon-initiated contributions, in which the $Z$ boson and the Higgs bosons couple to the gluons through a loop of heavy quarks. In total, VHH production amounts to about $0.94$ fb.

\noindent  As discussed, SM HH production at the LHC is a very rare process. Consequently, HH experimental searches — and those covered in this thesis — have primary focused on the dominant mechanisms, with gluon fusion providing the highest sensitivity to $\lambda_{HHH}$ and VBF production offering a complementary handle on it, together with access to the $HHVV$ interaction. In both cases, the small SM cross section results from the destructive interference between the contributing diagrams, and is therefore the outcome of a delicate cancellation. Deviations of the couplings from their SM values, or the presence of new interactions, can disrupt this cancellation and enhance the production rate by large factors, while also modifying the kinematics of the process, in particular the $m_{HH}$ distribution. Accordingly, HH production is not only a test of the SM, but also a sensitive probe of BSM physics, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:BSM_production}.

\subsection{BSM Di-Higgs production: Towards Effective Field Theories}
\label{subsec:BSM_production}
%
The limitations of the SM discussed in Section \ref{subsec:SM_limitations} motivate the search for new physics, and Higgs boson pair production is a particularly suited probe for it. Owing to its direct connection with the scalar potential, any new physics that modifies the scalar sector is expected to leave an imprint on this process, either through new particles that couple to the Higgs boson or through modified interactions among the particles involved in its production. Testing the SM only requires predicting the HH cross section and comparing it with the data, whereas exploring alternative theories demands in addition understanding how the cross section and the kinematics of the process vary outside the SM. Accordingly, the two complementary approaches illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_approaches} — direct searches for new states and indirect searches for deviations from the SM predictions — translate into two classes of signatures, each with its own interpretation framework: resonant production, naturally interpreted within the specific models that predict the new state, and non-resonant production — the one that also occurs in the SM —, interpreted in a model-independent way through coupling modifiers and effective field theories.\medskip

In the resonant scenario, BSM physics operates at energies accessible to the collider experiments: a new particle $X$ with a mass $m_X > 2m_H$ is produced directly and decays into a pair of Higgs bosons, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:HH_resonant}, so that the signal appears as a localized excess in the invariant mass spectrum of the pair on top of the non-resonant SM production. Such states arise naturally in many extensions of the SM, including extensions of the scalar sector — the singlet model \cite{Robens:2015gla}, the two-Higgs-doublet model \cite{Branco:2011iw} or the minimal supersymmetric SM \cite{Djouadi:2005gj} —, which predict additional spin-0 bosons, and models with warped extra dimensions \cite{Randall:1999ee}, which predict spin-0 radions \cite{Goldberger:1999un} and spin-2 Kaluza–Klein gravitons \cite{Agashe:2007zd}. Despite their different theoretical motivations, these models predict CP-even spin-0 or spin-2 states whose intrinsic width is typically negligible compared to the detector resolution, leading to similar experimental signatures. This common signature allows the models to be probed simultaneously in a model-independent way, by scanning a broad range of mass hypotheses from the kinematic threshold up to several TeV. The search for such resonances in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state with the Run 2 dataset, which covers masses between $250$ and $3000$ GeV \cite{CMS-PAS-B2G-24-011}, relies on the jet assignment algorithm described in Chapter \ref{ch:taggers}, whose adaptation to that search was part of this thesis. Resonant production is otherwise not covered further in this work.\medskip

In the non-resonant scenario, which is the focus of this thesis, the new physics lies at an energy scale beyond the direct reach of the LHC, and its effects appear instead at lower energies as modifications of the interactions involved in Higgs boson pair production, either rescaling the vertices of the SM diagrams of Figures \ref{fig:HH_ggF_diagrams} and \ref{fig:HH_VBF_diagrams} or introducing new contact interactions, such as those shown in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_diagrams} for gluon fusion. These modifications alter both the production rate and the kinematic properties of the pair, so that the experimental signature is not a localized excess but a deformation of the distributions predicted by the SM. Their description is addressed here in three steps of increasing generality. First, the couplings of the SM are rescaled by coupling modifiers, the parametrization in which the results of this thesis are expressed. Second, effective field theories are introduced as the systematic framework that generalizes this approach. Finally, the effective couplings that this framework yields for gluon fusion production are presented, which constitute the basis of the signal modeling developed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}.



\subsubsection{Coupling modifiers}
\label{subsubsec:coupling_modifiers}
%
In the SM, the value of the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ is entirely determined by the values of $v$ and $m_H$, as shown in Equation \ref{higgs_pot}. To remain sensitive to possible BSM effects, it is instead treated as a free parameter, and its deviations from the SM prediction are parametrized through the self-coupling modifier
%
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_{\lambda} = \frac{\lambda_{HHH}}{\lambda_{HHH}^{SM}},
    \label{eq:kappa_lambda}
\end{equation}
which extends to the trilinear self-coupling the $\kappa$-framework introduced in Section \ref{subsec:H_phenomenology}, the same in which the couplings of Figure \ref{fig:H_coupling} are measured. Expressing the results in terms of $\kappa_\lambda$ allows double and single Higgs boson production — the latter sensitive to $\lambda_{HHH}$ through electroweak loop corrections — to be interpreted consistently and combined. The modifiers of the top quark Yukawa coupling, $\kappa_t = y_t/y_t^{SM}$, and of the couplings to vector bosons, $\kappa_V$ and $\kappa_{2V}$, are defined analogously.

The quartic self-coupling $\lambda_{HHHH}$, which in the SM is also fixed by $m_H$ and $v$ and equal to $\lambda_{HHH}$, as shown in Equation \ref{higgs_pot}, can be parametrized analogously through $\kappa_4 = \lambda_{HHHH}/\lambda_{HHHH}^{SM}$. However, it only enters Higgs boson pair production through loop corrections, and the process that probes it directly, triple Higgs boson production, has an SM cross section more than two orders of magnitude smaller than that of HH production~\cite{deFlorian:2019app}. With the data collected so far, both self-couplings can therefore only be constrained through upper limits on the corresponding production rates. While HH searches already restrict $\kappa_\lambda$ to an interval of a few units around the SM value, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:Searches}, the searches for triple Higgs boson production performed with Run 2 data~\cite{ATLAS:2024xcs,CMS:2026xkc} only constrain $\kappa_4$ to values of the order of $10^2$.\medskip

\subsubsection{Effective Field Theories}
\label{subsubsec:EFT_frameworks}
%
Up to now, in non-resonant indirect searches, the self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ has been treated as a free parameter, and deviations from the SM prediction are parametrized via coupling strength modifiers within the $\kappa$-framework. This approach enables the simultaneous exploration of a wide range of BSM scenarios, and allows the results to be reinterpreted within specific models. A generalization of this approach with a systematic method is provided by EFTs, introduced in Section \ref{subsec:SM_limitations}, which permit the coherent and gauge-invariant incorporation of possible corrections to SM predictions. Within this framework, modifications to the Higgs self-coupling and other relevant interactions are organized into an expansion of higher-dimensional operators. Thus, EFTs offer a practical theoretical framework for interpreting experimental results and potentially connecting them to comprehensive UV models.


Accordingly, new physics at a scale $\Lambda \gg v$ can be described by incorporating additional fields into the SM Lagrangian that only act at extremely short distances and high energies, meaning their internal structure can be disregarded within the current LHC range. In practice, this entails describing physics at scales below $\Lambda$ by integrating the heavy fields of an extended renormalizable theory that interact only at high energies. Therefore, from a \textit{bottom-up} perspective, $\Lambda$ can be interpreted as the scale up to which only SM fields propagate, while from a \textit{top-down} approach, it corresponds to the scale of the new physics. The resulting effective theory is not renormalizable; however, this is not problematic since an EFT only describes the low-energy manifestation of a more fundamental, renormalizable theory defined at higher scales. Consequently, this procedure generates higher-dimensional, non-renormalizable operators that capture the residual effects of those fields in the low-energy regime.\medskip

There is not a single way to formulate such an effective description. The criterion used to expand the SM Lagrangian, and the symmetries imposed on the new theory and its fields, lead to the definition of different EFTs. In the context of Higgs boson pair searches, two main descriptions are used in the literature, and they differ precisely in the assumption they make about the nature of the observed scalar \cite{Alasfar:2023xpc}. According to the SM, the physical Higgs boson and the three Goldstone bosons absorbed by the $W$ and $Z$ bosons form a single $SU(2)_L$ doublet, as described in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}. However, the only experimentally established facts are the existence of those three Goldstone bosons and of a light neutral scalar with a mass of about $125$ GeV and couplings compatible with the SM predictions, while whether this scalar is the fourth component of the same doublet or an electroweak singlet remains a hypothesis rather than a measurement. The Standard Model EFT (SMEFT) adopts the first possibility, assuming that the Higgs boson belongs to a doublet and organizing the new physics effects in a linear, gauge-invariant expansion in higher-dimensional operators, whereas the Higgs EFT (HEFT) adopts the second, treating the Higgs boson as an electroweak singlet and realizing the electroweak symmetry non-linearly, which allows for a very general parametrization of the deviations in its interactions. Both lead to internally consistent, gauge-invariant Lagrangians, but they differ in their assumptions and in the correlations they impose among the couplings of the Higgs boson.\medskip

SMEFT retains the Higgs boson as part of an $SU(2)_L$ doublet, so that the electroweak symmetry is realized linearly, exactly as in the SM. Its expansion is therefore organized according to the canonical dimension $D$ of the operators, each additional order being suppressed by a further power of the scale $\Lambda$ of the new physics:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SMEFT}}
    = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}^{(D\leq4)}
    + \sum_{D>4}\sum_i \frac{c_i}{\Lambda^{D-4}}\, \mathcal{O}_i^{(D)},
    \label{eq:eft_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}_i^{(D)}$ are operators of dimension $D$ built from the SM fields and invariant under $SU(3)_C\times SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$ local transformations, $\Lambda$ is the energy scale at which the SM loses validity, and $c_i$ are the Wilson coefficients that parametrize the new physics. Once a complete BSM model is specified, it can be reduced to its low-energy manifestation to express the coefficients $c_i$ in terms of its fundamental parameters, which makes Equation \ref{eq:eft_lagrangian} a model-independent bridge between measurements and theory. The only operator of dimension five violates lepton number and has no impact on Higgs boson phenomenology, so that the leading effects arise at $D=6$, where a complete and non-redundant set of operators is provided by the Warsaw basis \cite{Grzadkowski:2010es}.

The doublet structure has a direct consequence for Higgs boson pair production. In SMEFT, the Higgs field enters the operators only through the combination $\phi^\dagger\phi$, so that a single operator generates the vertices with one and with two Higgs bosons at once. At leading order, the interactions relevant to gluon fusion are governed by five such operators — those modifying the kinetic term and the potential of the Higgs field, the top quark Yukawa interaction, and the contact interaction between the Higgs field and two gluons — and the couplings they generate are therefore correlated. This makes the description more restrictive but also more predictive, since it connects HH production with single Higgs boson and electroweak precision measurements through a common set of parameters. In exchange, SMEFT is only adequate when the new physics decouples, i.e., when the heavy states acquire their mass from a mechanism unrelated to EWSB, so that their effects vanish as their masses increase. Moreover, its predictions depend not only on the Wilson coefficients but also on the scale $\Lambda$ and on the order at which the expansion is truncated, and they remain valid only as long as $c_i E^2/\Lambda^2 \ll 1$, where the relevant energy $E$ is, in HH production, the invariant mass $m_{HH}$ \cite{Heinrich:2022huh}.\medskip

HEFT adopts instead the more agnostic hypothesis where the physical Higgs boson is treated as an electroweak singlet, independent of the Goldstone bosons, and the electroweak symmetry is realized non-linearly. The Higgs field can then appear in interaction terms of arbitrary canonical dimension, so that the expansion is no longer ordered by $D$ but by the loop order $L$ at which each interaction can first be generated~\cite{Buchalla:2013rka,Buchalla:2018yce},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{HEFT}}
    = \mathcal{L}_{\text{LO}}
    + \sum_{L\geq1}\sum_i \left(\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\right)^{L} c_i^{(L)}\,\mathcal{O}_i^{(L)},
    \label{eq:heft_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where each additional order is suppressed by the loop factor $1/16\pi^2$ instead of a power of $1/\Lambda$. The leading-order term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{LO}}$ has the field content of the SM, but the Higgs boson enters it through general polynomials in $H/v$ whose coefficients are independent parameters, so that the couplings involving one and two Higgs bosons are no longer correlated. The construction of this expansion is detailed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}. This more general description accommodates non-decoupling new physics, in which the heavy states acquire part of their mass from EWSB itself, as in composite Higgs models or in scenarios where the Higgs boson is a pseudo-Goldstone boson or a dilaton \cite{Agashe:2004rs,Contino:2010mh,Goldberger:2008zz}. Since any SMEFT Lagrangian can be rewritten in the HEFT language, SMEFT can be regarded as a restricted subset of HEFT, and the two descriptions are complementary rather than alternative: HEFT makes the fewest assumptions, while SMEFT imposes correlations that sharpen the predictions whenever its assumptions hold.\medskip

%Two frameworks are commonly used in this context: Higgs Effective Field Theory (HEFT) and Standard Model Effective Field Theory (SMEFT)~\cite{Buchalla:2018yce, Heinrich:2022huh}. In the former, the Higgs boson is treated as a generic scalar singlet, where EWSB is non-linearly realized, and the EFT expansion is organized in terms of chiral dimensions. In the latter, the Higgs boson is treated as part of an $SU(2)$ doublet, with the same quantum numbers as in the SM. The EWSB is assumed to be linearly realized, leading to a more constrained description of BSM effects in the Higgs sector, with the EFT expansion expressed as a power series in $1/\Lambda$ according to canonical dimensions.

\subsubsection{The effective coupling formalism}
\label{subsubsec:eff_couplings}
%
Whichever description is adopted, its effect on Higgs boson pair production can be expressed through a small number of effective couplings, which provide a common phenomenological language for the two formalisms. These couplings either modify interactions already present in the production amplitude or introduce new contact interactions, thereby altering both the production rate and the kinematic distributions.

In VBF HH production, the effective description only modifies interactions that already exist in the SM — the $HHH$, $HVV$ and $HHVV$ vertices —, rescaling them or introducing allowed tensor structures, so that no new contact interaction appears at leading order and the diagrams keep the same structure as in the SM.

In contrast, gluon fusion is sensitive to interactions that do not exist in the SM. Besides the anomalous trilinear and top quark Yukawa couplings, $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_t$, three contact interactions contribute at leading order, shown in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_diagrams}: $c_2$ couples a Higgs boson pair directly to a top quark pair, generating a triangle diagram without an intermediate Higgs boson; $c_g$ couples a single Higgs boson to two gluons, so that the gluons produce an off-shell Higgs boson that subsequently splits into the pair through the trilinear coupling; and $c_{2g}$ couples the Higgs boson pair directly to two gluons. Since the loops are dominated by the top quark, anomalous values of the bottom quark Yukawa coupling are neglected.

Therefore, the amplitude of $gg\to HH$ is the sum of five contributions, each multiplied by a fixed product of couplings. Consequently, the HH cross section, given by its squared modulus, is at leading order a polynomial in the five couplings with a finite number of terms — the five squared contributions and their ten interferences:
%
\begin{equation}
    R_{HH} = \frac{\sigma_{HH}}{\sigma_{HH}^{SM}} = \sum_{i=1}^{15} A_i\, f_i(\kappa_\lambda,\kappa_t,c_2,c_g,c_{2g}),
    \label{eq:RHH}
\end{equation}
where the $f_i$ are known monomials in the couplings and the $A_i$ are numerical coefficients determined from simulation \cite{Carvalho:2016rys}. In the absence of contact interactions, $c_2=c_g=c_{2g}=0$, Equation \ref{eq:RHH} reduces to the parametrization in $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_t$ of Equation \ref{eq:kl_kt_ggf}.

The set of five effective couplings $\kappa_{\lambda}$, $\kappa_t$, $c_2$, $c_g$ and $c_{2g}$ constitutes a common phenomenological language to which both EFT formalisms can be mapped under different considerations. In HEFT they are free and independent parameters, whereas in SMEFT, at leading order, they are generated by only four combinations of Wilson coefficients, so that their image is a four-dimensional region of the HEFT space: the two gluon contact couplings arise from the same operator, which fixes $c_{2g}$ in terms of $c_g$, and the top quark contact interaction $c_2$ cannot vary without simultaneously modifying the Yukawa coupling $\kappa_t$ \cite{Heinrich:2022huh}. This difference makes Higgs boson pair production one of the few processes capable of distinguishing between the two descriptions, since the couplings involving two Higgs bosons, $c_2$ and $c_{2g}$, do not contribute to single Higgs boson production at leading order. In HEFT they can therefore only be tested through HH production, whereas in SMEFT they are tied to the $t\bar{t}H$ and $ggH$ interactions, already strongly constrained by single Higgs boson measurements, so that the same measured distribution supports different physical conclusions depending on the description adopted.\medskip

Anomalous couplings not only enhance the HH production cross section, but also modify the kinematic properties of the events significantly. As in the simpler case of $\kappa_\lambda$, the five contributions interfere in a non-trivial way and give rise to a wide variety of signal topologies, so that interpreting a measurement in terms of these couplings requires predicting both the rate and the shape of the signal at every point of a five-dimensional space. This evidences the need to develop modeling tools for these effects in physics analyses. Since HEFT provides the most general description with the fewest assumptions, it is the one adopted in this thesis for that purpose: its formal construction for $gg\to HH$ production and the signal modeling techniques that make such an interpretation feasible are developed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}.

\section{Higgs boson pair searches at the LHC}
\label{sec:HH_searches}
%
The phenomenology described in the previous sections determines how Higgs boson pair production is searched for at the LHC. Since each of the two Higgs bosons decays in turn, an HH event can give rise to a large variety of final states, and the extremely small production cross section forces the searches to favor those with a sufficiently large BR. However, the channel with the largest BR is not necessarily the most sensitive one, since each final state poses its own experimental challenges, from the backgrounds it has to face to the number and type of objects that must be reconstructed. Consequently, HH searches do not rely on a single channel but on several complementary ones, and their combination provides the highest sensitivity.

Section \ref{subsec:HH_BR} summarizes the main HH final states accessible at the LHC, together with their BR and experimental challenges, with particular attention to the $bb\tau\tau$ final state studied in this thesis. Next, Section \ref{subsec:Searches} then reviews the experimental status of these searches, from the results obtained by ATLAS and CMS with the Run 2 dataset and their combination, including their interpretation in terms of EFTs, to the first results with Run 3 data, among which the result presented in this thesis is placed, and the prospects for the HL-LHC.



\subsection{Di-Higgs decay modes}
\label{subsec:HH_BR}
%
Since each Higgs boson decays independently, the BR of each HH final state is the product of the BRs of the two Higgs bosons, multiplied by two when the two decays differ. The final states with the largest BR are therefore those involving the dominant decays of the Higgs boson, shown in Figure \ref{fig:H_br}, namely into $b\bar{b}$, $WW$ and $\tau\tau$ pairs, while the decay into top quark pairs is kinematically forbidden. Figure \ref{fig:HH_BR} lists the BR of the main HH final states for $m_H = 125$ GeV. However, a large BR is not enough to provide sensitivity, since the main challenge of HH searches at hadron colliders is to reconstruct the decay products and to distinguish them from much larger backgrounds. The choice of the final state is therefore a trade-off between the BR and the background contamination, which depends on the particles involved and on the capability of the experiments to identify and reconstruct them.
%
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/chapter_I/HH_BR.pdf}
    \caption{Branching ratios, in \%, of the main final states of a Higgs boson pair for $m_H = 125$ GeV, computed from the SM branching ratios of the Higgs boson~\cite{deFlorian:2016spz}.}
    \label{fig:HH_BR}
\end{figure}

The sensitivity to HH production at the LHC is driven by three final states, which offer different balances between these two factors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $HH\to 4b$ has the largest BR, $33.9\%$, but suffers from the overwhelming background of QCD multijet production, which, together with the trigger thresholds on the jets, limits its sensitivity at low $m_{HH}$. In exchange, its large signal yield allows the high-$m_{HH}$ region to be probed, where the Higgs bosons are produced with a large boost and their decay products merge into a single large-radius jet. Advances in jet tagging have made this channel one of the most sensitive to HH production~\cite{CMS:2022bbbb,ATLAS:2023bbbb,CMS:2023boosted4b}, particularly in VBF~\cite{ATLAS:2024VBFbbbb} and VHH~\cite{ATLAS:2023VHH} production.
    \item $HH\to bb\tau\tau$ has a BR about five times smaller, $7.3\%$, but the presence of the $\tau$ lepton pair provides a much purer signature. Its main backgrounds are top quark pair production, Drell–Yan production of $\tau$ lepton pairs and QCD multijet events with jets misidentified as $\tau$ leptons, and the balance between a sizable BR and a moderate background makes it one of the most sensitive channels.
    \item $HH\to bb\gamma\gamma$ has a BR of only $0.26\%$, but is the purest of the three, since the excellent resolution on the diphoton invariant mass allows the signal to be identified as a narrow peak over the smoothly falling diphoton continuum, which can be estimated directly from data.
\end{itemize}
Other final states, such as $bbWW$, $bbZZ$ or those with several leptons, have a smaller sensitivity but complement the three main channels in the combinations described in Section \ref{subsec:Searches}.\bigskip

\noindent This thesis focuses on the $bb\tau\tau$ final state, which represents an intermediate and particularly interesting case. Its complexity stems from the $\tau$ leptons, which are unstable and decay either into lighter leptons or into hadrons, always accompanied by neutrinos. The search must therefore analyze several final states, and the neutrinos, which escape detection, prevent the invariant mass of the $\tau\tau$ system from being determined directly. The channel also suffers from irreducible backgrounds that are kinematically similar to the signal, so that the kinematic information of the event must be fully exploited to reduce them. For these reasons, $bb\tau\tau$ is considered one of the most challenging final states at the LHC, but this effort is rewarded with one of the best sensitivities to both resonant and non-resonant HH production, as anticipated by several phenomenological studies~\cite{Baglio:2012np,Dolan:2012rv,Barr:2013tda}. The properties of this final state and its main backgrounds are discussed in detail in Chapter \ref{ch:bbtt_analysis}.


\subsection{Experimental status of double Higgs searches}
\label{subsec:Searches}
%
The search for Higgs boson pair production started with the Run 1 dataset, of about $20$ fb$^{-1}$ per experiment at $\sqrt{s}=8$ TeV, whose analyses established the fundamental methodology and set upper limits of about $70$ times the SM prediction~\cite{ATLAS:2015sxd,CMS-HIG-15-013}. During Run 2, each experiment collected between $126$ and $140$ fb$^{-1}$ at $\sqrt{s}=13$ TeV, and this increase in luminosity, combined with significant advances in object reconstruction and analysis techniques, enabled a remarkable expansion of the experimental scope. Searches were performed in most of the final states described in Section \ref{subsec:HH_BR}, reaching sensitivities more than an order of magnitude better than those of Run 1, which opened access to regions of the trilinear self-coupling parameter space that were previously inaccessible and allowed the results to be reinterpreted in terms of EFTs. Since these final states complement each other, both in the SM and in BSM scenarios, the highest sensitivity is obtained by combining them, and the combinations of channels constitute the reference result of each data-taking period.


\subsubsection{The Run 2 legacy}
%
Both ATLAS and CMS collaborations combined their searches in the main final states with the complete Run 2 dataset. The ATLAS combination excludes HH production rates greater than $2.9$ times the SM prediction at $95\%$ confidence level (CL), with an expected limit of $2.4$, and constrains the coupling modifiers to $-1.2 < \kappa_\lambda < 7.2$ and $0.6 < \kappa_{2V} < 1.5$~\cite{ATLAS:2024ish}. The CMS combination yields an observed (expected) upper limit of $3.5$ ($2.5$) times the SM prediction and the intervals $-1.35 < \kappa_\lambda < 6.37$ and $0.64 < \kappa_{2V} < 1.40$, and excludes the absence of the $HHVV$ interaction, $\kappa_{2V}=0$, with a significance greater than five standard deviations for any value of $\kappa_\lambda$~\cite{CMS_combination}. In both cases, each interval is obtained with the remaining couplings fixed to their SM values, the sensitivity is driven by the $4b$, $bb\tau\tau$ and $bb\gamma\gamma$ final states, and the statistical procedure used to derive the results is the one described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_statistics}.


The two experiments have subsequently combined their results, which constitutes the most sensitive search for HH production to date~\cite{ATLASCMS:2026HH}. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:comb_upper_limits}, the combination excludes production rates greater than $2.5$ times the SM prediction, with an expected limit of $1.7$, and measures a signal strength of $\mu_{HH} = 0.8^{+0.9}_{-0.7}$, which corresponds to an observed (expected) significance of $1.1$ ($1.3$) standard deviations. Since the predicted HH production rate and kinematics depend on the couplings involved, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:BSM_production}, the same measurement also constrains the coupling modifiers, shown in Figure \ref{fig:comb_trilinear}. Combining final states with different kinematic coverage, together with the data of both experiments, considerably improves this sensitivity, so that the combination restricts the trilinear coupling to $-0.71 < \kappa_\lambda < 6.1$, an interval narrower than that of either experiment alone. In turn, the searches targeting VBF production constrain $\kappa_{2V}$ to $0.73 < \kappa_{2V} < 1.3$. These combined results establish the state of the art at the end of Run 2, and the benchmark against which the Run 3 analyses, including the one presented in this thesis, are measured.

Beyond the coupling modifiers, the Run 2 results have been interpreted within the EFT frameworks introduced in Section \ref{subsubsec:EFT_frameworks}. Within HEFT, the CMS combination sets upper limits on the HH cross section at a set of benchmark points, each representative of a characteristic $m_{HH}$ distribution, constrains the contact couplings with no counterpart in the SM, such as $c_2$, and translates these constraints into the parameters of UV-complete models with an extended scalar sector~\cite{CMS_combination}. The ATLAS combination provides the most stringent HEFT constraints to date~\cite{ATLAS:2024ish}, and the ATLAS search in the $bb\gamma\gamma$ final state with $308$ fb$^{-1}$ has been interpreted in both HEFT and SMEFT, constraining individual couplings and pairs of them~\cite{ATLAS:2025tkn}. \subsubsection{First results with Run 3 data}
%
Run 3, which started in 2022 at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV, signifies a new phase in the search for HH, and has already provided the first results with the new dataset, although a combination of channels has yet to be performed. ATLAS has analyzed the $bb\gamma\gamma$ final state with $308$ fb$^{-1}$, combining the Run 2 dataset with $168$ fb$^{-1}$ of Run 3 data, and measures $\mu_{HH} = 0.9^{+1.4}_{-1.1}$, which translates into an upper limit of $3.8$ times the SM prediction and the interval $-1.7 < \kappa_\lambda < 6.6$~\cite{ATLAS:2025hhd}. In the $bb\tau\tau$ final state, with $196$ fb$^{-1}$, ATLAS measures $\mu_{HH} = 2.6^{+1.4}_{-1.0}$, an excess over the background-only hypothesis with an observed (expected) significance of $2.6$ ($1.2$) standard deviations, compatible with the SM prediction within $1.65$ standard deviations~\cite{ATLAS-HH-bbtautau-Run3}. In CMS, the $4b$ final state with $62$ fb$^{-1}$ at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV sets an observed (expected) upper limit of $4.4$ ($4.4$) times the SM prediction, and its combination with a reanalysis of the Run 2 data reaches $4.7$ ($2.8$)~\cite{CMS:2026znu}, while the $bb\gamma\gamma$ final state, analyzed with a partial Run 3 dataset of $61.9$ fb$^{-1}$, yields $11.0$ ($7.3$)~\cite{CMS-PAS-HIG-25-007}. The search in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state presented in this thesis, performed with the $172$ fb$^{-1}$ collected by CMS between 2022 and 2024, completes the three main final states of CMS in Run 3, and its results are placed in the context of these searches in Chapter \ref{ch:bbtt_results}.

\subsubsection{Prospects for the HL-LHC}
%
The HL-LHC is expected to collect $3$ ab$^{-1}$ per experiment at $\sqrt{s}=14$ TeV. The projections of ATLAS and CMS, which extrapolate the Run 2 analyses to this scenario, foresee the observation of HH production, with an expected significance of up to $7.6$ standard deviations for their combination, and a determination of $\kappa_\lambda$ with an uncertainty of about $30\%$~\cite{ATLASCMS:2025HLLHC}, as discussed in more detail in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_prospects_hllhc}. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:HH_HLLHC}, these expectations have improved substantially with respect to the projections prepared for the previous update of the European Strategy, in which the combination reached about $4$ standard deviations with the same dataset. This improvement is driven by the progress in analysis techniques, detector calibration and jet flavor tagging, and has made $bb\tau\tau$ the most sensitive final state. Run 3 therefore represents the opportunity to consolidate the analysis strategies and the understanding of the backgrounds on which these measurements will rely.

Beyond the HL-LHC, the determination of the Higgs self-coupling is one of the main goals of the future colliders considered in the 2026 update of the European Strategy for Particle Physics~\cite{deBlas:2025gyz}. Higgs factories operating at low energies, such as the FCC-ee, can only access the self-coupling through its loop effects on single Higgs boson production, reaching a precision of about $20\%$ when combined with the HL-LHC data. In contrast, high-energy machines such as the FCC-hh or a muon collider at $10$ TeV would measure HH production directly and determine the self-coupling with a precision of a few percent, while the FCC-hh would also constrain the quartic self-coupling through triple Higgs boson production at the level of about ten times its SM value.




\section{Phenomenology of proton-proton collisions}   
\label{sec:pp_pheno}
% 
Both the results reviewed in the previous section and the improvement in sensitivity expected from Run III and the HL-LHC rely on an accurate prediction of the signal and the backgrounds. However, obtaining such a prediction requires more than the production mechanisms described in Section \ref{subsec:SM_production}, since the LHC does not collide elementary particles, but the protons that contain them. Although the interaction of interest takes place between the constituents of the proton — the quarks and gluons described in Section \ref{subsec:particle_content}, collectively referred to as partons — the way in which they are distributed within the proton cannot be calculated from first principles, and each of them carries only a fraction of the proton momentum. Furthermore, the hard interaction does not occur in isolation: the partons radiate before and after it, the quarks and gluons in the final state hadronize, and the remnants of the protons also interact with one another. As a result, the final state recorded by the detector contains much more than the decay products of the Higgs boson pair. Describing a proton-proton collision therefore requires separating the parts of the process that can be calculated in perturbation theory from those that must be extracted from data or modeled phenomenologically, a separation formalized by the factorization theorem~\cite{Collins:1989gx}.

The following subsections go through this description in the same order in which the prediction is built. First, Section \ref{subsec:pp_factorization} presents the factorization of the hadronic cross section into a partonic cross section, calculated to a given order in perturbation theory, and the parton distribution functions (PDFs) of the protons. Next, Section \ref{subsec:pp_event} describes the processes that complete the event beyond the hard interaction: initial- and final-state radiation, hadronization, and the underlying event. Finally, Section \ref{subsec:pp_simulation} summarizes the Monte Carlo simulation chain that reproduces all these steps, from the matrix-element generators to the simulation of the detector response. These elements form the basis of the simulated samples used to model the signal and the backgrounds in Chapter \ref{ch:bbtt_analysis}, and their uncertainties reappear there as theoretical uncertainties of the search.

\subsection{Factorization and parton distribution functions}
\label{subsec:pp_factorization}
%
Since hard interactions occur between the partons that make up the proton, the energy available in each interaction is not fixed. Each parton carries a fraction $x$ of the momentum of the proton to which it belongs, so the center-of-mass energy of the partonic interaction, $\sqrt{\hat{s}}=\sqrt{x_1x_2\,s}$, is lower than the nominal collision energy, $\sqrt{s}$, and varies from event to event. The probability of finding a parton of type $i$ with a momentum fraction $x$ in the proton is given by its parton distribution function (PDF), $f_i(x)$, which therefore determines how often the energy required to produce a given final state is reached.


The factorization theorem~\cite{Collins:1989gx} allows the cross section of a process $pp\to X$ to be written as the sum, over all pairs of partons $i,j$ that can initiate it, of the partonic cross section weighted by the PDFs of the two partons:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{pp\to X} = \sum_{i,j}\int_0^1 dx_1\,dx_2\; f_i(x_1,\mu_F)\,f_j(x_2,\mu_F)\;\hat{\sigma}_{ij\to X}(x_1x_2\,s,\,\mu_R,\,\mu_F),
    \label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
where $f_i(x,\mu_F)$ are the PDFs and $\hat{\sigma}_{ij\to X}$ is the cross section for the interaction between the two partons. This separation is possible owing to the behavior of the strong interaction described in Section \ref{subsec:particle_content}. In hard interactions, which involve high energies, $\alpha_s$ is small, and $\hat{\sigma}_{ij\to X}$ can be calculated from the Feynman diagrams of the process as a power series in $\alpha_s$, whose first term defines the leading-order (LO) prediction and whose subsequent terms — NLO, NNLO — incorporate the emission of additional partons and loop corrections, which are sizable in gluon-initiated processes. At the energies characteristic of the structure of the proton, however, $\alpha_s$ is large and this series is no longer applicable, so the PDFs cannot be calculated in this way. The scales $\mu_F$ and $\mu_R$ in Equation \ref{eq:factorization} determine, respectively, the boundary between the two regimes and the energy at which $\alpha_s$ is evaluated.

The PDFs are therefore extracted from global fits to measurements of other processes — deep inelastic scattering, Drell–Yan production, jet production, or $t\bar{t}$ pair production — and, being universal, can then be used in any prediction~\cite{NNPDF:2017mvq}. Their dependence on the scale, in contrast, can be calculated~\cite{Gribov:1972ri,Dokshitzer:1977sg,Altarelli:1977zs}. Figure \ref{fig:pdfs} shows the PDFs from the NNPDF3.1 set at two energy scales, $Q^2=10$ and $10^4$ GeV$^2$, the latter being on the order of those involved in Higgs boson production. Valence quarks dominate at high values of $x$, while gluons dominate at low values, increasingly so as the scale grows. Since producing a pair of Higgs bosons at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV requires $\sqrt{\hat{s}}\geq2m_H\approx250$ GeV, that is, typical momentum fractions of the order of $x\sim10^{-2}$, the process takes place precisely in the region dominated by gluons, which helps explain the predominance of gluon fusion described in Section \ref{subsec:SM_production}.

When the partonic cross-section series is truncated at a given order, the cross section of Equation \ref{eq:factorization} depends slightly on the values chosen for $\mu_R$ and $\mu_F$, which are set to a scale characteristic of the process. The uncertainty due to the missing higher orders is therefore estimated by varying both scales by a factor of two~\cite{deFlorian:2016spz}. This is combined with the uncertainty of the PDFs themselves — the band in Figure \ref{fig:pdfs} — and that of the value of $\alpha_s$. These are the scale and PDF$+\alpha_s$ uncertainties quoted for the cross sections in Section \ref{subsec:SM_production}, and the same variations, applied to the simulated samples, constitute the theoretical uncertainties of the search, described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}.



\subsection{Parton showers, hadronization and underlying event}
\label{subsec:pp_event}
%
Equation \ref{eq:factorization} predicts how often the final state of the hard interaction is produced, but it does not describe the complete event recorded by the detector. The quarks and gluons involved in the interaction emit gluons, both before the interaction — initial-state radiation (ISR) — and after it — final-state radiation (FSR) — and the emitted gluons can in turn radiate further or split into quark-antiquark pairs. The first of these emissions are part of the higher-order corrections to the partonic cross section described in Section \ref{subsec:pp_factorization} — an NLO calculation includes one additional emission, and an NNLO calculation two — but the rest must be described approximately by a parton shower, an algorithm included in the event generators of Section \ref{subsec:pp_simulation} that generates successive emissions probabilistically, each at a lower energy scale than the previous one, from the scale of the hard interaction down to energies of the order of $1$ GeV, where $\alpha_s$ becomes large and the perturbative description is no longer valid~\cite{Buckley:2011ms}. This radiation gives rise to the additional jets that accompany the hard interaction and, by carrying away part of the momentum of the initial partons, causes the particles produced in it — for example, the $Z$ boson in Drell–Yan events — to acquire a nonzero $p_T$, whose modeling in the simulation is corrected in this search, as detailed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_corrections}.

Below the $1$ GeV scale, the resulting quarks and gluons are no longer able to propagate freely. Instead, confinement, described in Section \ref{subsec:particle_content}, forces them to combine into color-neutral hadrons, in a process called hadronization. Since this is a non-perturbative regime, hadronization is described by phenomenological models whose parameters are fitted to data. Only color-charged particles take part in this process: leptons and photons, which do not interact strongly, do not hadronize. After the decay of unstable hadrons, the combined result of the parton shower and hadronization is a set of jets: collimated sprays of particles whose direction and energy approximately match those of the original quark or gluon, and which are the objects reconstructed in the detector (Section \ref{subsec:Jets}).

Finally, the hard interaction involves only one parton from each proton, but the rest of the proton does not disappear. When a color-charged parton is removed, what remains of each proton — the beam remnants — is no longer color-neutral and, for the same reason, also hadronizes, giving rise to particles emitted mostly along the beam direction. In addition, other partons from the same two protons may interact with each other in the same collision, usually with a lower momentum transfer, in what are known as multiple parton interactions. Everything that arises from the same proton-proton collision but not from the hard interaction constitutes the \textit{underlying event}\footnote{The underlying event should not be confused with pile-up, which arises from collisions of other proton pairs in the same bunch crossing (Section \ref{subsec:LHC_design}).}, which adds low-energy particles throughout the event, superimposed on the products of the hard interaction, and affects, for example, the isolation of leptons or the energy of the jets.

None of these stages can be calculated with the precision of the hard interaction: the parton shower is an approximation, and hadronization and the underlying event depend on models whose parameters are fitted to data. Their modeling therefore constitutes a source of uncertainty in its own right. In this search, it is evaluated by varying the parameters that control initial- and final-state radiation, which, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}, mainly affects the number and energy of the additional jets. All of these stages are combined in the Monte Carlo event generators described below.


\subsection{Monte Carlo event simulation}
\label{subsec:pp_simulation}
%
The steps described in the two previous sections are reproduced in practice using Monte Carlo (MC) event generators, programs that produce simulated events one by one, choosing the outcome of each step at random — the initial partons and their momentum fractions, the configuration of the hard interaction, each emission of the parton shower — with the probability assigned to it by theory~\cite{Buckley:2011ms}. Each simulated event thus contains the complete list of particles in the final state, just like a real event, and the ensemble of many such events reproduces the predicted distributions, so that simulation and data can be analyzed in exactly the same way.

This task is typically divided between two types of programs. Matrix-element generators compute the partonic cross section of Equation \ref{eq:factorization} at LO or NLO and use it to generate the hard interaction; among the most commonly used in CMS are \MGaMC~\cite{Alwall:2014hca} and \POWHEG~\cite{powheg1,powheg2,powheg3}. The resulting events are then completed by a general-purpose generator, such as \PYTHIA~\cite{Bierlich:2022pfr}, which adds the parton shower, hadronization, and the underlying event, and simulates the decay of unstable particles. Since the matrix element and the parton shower can generate the same emissions, the two must be combined so that no emission is counted twice. When the starting point is an NLO calculation, which already includes the first additional emission, this combination is called \textit{matching}~\cite{powheg2,Alwall:2014hca}. When several samples with different numbers of additional partons in the matrix element are combined — as in the simulation of Drell–Yan production in association with jets — it is called \textit{merging}, and relies on a scale that determines which emissions are described by the matrix element and which by the parton shower~\cite{Frederix:2012ps}. The parameters of the parton shower, hadronization, and the underlying event that are fitted to data are grouped into sets called \textit{tunes}: the CMS simulations for Run II and Run III use the CP5 tune of \PYTHIA~\cite{CMS:2019csb}, derived with the PDFs shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdfs}.

Finally, the generated particles are propagated through a detailed model of the detector using \GEANT~\cite{GEANT4:2002zbu}, which simulates their interactions with the various subdetectors and the signals these interactions produce. Additional interactions are superimposed on these events to reproduce the pile-up conditions, and the resulting events are reconstructed with the same algorithms as the data; both the detector and these algorithms are described in the following chapter.

Since each simulated event preserves the complete history of its generation, the parton or decay from which each particle originates is known — key information for measuring efficiencies, defining which events are signal, and training identification algorithms such as those of Chapter \ref{ch:taggers}. However, none of the stages of the simulation is perfect, and the remaining differences with respect to data must be corrected and covered by uncertainties, as is done in this search in Sections \ref{sec:bbtt_corrections} and \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}.\bigskip

\noindent This chapter has presented the theoretical framework in which this thesis is set: the SM and the BEH mechanism, which predicts the self-interaction of the Higgs boson; Higgs boson pair production, the most direct way to measure it and a probe of physics beyond the SM; the current status of its search at the LHC; and the elements used to build predictions for proton-proton collisions. All of this also determines the experimental requirements of the search. On the one hand, with a cross section of about $34$ fb at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV, approximately one Higgs boson pair is produced for every $10^{12}$ inelastic collisions, so the highest attainable energy and luminosity are required. On the other hand, the $bb\tau\tau$ final state requires identifying jets originating from $b$ quarks, reconstructing $\tau$ leptons in their various decay modes, and inferring the accompanying neutrinos from $p_T^\text{miss}$, all in an environment with dozens of simultaneous collisions. The following chapter describes the LHC and the CMS detector, which respectively provide and record these collisions, as well as the algorithms used to reconstruct the objects of this final state.
