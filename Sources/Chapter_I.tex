\chapter{Theoretical context}
\label{ch:theory}
\noindent The Standard Model (SM) of particle physics~\cite{Herrero:1998eq,Thomson:2013zua} is a renormalizable quantum field theory that describes the elementary particles and their fundamental interactions. Formulated in the early 1970s as a consolidation of theories and experimental discoveries from the previous decade, SM predictions have been well corroborated by numerous observations in collider experiments. The discovery of the Higgs boson is the final entry on this extensive checklist, theorized as a direct consequence of the Brout-Englert-Higgs (BEH) mechanism, which is responsible for the spontaneous electroweak symmetry breaking (EWSB).

Nevertheless, despite the excellent agreement between its predictions and the experimental measurements, a large body of evidence ranging from subnuclear to astrophysical phenomena suggests that its current formulation is incomplete. This makes a more comprehensive theory necessary to provide a complete picture. Exploring the scalar sector is a promising way to research physics Beyond the SM (BSM), where the connection between the Higgs boson self-interaction and the nature of the Higgs potential plays a fundamental role. Therefore, measuring the Higgs boson self-coupling constant, which is currently unconstrained by observation, could offer invaluable insights.

The present chapter establishes the theoretical basis of this work and motivates the study of Higgs boson pair production in this context. First, Section \ref{sec:SM} provides a concise overview of the SM, from its particle content and fundamental interactions to the BEH mechanism, the phenomenology of the Higgs boson, and the limitations that point to physics beyond it. Next, Section \ref{sec:HH_production} focuses on Higgs boson pair production, first within the SM and then in the presence of new physics, introducing the coupling modifiers and effective field theories in which its interpretation is framed. Section \ref{sec:HH_searches} then reviews the decay channels accessible at the LHC and the current status of the experimental searches, which sets the context for the contributions of this thesis. Finally, Section \ref{sec:pp_pheno} describes the phenomenology of proton-proton collisions on which the prediction of the signal and the backgrounds relies.


\section{Structure of the Standard Model of particle physics}
\label{sec:SM}
%
The SM was initially proposed by S. Glashow, A. Salam, and S. Weinberg~\cite{Glashow:1961tr,Salam:1968rm,Weinberg:1967tq} and has been demonstrated to be the most compelling theoretical framework for synthesizing fundamental constituents and their interactions. It is a renormalizable quantum field theory (QFT) based on quantum mechanics and special relativity that satisfies gauge invariance. According to this framework, elementary particles are defined as indivisible entities devoid of internal structure, and correspond to excited states of underlying quantum field, which are mathematically described by QFT.

The SM also encompasses three of the four fundamental interactions. These are the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions, each connected to different intrinsic charges of the matter particles: the electromagnetic charge, the weak isospin, and the color charge. These interactions are described by the exchange of particle mediators, which arise as a consequence of local gauge invariance. The description of the gravitational force is not included in the SM, which is a limitation of the model. However, it can be disregarded at the particle level scale since it is a force with such a weak intensity that its effect has practically no influence compared to the other forces.

The mathematical formulation of the SM is based on a Lagrangian density, $\mathcal{L}$, which condenses all the necessary information to describe the propagation and interactions of the particle fields. The Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ is defined as a scalar function of the fields $\phi_i$, their derivatives $\partial_\mu \phi_i$, and the spacetime coordinates, $x^\mu$, and its invariance under a specific set of symmetry transformations constitutes the fundamental principle on which the SM is built. According to the Noether theorem~\cite{Noether:1918zz}, each continuous global symmetry of a physical system leads to a conserved quantity. These quantities are the intrinsic charges of the matter particles associated with each interaction, and they manifest as a consequence of the symmetry of the SM Lagrangian under the continuous group $SU(3)_C \times SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$. Furthermore, as a direct consequence of local gauge symmetry, the introduction of vector fields is imperative, and their quantum excitations result in the particles that mediate the interactions: the strong interaction, invariant under $SU(3)_C$ and described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD); and the electroweak (EWK) force, unifying the $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ symmetries.


\subsection{Particle content}
%
According to the SM, the elementary particles can be classified depending on their spin. Two types of particles are distinguished: fermions with spin 1/2 and bosons with integer spin — 0 or 1. The former arise from Lorentz spinor fields and constitute all known matter. In regard to the latter, a distinction is made between gauge bosons, corresponding to spin 1, which are associated with the vector fields and responsible for mediating interactions between fermions; and the Higgs boson, with spin 0, linked to a scalar field and responsible for the mechanism by which fermions and bosons acquire their mass. The particle content and their properties are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:SM_particles}, and discussed in detail next.

\subsubsection{Matter particles: Fermions}
%
Due to the relativistic nature of the SM, quantum excitations of spinorial fields that transform in the spin-$1/2$ representation of the Lorentz group give rise to half-integer spin particles called fermions. Experimental observations have demonstrated the existence of twelve fermions: six quark fields and six lepton fields, which will be detailed below. The 12 fermions are arranged into three generations of mass, with each generation consisting of two quarks with electric charges of $Q=+2/3$ and $Q=-1/3$ and two leptons with charges of $Q=-1$ and $Q=0$. The difference in mass between the families is related to their coupling strength with scalar fields. The first family, being the one with the lowest mass, constitutes stable matter, while the particles of the other families are predominantly unstable and decay rapidly. Each fermion has a corresponding antiparticle with the same spacetime characteristics — mass and spin — but opposite intrinsic quantum numbers — charges —, meaning when a particle and its antiparticle interact, they annihilate each other.


\paragraph{Quarks}
%
Each generation contains a quark doublet composed of an up-type quark, with $Q=+2/3$ — up ($u$), charm ($c$) and top ($t$) —, and a down-type quark, with $Q=-1/3$ — down ($d$), strange ($s$) and bottom ($b$). The first-generation quarks are the lightest, with masses of $m_u = 2.16 \pm 0.07$ MeV and $m_d = 4.70 \pm 0.07$ MeV. Their counterparts in the second generation reach masses of $m_s = 92.9 \pm 0.7$ MeV and $m_c = 1.2729 \pm 0.0045$ GeV, while the third generation comprises the bottom quark, with $m_b = 4.186 \pm 0.006$ GeV, and the top quark, the heaviest known elementary particle, with $m_t = 172.60 \pm 0.27$ GeV~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026mpi}. 

Of all the particle content of the SM, only quarks are subject to all three forces. They are the only fermions that carry color charge — connected to the strong force — in addition to their electric charge. The strong interaction, as described by quantum chromodynamics, leads to the phenomena known as QCD color confinement, thereby implying that quarks cannot exist as a free states, as observed for leptons, but can only be detected as bound states, called \textit{hadrons}. Only color-neutral hadrons exist in nature, so the only possible states are mesons, composed of a quark-antiquark pair, and baryons, formed by three quarks. The top quark, however, is an exception, since its very high mass and therefore its high instability cause it to decay via electroweak interaction before it can form a bound state. At very high energies, the strong force weakens, causing quarks to behave like asymptotically free particles and allowing their interactions to be studied in proton colliders like the LHC.

\paragraph{Leptons}
The other variety of fermions are known as leptons, which, devoid of color charge, interact solely through the weak and electromagnetic forces. Each family of lepton doublets consists of a charged lepton with electric charge $Q=1$: electron ($e$), muon ($\mu$) and tau ($\tau$); and the corresponding neutrino ($\nu$) with $Q=0$.

The three charged leptons have masses that span more than three orders of magnitude, $m_e = 0.511$ MeV, $m_\mu = 105.66$ MeV and $m_\tau = 1776.93 \pm 0.09$ MeV, the first two being known with a relative precision better than $10^{-7}$~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026mpi}. As a consequence, the electron is the only stable charged lepton, while the muon has a mean lifetime of $2.197$ $\mu$s and the tau of $(290.3 \pm 0.5) \times 10^{-15}$ s. In collider experiments, muons are produced with a Lorentz boost large enough to cross the whole detector before decaying, so that they behave as stable particles, whereas the tau decays before reaching the first layer of the tracker and can only be inferred from its decay products.

Neutral leptons, neutrinos, share the same lepton flavor as their corresponding charged leptons, finding $\nu_e$,$\nu_{\mu}$ and $\nu_{\tau}$. Because they are electrically neutral, neutrinos only interact with matter through the weak force, which makes direct detection in experiments unfeasible. Their indirect signature is revealed by the missing energy in the final state. In the classical formulation of the SM, the neutrino is assumed to be massless. This assumption, however, is contradicted by the observation of neutrino oscillations, first established with atmospheric neutrinos by Super-Kamiokande in 1998~\cite{Super-Kamiokande:1998kpq} and confirmed with solar neutrinos by SNO~\cite{SNO:2002tuh}, implying that neutrinos are massive particles, with masses below $0.45$ eV according to the direct measurement from tritium $\beta$ decay~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026mpi}. In order to account for the phenomenon of neutrino oscillations, the interplay between flavor and mass eigenstates is depicted by the Pontecorvo-Maki-Nakagawa-Sakata (PMNS) matrix, introduced as a revision to the SM. 

\subsubsection{Bosons}

\paragraph{Force-carrying particles: Gauge Bosons}
%
Gauge bosons are spin-1 particles responsible for mediating the fundamental interactions of the SM. They are the excitations of each gauge field associated with local symmetries, so the number of bosons mediator each force depends on the dimension of the gauge group, and their properties arise from the corresponding interaction's Lagrangian.

The symmetry gauge group $SU(3)_C$ is associated with the strong interaction and involves eight generators known as gluons (g). Gluons are massless and electrically neutral particles that carry color charge and therefore interact with other colored particles, meaning themselves and quarks. As a result of being colored particles, gluons cannot be directly observed as free particles, yet their existence is inferred from the hadronic jets produced by gluon emission, with the firs experimental evidence appearing in 1979 {\color{red} [ref]}.

The electroweak sector unified in the symmetry $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ consists of four generators. The photon ($\gamma$), a neutral, massless particle, is associated with the electromagnetic force. The other three generators are the two $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons, which are responsible for the charged and neutral currents of the weak force, respectively. The $W$ and $Z$ bosons were directly observed in 1983, and the two charged $W$ bosons with opposite charges $Q=\pm1$ have identical masses of 80.3 GeV while the mass of the neutral $Z$ boson is 91.2 GeV.

\paragraph{Scalar Boson: Higgs Boson}
%
To endow the $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons with mass, the $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ must be broken. This symmetry breaking process is realized through the Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism, which introduces a scalar field that preserves renormalizability and gauge invariance within the SM. The quantum excitation of this field is the Higgs boson, the only fundamental non-gauge spin-0 or scalar boson in the SM. It couples directly to all other particles, except for photons and gluons, imparting mass to them, as has been proven experimentally. Its description and properties are intimately linked to the EWSB mechanism, detailed in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}.

% The Higgs boson is unique among the other particles because it is the only non-gauge spin-0 or scalar boson in the SM. It emerged as a result of the Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism, which was developed to explain bosons with non-zero mass, contrary to previous theoretical predictions. Likewise, the Higgs boson interacts directly with the other particles, except for photons and gluons, imparting mass to them, as has been proven experimentally. Its description and properties are intimately linked to the EWSB mechanism, detailed in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}.

\subsection{Fundamental interactions}
%
\subsubsection{Strong interaction}
%
The strong interaction is described by QCD, a gauge theory based on the non-abelian group $SU(3)_C$, where the subscript $C$ refers to color, the charge conserved by the symmetry. Since only quarks carry color, only the quark fields transform non-trivially under this group, and the construction of the theory starts from the Lagrangian density of a free fermion field, the Dirac Lagrangian,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dirac}} = \bar{\psi}(x)\,(i\slashed{\partial} - m)\,\psi(x),
    \label{eq:dirac_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
%
where $\psi(x)$ is the quark spinor field at the spacetime point $x$, $m$ is the quark mass, and $\slashed{\partial} = \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu$ denotes the contraction of the Dirac matrices $\gamma^\mu$ with the spacetime derivatives. This Lagrangian is invariant under global $SU(3)_C$ transformations, but not under local ones, in which the quark field transforms as
\begin{equation}
    \psi(x) \rightarrow \exp\!\left(i g_s\, \theta^a(x)\, \frac{\lambda_a}{2}\right) \psi(x),
    \label{lagrangian_transformation}
\end{equation}
%
with $\lambda_a$ the eight Gell-Mann matrices that generate the group, $\theta^a(x)$ arbitrary real functions of the spacetime point, and $g_s$ the strong coupling constant. Because the transformation depends on $x$, the derivative of the transformed field acquires an additional term, and invariance can only be restored by replacing $\partial_\mu$ with the covariant derivative
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i g_s\, \frac{\lambda_a}{2}\, G^a_\mu,
    \label{covariant_derivative}
\end{equation}
%
which introduces eight vector fields $G^a_\mu$, the gluon fields, whose transformation compensates that additional term so that $D_\mu\psi$ transforms as $\psi$ itself. Since it is precisely this dependence on $x$ that distinguishes local from global transformations, it is omitted hereafter. In order to propagate, the gluons in turn require a kinetic term, which is built from the gluon field-strength tensor
\begin{equation}
    G^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu G^a_\nu - \partial_\nu G^a_\mu - g_s f^{abc}\, G^b_\mu G^c_\nu,
    \label{gluon_field}
\end{equation}
%
where $f^{abc}$ are the structure constants of $SU(3)_C$. Combining these ingredients, the QCD Lagrangian reads
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{QCD}} = \bar{\psi}\,(i\slashed{\partial} - m)\,\psi - g_s\, \bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu \frac{\lambda_a}{2}\psi\, G^a_\mu - \frac{1}{4}\, G^a_{\mu\nu} G_a^{\mu\nu}.
    \label{QCD_Lagrangian}
\end{equation}
%
The first term is the Dirac Lagrangian of Equation \ref{eq:dirac_lagrangian}, which describes the free propagation of the quarks. The second term arises from the covariant derivative and describes the interaction between quarks and gluons, with a strength set by $g_s$ — usually expressed through $\alpha_s = g_s^2/4\pi$ —, while the third is the kinetic term of the gluons, which, owing to the last term of Equation \ref{gluon_field}, also contains cubic and quartic gluon self-interactions, a direct consequence of the non-abelian nature of $SU(3)_C$. The requirement of local gauge invariance therefore generates the eight gluons, which differ only in the color charge they carry, and fixes their interactions with the quarks and among themselves. In contrast, a gluon mass term, of the form $m_g^2\, G^a_\mu G_a^\mu$, would break this invariance, so that gluons are necessarily massless.

\subsubsection{Electroweak interaction}
%
The electroweak (EWK) theory provides a unified description of the electromagnetic and weak interactions, obtained by imposing local gauge invariance under the group $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$. Its structure is more complex than that of QCD because the weak interaction differs substantially from the other forces: it couples universally to all fermions, connects different quark flavors through the Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa (CKM) mixing matrix, and is the only interaction observed to violate charge (C), parity (P), and combined charge-parity (CP) symmetries. The violation of parity {\color{red} [ref]} reflects the fact that fermions couple differently depending on their \textit{chirality}, a Lorentz-invariant property defined through the eigenvalues of $\gamma^5 = i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$, from which the left- and right-chirality projection operators, $(1-\gamma^5)/2$ and $(1+\gamma^5)/2$, are constructed. In the limit of a massless particle, chirality coincides with helicity, the normalized projection of the spin onto the direction of motion.

The weak interaction affects only left-handed fermions — and right-handed antifermions —, as indicated by the subscript $L$ of $SU(2)_L$. Accordingly, the fermion fields of each generation are arranged into left-handed doublets, which transform under $SU(2)_L$, and right-handed singlets, which do not — two for quarks, and only one for leptons, since the SM contains no right-handed neutrino:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_L \equiv \frac{1 - \gamma^5}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\psi \\ \psi'\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_L \\ \psi'_L\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
    \psi_R \equiv \frac{1 + \gamma^5}{2}\,\psi, \qquad
    \psi'_R \equiv \frac{1 + \gamma^5}{2}\,\psi',
    \label{fermionic_fields}
\end{equation}
%
where $\psi$ and $\psi'$ denote the two members of a doublet: the neutrino and the charged lepton, or the up- and down-type quarks. Leptons and quarks thus belong to separate doublets, and since no interaction of the SM connects them, a quark can never be transformed into a lepton or vice versa. The charge associated with $SU(2)_L$ is the weak isospin $T_3$, which takes the values $T_3=+1/2$ for the upper and $T_3=-1/2$ for the lower component of the doublets, and $T_3=0$ for the right-handed singlets. The charge of $U(1)_Y$ is the weak hypercharge $Y$, which is related to the electric charge through the Gell-Mann-Nishijima relation, $Q = T_3 + Y/2$. A right-handed neutrino — and a left-handed antineutrino — would therefore carry no electric charge, no weak isospin, no hypercharge and no color, and would be sterile with respect to all the interactions of the SM.

As in QCD, local invariance under $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ requires replacing the derivative with a covariant derivative, which now contains one gauge field for each generator of the group — three fields $W^i_\mu$, with $i=1,2,3$, for $SU(2)_L$, and one field $B_\mu$ for $U(1)_Y$:
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i g\, T_i\, W^i_\mu + i g'\, \frac{Y}{2}\, B_\mu,
    \label{eq:ew_covariant_derivative}
\end{equation}
%
where $g$ and $g'$ are the coupling constants of $SU(2)_L$ and $U(1)_Y$, and $T_i = \sigma_i/2$, with $\sigma_i$ the Pauli matrices, for the left-handed doublets, while $T_i = 0$ for the right-handed singlets, which therefore couple only to $B_\mu$. The kinetic terms of the gauge fields are built, as in QCD, from the field-strength tensors
\begin{equation}
    W^i_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu W^i_\nu - \partial_\nu W^i_\mu - g\, \epsilon^{ijk}\, W^j_\mu W^k_\nu, \qquad
    B_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu B_\nu - \partial_\nu B_\mu,
    \label{eq:ew_field_strengths}
\end{equation}
%
where the Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon^{ijk}$ provides the structure constants of $SU(2)_L$. Only the tensor of the non-abelian group $SU(2)_L$ contains a quadratic term, which gives rise to cubic and quartic self-interactions among the $W^i_\mu$ fields. The EWK Lagrangian finally reads
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{EWK}} = {}& \bar{\Psi}_L\, i \slashed{D}\, \Psi_L + \bar{\psi}_R\, i \slashed{D}\, \psi_R + \bar{\psi}'_R\, i \slashed{D}\, \psi'_R \\
    & - \frac{1}{4}\, W^i_{\mu\nu} W_i^{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{4}\, B_{\mu\nu} B^{\mu\nu},
    \end{split}
    \label{EW_Lagrangian}
\end{equation}
%
where the covariant derivative acts on each field with its corresponding generators and hypercharge. Once written in terms of the physical fields, this Lagrangian can be decomposed into four contributions, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWK}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{free}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{CC}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{NC}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{kin}}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The free term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{free}}$ describes the propagation of the fermion fields in the absence of interactions.
    \item The charged-current term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CC}}$ couples the two components of the left-handed doublets, $\psi_L$ and $\psi'_L$. It is mediated by the combinations of the first two $SU(2)_L$ gauge fields $W^{\pm}_{\mu} = (W^{1}_{\mu} \mp i\, W^{2}_{\mu})/\sqrt{2}$, which correspond to the charged $W^\pm$ bosons.
    \item The neutral-current term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{NC}}$ is mediated by two orthogonal combinations of the neutral fields $W^3_\mu$ and $B_\mu$, the $Z$ boson field, $Z_\mu$, and the photon field, $A_\mu$:
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{pmatrix} A_\mu \\ Z_\mu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta_W & \sin\theta_W \\ -\sin\theta_W & \cos\theta_W \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} B_\mu \\ W^3_\mu \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tan\theta_W = \frac{g'}{g},
        \label{eq:weinberg_mixing}
    \end{equation}
    %
    where $\theta_W$ is the Weinberg angle, a free parameter of the SM. The photon couples to the electric charge $Q$ with a strength $e = g\sin\theta_W = g'\cos\theta_W$, the unit of electric charge, so that this term contains quantum electrodynamics (QED), the theory of the electromagnetic interaction, invariant under the abelian group $U(1)_{\text{em}}$. Unlike gluons, photons do not interact with each other, as a consequence of the abelian nature of this group.
    \item The kinetic term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{kin}}$ of the gauge fields, built from the field-strength tensors of Equation \ref{eq:ew_field_strengths}, gives rise to trilinear and quadrilinear self-couplings among the $W^\pm$, $Z$ and $\gamma$ bosons.
\end{itemize}

Up to this point, however, all the particles of the theory are massless. A mass term for the gauge bosons, of the form $m^2 W^i_\mu W_i^\mu$, would break gauge invariance, as in the case of the gluons, and a fermion mass term, $m\bar{\psi}\psi = m(\bar{\psi}_R\psi_L+\bar{\psi}_L\psi_R)$, mixes the two chiralities, which transform differently under $SU(2)_L$, so that it cannot be invariant either. Local gauge invariance is therefore only preserved if all the masses vanish, in stark contradiction with the observed masses of the $W^\pm$ and $Z$ bosons and of the fermions — only the photon and the gluons are massless. In order to account for this evidence while maintaining gauge invariance, the mechanism proposed in 1964 by R. Brout, F. Englert, and P. Higgs is relied on, as it explains the origin of the masses through the spontaneous breaking of the electroweak symmetry.


\subsection{The Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism}
\label{subsec:BEH}
%
Given that the EWK theory as it stands fails to account for the masses of the electroweak bosons, additional mass terms must be incorporated into the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWK}}$ without breaking its gauge invariance. These terms arise from the so-called electroweak symmetry breaking (EWSB), a spontaneous symmetry breaking, which occurs when the potential of a system is invariant under a set of symmetry transformations, but its individual ground states are not.

In the SM, spontaneous symmetry breaking takes place through the Brout-Englert-Higgs (BEH) mechanism {\color{red} [ref]}, which explains how the masses of the gauge bosons and fermions are generated upon the breaking of the electroweak symmetry, $SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$, at low energies. According to the Goldstone theorem {\color{red} [ref]}, a massless spin-0 boson, the Goldstone boson, appears for each generator of a continuous global symmetry that is spontaneously broken. In a theory with a local gauge symmetry, however, the Goldstone bosons are absorbed by the gauge bosons, which thereby become massive — the essence of the Higgs mechanism. The breaking of the EWK symmetry is therefore based on the introduction of a complex scalar $SU(2)_L$ doublet with hypercharge $Y_\phi=1$, the Higgs field,
\begin{equation}
    \phi = \begin{pmatrix} \phi^{+} \\ \phi^{0} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} \phi_{1} + i\,\phi_{2} \\ \phi_{3} + i\,\phi_{4} \end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:higgs_doublet}
\end{equation}
%
where $\phi^+$ is a charged field with $T_3=+1/2$ and $\phi^0$ a neutral field with $T_3=-1/2$. The field must be a scalar in order to satisfy spatial isotropy, since otherwise its expectation value in the vacuum would be frame-dependent, and this value must be constant to satisfy homogeneity. With the covariant derivative of Equation \ref{eq:ew_covariant_derivative}, the BEH Lagrangian reads
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{BEH}} = (D_\mu \phi)^\dagger (D^\mu \phi) - V(\phi), \qquad V(\phi) = -\mu^2\,\phi^\dagger \phi + \lambda\,(\phi^\dagger \phi)^2,
    \label{higgs_potential}
\end{equation}
%
where the scalar potential $V(\phi)$ is chosen with a form that induces spontaneous symmetry breaking, and $\mu^2$ and $\lambda$ are constants of the model. The values these parameters can take are restricted by the requirement that the potential be bounded from below, which demands $\lambda>0$. If $\mu^2<0$, the potential has a single minimum at the origin, $\phi=0$, which is symmetric under the transformations of the group, so that the symmetry is exact. Conversely, if $\mu^2>0$, the potential adopts the \textit{Mexican hat} shape illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:higgs_potential}: the origin becomes an unstable local maximum, while the minimum is found for the field configurations that satisfy $\phi^\dagger \phi = \mu^2/2\lambda \equiv v^2/2$, where $v$ is the vacuum expectation value (VEV).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/chapter_I/higgs-champ.png}
    \caption{Shape of the Higgs potential of Equation \ref{higgs_potential} for $\mu^2>0$: the origin becomes an unstable local maximum, and the minimum, located at $|\phi| = v/\sqrt{2}$, is infinitely degenerate.}
    \label{fig:higgs_potential}
\end{figure}

Since the Lagrangian is invariant under $SU(2)_L$ and $U(1)_Y$ phase transformations, this minimum is infinitely degenerate: all the configurations $\phi_0 = (v/\sqrt{2})\,e^{i\theta}$, which form a circle around the origin, have the same energy, $V(\phi_0) = -\lambda v^4/4$, and a phase transformation turns any of them into another. This degeneracy is the origin of the symmetry breaking, since the system must select one of these states as its ground state, which, without loss of generality, can be chosen as
\begin{equation}
    \phi_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:higgs_vev}
\end{equation}
%
This choice breaks both $SU(2)_L$ and $U(1)_Y$, but preserves the combination generated by the electric charge, since $Q\phi_0 = 0$, so that the symmetry is broken as $SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y\rightarrow U(1)_{\text{em}}$. Three of the four generators are thus broken spontaneously, yielding three massless Goldstone bosons, in accordance with the Goldstone theorem. The particle spectrum is obtained by expanding the field around this ground state,
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = \exp\!\left( \frac{i\,\sigma_i\,\theta^i(x)}{2v} \right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v + H(x) \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
%
where $H(x)$ and the three $\theta^i(x)$ are four real fields that represent small perturbations. The fields $\theta^i(x)$ correspond to the Goldstone bosons, which are not observed in nature, since the freedom to perform local $SU(2)_L$ transformations can be used to remove them entirely from the Lagrangian. In this so-called unitary gauge, the Higgs field reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v + H(x) \end{pmatrix},
    \label{phi_parametrization}
\end{equation}
%
and the only remaining scalar is the real field $H(x)$, associated with a massive particle, the Higgs boson, which completes the SM. Substituting Equation \ref{phi_parametrization} into Equation \ref{higgs_potential}, and rewriting the gauge fields in terms of the physical fields introduced above, the BEH Lagrangian takes the form
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{BEH}} = \frac{1}{2}\,\partial_\mu H\,\partial^\mu H
    + \left(m_W^2\, W^+_\mu W^{-\mu} + \frac{1}{2}\, m_Z^2\, Z_\mu Z^\mu\right)\left(1+\frac{H}{v}\right)^{2} - V(H),
    \label{eq:beh}
\end{equation}
%
where the degrees of freedom of the three Goldstone bosons have become the longitudinal polarizations of the $W^\pm$ and $Z$ bosons, which thereby acquire the masses
\begin{equation}
    m_W = \frac{g\,v}{2}, \qquad m_Z = \frac{\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}}{2}\,v = \frac{m_W}{\cos\theta_W}.
    \label{eq:boson_masses}
\end{equation}
%
The absence of any term involving the photon field reflects the fact that $U(1)_{\text{em}}$ remains unbroken, and confirms that the photon is massless. Expanding the factor $(1+H/v)^2$, Equation \ref{eq:beh} also yields the trilinear ($HVV$) and quadrilinear ($HHVV$) interactions between the Higgs boson and the vector bosons, $V=W^\pm, Z$, with strengths proportional to $m_V^2$. The scalar potential, in turn, becomes
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
    V(H) = \frac{1}{2}\, m_H^2 H^2 + \lambda_{HHH}\, v\, H^3 + \frac{1}{4}\, \lambda_{HHHH}\, H^4, \\
    m_H = \sqrt{2\lambda}\,v = \sqrt{2}\,\mu, \qquad \lambda_{HHH} = \lambda_{HHHH} = \lambda = \frac{m_H^2}{2v^2},
    \end{gathered}
    \label{higgs_pot}
\end{equation}
%
which contains the mass term of the Higgs boson and its trilinear and quartic self-interactions. Since $\lambda$ is a free parameter of the theory, the mass of the Higgs boson is not predicted by the SM, but once it is measured, the self-couplings are entirely determined by $m_H$ and $v$. The presence of these self-interaction terms implies that the production of pairs and triplets of Higgs bosons is predicted by the SM, at rates directly related to the parameters of the scalar potential, so that measuring double Higgs boson production represents a crucial test of the validity of the SM and of the EWSB mechanism. The constant term $V(\phi_0) = -\lambda v^4/4$, omitted in Equation \ref{higgs_pot}, does not affect the dynamics of the SM, but contributes to the vacuum energy, which is associated with the cosmological constant that determines the curvature of the Universe. The value predicted by the SM for this constant is incompatible with astronomical observations, thus constituting a dilemma that necessitates a proper quantum theory of gravity or a mechanism that reduces the vacuum energy density of the Higgs field.

At this stage, the free parameters of the BEH mechanism are the VEV, $v$, and the mass of the Higgs boson, $m_H$. The former sets the energy scale of the electroweak symmetry breaking, and is determined from the relation between the mass of the $W^{\pm}$ boson and the Fermi constant, $G_F$, which is precisely measured from the muon lifetime:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{G_F}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{g^2}{8\,m_W^2} = \frac{1}{2v^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \left(\sqrt{2}\,G_F\right)^{-1/2} \approx 246\ \text{GeV}.
\end{equation}
%
Thus far, the mechanism that generates the masses of the vector bosons and of the Higgs boson itself has been described, while the fermions have remained massless. Their masses arise from an additional term in the SM Lagrangian, the Yukawa interaction between the Higgs field and the fermions, which couples the left-handed doublets to the right-handed singlets of Equation \ref{fermionic_fields}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Yukawa}} = -y_{\psi'}\, \bar{\Psi}_L\, \phi\, \psi'_R - y_{\psi}\, \bar{\Psi}_L\, \tilde{\phi}\, \psi_R + \text{h.c.},
    \label{yukawa_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
%
where $y_{\psi}$ and $y_{\psi'}$ are the Yukawa couplings and h.c. denotes the hermitian conjugate terms. The lower component of the doublet, with $T_3=-1/2$, couples to the Higgs field $\phi$, whose VEV lies in its lower component, while the upper component, with $T_3=+1/2$, couples to the conjugate field $\tilde{\phi} = i\sigma_2\phi^*$, which after symmetry breaking takes the form
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\phi}(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} v + H(x) \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{phi_conjugated}
\end{equation}
%
Introducing Equations \ref{phi_parametrization} and \ref{phi_conjugated}, the Yukawa Lagrangian can be written more conveniently as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Yukawa}} = -\sum_f m_f \left( \bar{\psi}_{f,L}\, \psi_{f,R} + \bar{\psi}_{f,R}\, \psi_{f,L} \right)\left( 1 + \frac{H}{v} \right), \qquad m_f = \frac{y_f\, v}{\sqrt{2}},
    \label{eq:yukawa_masses}
\end{equation}
%
where the sum runs over all the fermions except the neutrinos. The fermion mass terms thus appear explicitly, each proportional to the Yukawa coupling of the fermion and to the VEV, together with an interaction with the Higgs boson whose strength is in turn proportional to the fermion mass. Since the SM contains no right-handed neutrinos, neutrinos do not acquire mass through this mechanism.

In essence, the BEH mechanism resolves the inconsistency of the electroweak theory by introducing a scalar field with a non-zero VEV, thereby providing bosons and fermions with mass. Following the symmetry breaking, three of the four degrees of freedom of the scalar doublet are absorbed by the $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons as their longitudinal polarizations, while the fourth gives rise to the Higgs boson, which also couples to the fermions through the Yukawa interaction. These interactions are governed by the gauge and Yukawa couplings, together with the VEV, which constitute free parameters of the theory and establish, after EWSB, the masses of all the SM particles. As a result, the coupling of the Higgs boson to each particle is proportional to its mass — linearly for fermions and quadratically for vector bosons —, so that the Higgs boson decays preferentially into the heaviest particles that are kinematically accessible, as detailed in the following section.

\subsection{Phenomenology of the Higgs boson}
\label{subsec:H_phenomenology}
%
Despite the theoretical robustness of the proposed BEH mechanism in 1964, experimental confirmation did not occur until July 4, 2012, when the CMS and ATLAS collaborations at the LHC claimed the discovery of a new particle compatible with the Higgs boson predicted by the SM {\color{red} [ref]}. The discovery was accomplished independently by the two experiments utilizing data collected during LHC Run I at center-of-mass energies of $\sqrt{s}=7$ and $\sqrt{s}=8$ TeV. The experimental observation of the Higgs boson led to the confirmation of the BEH mechanism as a part of the SM, and the measurement of its mass, with a value of approximately $m_H=125$ GeV, allowed the final free parameter of the theory to be determined.

In the wake of the recent discovery, the scientific community is now directing its efforts toward characterizing the new particle, with the aim of furthering the exploration of the EWSB. A thorough investigation is necessary to make precise measurements of the Higgs properties: measurements of the Higgs couplings via exclusive production modes and decay channels, its spin-parity, and its differential production cross section must be performed and contrasted with the predictions of the SM. The following paragraphs provide an overview of the results of more than a dozen years of research at the LHC on the characterization of the Higgs boson phenomenology.

Several mechanisms contribute to the production of the Higgs boson. The dominant mechanism at the LHC ($\sqrt{s} = 13$ TeV) is gluon-gluon fusion (ggF), which proceeds through a heavy quark loop and has a cross section of about 49 pb. This is followed by vector boson fusion (VBF) production, which has a cross section of 3.8 pb, about 10 times smaller. In the VBF mechanism, two vector bosons interact to produce a Higgs boson, which in the final state appears alongside two high-momentum quarks, very close to the direction of the incident particles — the experimental signature that characterizes the process. The next mechanism is associated production with a single vector boson ($VH, V=W^{\pm},Z$), with a cross section of 2.3 pb. The study of the rare VH and VBF production mechanisms allows for a deeper dive into the Higgs boson's coupling to vector bosons. Lastly, there is associated production with heavy quarks: top pair ($t\overline{t}H$), bottom pair ($b\overline{b}H$, or single top ($tH$), which benefits from the Higgs boson's enhanced coupling with the heaviest fermions. The cross sections for these latter processes are around one order of magnitude smaller than the previous mechanisms. The $t\overline{t}H$ mechanism is of particular interest because it enables the direct determination of the top Yukawa coupling, $y_t$, as opposed to the indirect determination from ggF.

\noindent \textbf{Decay modes}

\noindent The observed mass value of the Higgs boson is a genuine natural phenomenon: it lies at the intersection of all significant decay modes, preventing any single mode from dominating and enabling an exceptionally rich range of experimental channels. Thus, at a mass of 125 GeV, the Higgs boson decays into a variety of SM particles. The probability of its decay into each final state is described by the branching fractions, or branching ratios (BR), which are shown as a function of Higgs boson mass in the Figure \ref{fig:H_br}. The SM Higgs boson couples to vector bosons ($V$) with a strength proportional to the square of their mass, $m_V^2$, and to fermions ($f$) with a strength proportional to their mass, $m_f$, as summarized in Table \ref{tab:higgs_couplings}, so that its couplings are enhanced for heavy particles. As presented in Figure \ref{fig:H_br}, the decay probabilities indicate that for a Higgs boson with a mass of 125 GeV, the most probable decay modes are $H\rightarrow bb$ and $H\rightarrow WW$, followed by $H\rightarrow gg$ and $H\rightarrow \tau\tau$, which involve vector bosons and third-generation fermions. Higgs decays into massless particles do not occur directly; rather, they require quantum loop-induced processes, which are in fact a key tool for investigating physics at energies higher than those directly reachable.

\noindent \textbf{Experimental status}

\noindent The Run I discovery of the Higgs boson was performed inclusively for all production modes, and led by the combination of high-resolution $H\rightarrow ZZ \rightarrow 4l$ and $H\rightarrow\gamma\gamma$ channels, which allowed for a precise determination of $m_H$. After the discovery claim, the analysis of the angular distributions in the $H\rightarrow ZZ \rightarrow 4l$ decay channel confirmed the spin $J$ and parity $P$ of the newly discovered particle, $J^P=0^+$, ruling out all alternative hypothesis at 99.9\% confidence level. Combining various decay channels also allowed for precise measurements of decay rates and coupling strengths, all consistent with SM predictions. Continued research into Higgs boson properties has advanced thanks to the LHC's exceptional performance, the robustness of the experiments, and the development of advanced experimental techniques. The latest findings on Higgs boson couplings, as measured by the CMS collaboration, are summarized in Figure \ref{fig:H_coupling}. Thus far, all observations confirm that the couplings with the $W$ and $Z$ gauge bosons scale quadratically with their masses and that the couplings with third-generation charged fermions and muons depend linearly on their masses.

Thus far, the exploration of Higgs boson properties has revealed that it behaves in a manner consistent with the SM. Although constraints on its couplings partially depend on assumptions about beyond SM contributions and still allow for possible deviations, the scalar boson discovered 12 years ago is now precisely characterized and compatible with a SM Higgs boson. Experimentally, it has been confirmed that the Higgs mechanism breaks degeneracy between the three fermion families by coupling proportionally to their mass. However, there is still a crucial element missing from the full picture: the next fundamental stage in validating the EWSB mechanism is determining the Higgs boson's coupling to itself. The self-coupling value can be derived in the SM from Equation \ref{higgs_pot}, $\lambda_{HHH} = m_H^2/2v^2 \approx 0.13$, fully determined by $m_H$ and $v$.

Experimentally, the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ parameter is accessible at leading order (LO) from Higgs boson pair production rates, and at next-to-leading order (NLO) from cross-section measurements of single Higgs boson production, thanks to radiative NLO electroweak corrections arising from the trilinear coupling. This underscores the key importance of searches for HH production. Furthermore, the experimental determination of HH production has profound implications for BSM theories that introduce additional effective terms into the H potential, given that the kinematic and HH production rates are highly sensitive to new physics. Deviations from SM predictions may indicate the presence of extensions in the scalar sector, ranging from models with additional doublets or singlets to frameworks such as compositeness or supersymmetry. Therefore, directly determining the self-coupling value from HH production constitutes an essential closure test of the BEH mechanism and ultimately probes SM consistency.

\subsection{Limitations of the Standard Model}
\label{subsec:SM_limitations}
The relevance of characterizing SM consistency is supported by a substantial body of theoretical considerations and experimental evidence that underscores the existence of physics beyond the SM that has yet to be discovered. Despite the remarkable success in theoretically describing all observed particle fields, the SM falls short in addressing fundamental unsolved problems in Nature, such as the matter-antimatter asymmetry observed in the Universe, nor the existence of a particle species compatible with the dark matter, which is observed from its gravitational effects. Moreover, the present SM formalism is capable of accounting for only three of the fundamental interactions, excluding the gravitational force. In addition to these limitations, the SM is unable to provide an explanation for the experimentally observed neutrino oscillations, which require leptonic masses and mixing that are not covered by the SM theory.

In the electroweak sector, the SM fails to explain three main theoretical elements that are closely related to the physics of the Higgs boson. Firstly, the current theory does not provide an answer to the question of the origin of the three families of fermions, which are identical in all respects except for their coupling to the Higgs boson, spanning several orders of magnitude. Secondly, the hierarchy problem arises from the value ascribed to the Higgs boson mass. Given the Planck scale ($\sim10^{19}$ GeV), the Higgs boson mass should assume high values, due to quadratically divergent radiative corrections that tend to push the mass towards values much larger than the observed $m_{H}$, suggesting the existence of a mechanism to stabilize it and avoid unnatural fine-tuning. Finally, the validity of the theory up to the Planck scale can only be assured if the scalar potential is bounded from below, thereby guaranteeing vacuum stability. In view of the observed values of $m_H$ and $m_t$, the SM is inclined towards a state of metastability in the scalar potential \cite{EliasMiro:2011aa}, which suggests that the stability of electroweak vacuum may be questionable at very high energies. At such scales, the behavior of the Higgs potential is also relevant for cosmology since certain scenarios propose that the Higgs field could have acted as the inflaton in the early Universe \cite{Bezrukov:2007ep,Bezrukov:2013fka}. The question of vacuum stability and the viability of Higgs-driven inflation are dependent on the shape of the scalar potential, which is determined by the running value of the BEH potential parameter $\lambda$. This parameter is intimately connected to the Higgs boson self-coupling, which is the primary subject of investigation in this thesis.

In this regard, the Standard Model is naturally conceived as a part of a broader theoretical framework that extends beyond it, operating below the Planck scale and providing a resolution to the aforementioned shortcomings of the SM. Consequently, the existence of beyond the SM physics may offer solutions to the limitations of the current theory, while preserving its remarkable success in explaining the phenomena observed in collider experiments.

When measuring possible deviations from SM predictions, it is possible to distinguish between two different approaches depending on the energy scale of the new physics, If BSM physics operates at energies accessible to collider experiments, the new states or particles can be directly produced and experimentally tested. In this case, the presence of new physics would manifest as a new resonance that enhance the SM process cross section for a given value of the invariant mass $m_X$. Conversely, if the energy scale of the new physics is beyond the current experimental reach, it si possible to detect the effects of this new physics indirectly though hypothetical deviations from the SM predictions in the process cross section or kinematics. A graphical representation of these two approaches can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_approaches}. In indirect searches, new results can be interpreted using effective Lagrangians with higher-dimensional operators designed to capture potential BSM effects. In this context, the SM becomes an Effective Field Theory (EFT), which serves as a low-energy approximation of a more comprehensive theory. This approach is not dependent on a specific BSM model, making it a highly versatile tool for exploring unknown aspects of BSM physics.

\section{Higgs boson pair production}
\label{sec:HH_production}
%
Higgs boson pair production (HH) represents one of the primary goals of the LHC program in the coming decades precisely because it provides direct access to the Higgs sector. The HH production rate depends directly on the trilinear self-coupling, $\lambda_{HHH}$, which, according to Equation \eqref{higgs_pot}, determines the structure of the Higgs potential. Therefore, the study of HH production is directly linked to the $\lambda_{HHH}$ parameter, whose crucial role has been strongly justified in this context.

Conversely, the trilinear coupling is not the only interaction that contributes to Higgs boson pair production. In addition to the Higgs self-coupling, which is often considered the primary driver of HH searches, HH production provides a means of testing other SM parameters. Specific production modes involve the Yukawa coupling to the top quark, $y_t$, as well as the trilinear and quadrilinear couplings with vector bosons, $\lambda_V$ and $\lambda_{2V}$. The produciton mechanisms of an on-shell Higgs boson pair, as detailed in this section, are characterized by different interactions from which the contribution of the trilinear coupling must be disentangled from other effects.

Di-Higgs production is a very rare process in particle colliders: its SM cross-section for proton-proton collisions at center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=13$ TeV is expected to be $\sigma_{HH} \sim 32.7$ fb, three orders of magnitude smaller than single Higgs production. Precise measurements of couplings involved in HH production are still challenging to perform directly; the trilinear self-coupling is expected to be determined with precision of approximately 30\% by the end of the High Luminosity LHC phase (expected in 2041) {\color{red}[ref]}. Sec. \ref{subsec:SM_production} details all production mechanisms that contribute to SM HH production. Moreover, effects of physics BSM can already be probed with the current LHC configuration (see \ref{sec:LHC}): small deviations of SM values can lead to significant variations in cross-section rates and kinematics of HH production. An overview of these effects is given in Sec. \ref{subsec:BSM_production}.

\noindent As detailed below and shown in Figure \ref{fig:HH_xsec}, SM HH production at the LHC is a very rare process, with a production rate $\mathcal{O}(10^3)$ times smaller than single Higgs production. Consequently, HH experimental searches — and those covered in this thesis — have primarly focused on the dominant mechanisms, ggF and VBF. Since the gluon fusion is the leading production mode, ggF searches provide the highest sensitivy to the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$; on the other hand, VBF HH production provides additional handles for measuring $\lambda_{HHH}$ and allows for exploration of the VVHH interaction.

It has been underscored that the total cross-section of each production mode stems from the interference of its corresponding diagrams. Thus, small SM HH cross-sections result from processes such as destructive interference between the triangle and box diagrams of the ggF mechanism (see Figure \ref{fig:ggf_contributions}). This phenomenon can be interpreted as meaning that the total HH cross-section depends heavily on the value of the Higgs self-coupling, and small deviations from the SM $\lambda_{HHH}$ value can significantly alter the interference, leading to large enhancements in the HH cross-section. Therefore, the focus shifts from the initial conception of HH production as a test of the SM to its interpretation as a probe of BSM physics, as discussed in more detail in the following section.

\subsection{BSM Di-Higgs production: Towards Effective Field Theories}
\label{subsec:BSM_production}
%
The limitations of the SM discussed in Section \ref{subsec:SM_limitations} motivate the search for new physics, and Higgs boson pair production is a particularly suited probe for it. Owing to its direct connection with the scalar potential, any new physics that modifies the scalar sector is expected to leave an imprint on this process, either through new particles that couple to the Higgs boson or through modified interactions among the particles involved in its production. Testing the SM only requires predicting the HH cross section and comparing it with the data, whereas exploring alternative theories demands in addition understanding how the cross section and the kinematics of the process vary outside the SM. Accordingly, the two complementary approaches illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_approaches} — direct searches for new states and indirect searches for deviations from the SM predictions — translate into two classes of signatures, each with its own interpretation framework: resonant production, naturally interpreted within the specific models that predict the new state, and non-resonant production — the one that also occurs in the SM —, interpreted in a model-independent way through coupling modifiers and effective field theories.

In the resonant scenario, BSM physics operates at energies accessible to the collider experiments: a new particle $X$ with a mass $m_X > 2m_H$ is produced directly and decays into a pair of Higgs bosons, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:HH_resonant}, so that the signal appears as a localized excess in the invariant mass spectrum of the pair on top of the non-resonant SM production. Such states arise naturally in many extensions of the SM, including extensions of the scalar sector — the singlet model \cite{Robens:2015gla}, the two-Higgs-doublet model \cite{Branco:2011iw} or the minimal supersymmetric SM \cite{Djouadi:2005gj} —, which predict additional spin-0 bosons, and models with warped extra dimensions \cite{Randall:1999ee}, which predict spin-0 radions \cite{Goldberger:1999un} and spin-2 Kaluza–Klein gravitons \cite{Agashe:2007zd}. Despite their different theoretical motivations, these models predict CP-even spin-0 or spin-2 states whose intrinsic width is typically negligible compared to the detector resolution, leading to similar experimental signatures. This common signature allows the models to be probed simultaneously in a model-independent way, by scanning a broad range of mass hypotheses from the kinematic threshold up to several TeV. The search for such resonances in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state with the Run 2 dataset, which covers masses between $250$ and $3000$ GeV \cite{CMS-PAS-B2G-24-011}, relies on the jet assignment algorithm described in Chapter \ref{ch:taggers}, whose adaptation to that search was part of this thesis. Resonant production is otherwise not covered further in this work.

In the non-resonant scenario, which is the focus of this thesis, the new physics lies at an energy scale beyond the direct reach of the LHC, and its effects appear instead at lower energies as modifications of the interactions involved in Higgs boson pair production. These modifications alter both the production rate and the kinematic properties of the pair, so that the experimental signature is not a localized excess but a deformation of the distributions predicted by the SM. Their description is addressed here in three steps of increasing generality. First, the couplings of the SM are rescaled by coupling modifiers, the parametrization in which the results of this thesis are expressed. Second, effective field theories are introduced as the systematic framework that generalizes this approach. Finally, the effective couplings that this framework yields for gluon fusion production are presented, which constitute the basis of the signal modeling developed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}.



\subsubsection{Coupling modifiers}
%
In the SM, the value of the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ is entirely determined by the values of $v$ and $m_H$, as shown in Equation \ref{higgs_pot}. To remain sensitive to possible BSM effects, it is instead treated as a free parameter, and its deviations from the SM prediction are parametrized through the self-coupling modifier
%
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_{\lambda} = \frac{\lambda_{HHH}}{\lambda_{HHH}^{SM}},
    \label{eq:kappa_lambda}
\end{equation}
which follows the approach of the $\kappa$-framework, in which each coupling of the Higgs boson is rescaled by a multiplicative factor with respect to its SM value \cite{LHCHiggsCrossSectionWorkingGroup:2013rie}. This is the same framework in which the couplings of Figure \ref{fig:H_coupling} are measured, and expressing the results in terms of $\kappa_\lambda$ allows double and single Higgs boson production — the latter sensitive to $\lambda_{HHH}$ through electroweak loop corrections — to be interpreted consistently and combined. The modifiers of the top quark Yukawa coupling, $\kappa_t = y_t/y_t^{SM}$, and of the couplings to vector bosons, $\kappa_V$ and $\kappa_{2V}$, are defined analogously.

% This coupling rescaling approach is usually referred to as the $\kappa$-framework. The framework is also sensitive to single Higgs measurements affected by deviations from $\lambda_{HHH}$ SM value, due to loop corrections influencing several of these processes. Expressing the experimental results in terms of the coupling modifier allows for the consistent interpretation of both single and double Higgs production, opening the possibility of establishing combined constraints on the value of the trilinear Higgs boson self-coupling.

Modifications in the value of $\lambda_{HHH}$ predicted by the SM have a profound impact on the HH total cross section, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:HH_xsec_vs_kl} for all HH production mechanisms covered in \ref{subsec:SM_production}.

\subsubsection{Effective Field Theories}
%
Up to now, in non-resonant indirect searches, the self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ has been treated as a free parameter, and deviations from the SM prediction are parametrized via coupling strength modifiers within the $\kappa$-framework. This approach enables the simultaneous exploration of a wide range of BSM scenarios, and allows the results to be reinterpreted within specific models. A generalization of this approach with a systematic method is provided by the effective field theories (EFTs): the EFT formalism permits the coherent and gauge-invariant incorporation of possible corrections to SM predictions. Within this framework, modifications to the Higgs self-coupling and other relevant interactions are organized into a expansion of higher-dimensional operators. Thus, EFTs offer a practical theoretical framework for interpreting experimental results and potentially connecting them to comprehensive UV models. Furthermore, the EFT approach is not dependent on a specific BSM model, thereby making it a versatile tool for investigating unknown aspects of BSM physics.

Accordingly, new physics at a scale $\Lambda \gg v$ can be described by incorporating additional fields into the SM Lagrangian that only act at extremely short distances and high energies, meaning their internal structure can be disregarded within the current LHC range. In practice, this entails describing physics at scales below $\Lambda$ by integrating the heavy fields of an extended renormalizable theory that interact only at high energies. Therefore, from a \textit{bottom-up} perspective, $\Lambda$ can be interpreted as the scale up to which only SM fields propagate, while from a \textit{top-down} approach, it corresponds to the scale of the new physics. The resulting effective theory is not renormalizable; however, this is not problematic since an EFT only describes the low-energy manifestation of a more fundamental, renormalizable theory defined at higher scales. Consequently, this procedure generates higher-dimensional, non-renormalizable operators that capture the residual effects of those fields in the low-energy regime.

There is not a single way to formulate such an effective description. The criterion used to expand the SM Lagrangian, and the symmetries imposed on the new theory and its fields, lead to the definition of different EFTs. In the context of Higgs boson pair searches, two main descriptions are used in the literature, and they differ precisely in the assumption they make about the nature of the observed scalar \cite{Alasfar:2023xpc}. According to the SM, the physical Higgs boson and the three Goldstone bosons absorbed by the $W$ and $Z$ bosons form a single $SU(2)_L$ doublet, as described in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}. However, the only experimentally established facts are the existence of those three Goldstone bosons and of a light neutral scalar with a mass of about $125$ GeV and couplings compatible with the SM predictions, while whether this scalar is the fourth component of the same doublet or an electroweak singlet remains a hypothesis rather than a measurement. The Standard Model EFT (SMEFT) adopts the first possibility, assuming that the Higgs boson belongs to a doublet and organizing the new physics effects in a linear, gauge-invariant expansion in higher-dimensional operators, whereas the Higgs EFT (HEFT) adopts the second, treating the Higgs boson as an electroweak singlet and realizing the electroweak symmetry non-linearly, which allows for a very general parametrization of the deviations in its interactions. Both lead to internally consistent, gauge-invariant Lagrangians, but they differ in their assumptions and in the correlations they impose among the couplings of the Higgs boson.

SMEFT retains the Higgs boson as part of an $SU(2)_L$ doublet, so that the electroweak symmetry is realized linearly, exactly as in the SM. Its expansion is therefore organized according to the canonical dimension $D$ of the operators, each additional order being suppressed by a further power of the scale $\Lambda$ of the new physics:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SMEFT}}
    = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}^{(D\leq4)}
    + \sum_{D>4}\sum_i \frac{c_i}{\Lambda^{D-4}}\, \mathcal{O}_i^{(D)},
    \label{eq:eft_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}_i^{(D)}$ are operators of dimension $D$ built from the SM fields and invariant under $SU(3)_C\times SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$ local transformations, $\Lambda$ is the energy scale at which the SM loses validity, and $c_i$ are the Wilson coefficients that parametrize the new physics. Once a complete BSM model is specified, it can be reduced to its low-energy manifestation to express the coefficients $c_i$ in terms of its fundamental parameters, which makes Equation \ref{eq:eft_lagrangian} a model-independent bridge between measurements and theory. The only operator of dimension five violates lepton number and has no impact on Higgs boson phenomenology, so that the leading effects arise at $D=6$, where a complete and non-redundant set of operators is provided by the Warsaw basis \cite{Grzadkowski:2010es}.

The doublet structure has a direct consequence for Higgs boson pair production. In SMEFT, the Higgs field enters the operators only through the combination $\phi^\dagger\phi$, so that a single operator generates the vertices with one and with two Higgs bosons at once. At leading order, the interactions relevant to gluon fusion are governed by five such operators — those modifying the kinetic term and the potential of the Higgs field, the top quark Yukawa interaction, and the contact interaction between the Higgs field and two gluons — and the couplings they generate are therefore correlated. This makes the description more restrictive but also more predictive, since it connects HH production with single Higgs boson and electroweak precision measurements through a common set of parameters. In exchange, SMEFT is only adequate when the new physics decouples, i.e., when the heavy states acquire their mass from a mechanism unrelated to EWSB, so that their effects vanish as their masses increase. Moreover, its predictions depend not only on the Wilson coefficients but also on the scale $\Lambda$ and on the order at which the expansion is truncated, and they remain valid only as long as $c_i E^2/\Lambda^2 \ll 1$, where the relevant energy $E$ is, in HH production, the invariant mass $m_{HH}$ \cite{Heinrich:2022huh}.

HEFT adopts instead the more agnostic hypothesis where the physical Higgs boson is treated as an electroweak singlet, independent of the Goldstone bosons, and the electroweak symmetry is realized non-linearly. The Higgs field can then appear in interaction terms of arbitrary canonical dimension, so that the expansion is no longer ordered by $D$ but by the loop order at which each interaction can first be generated, and the couplings involving one and two Higgs bosons become independent parameters \cite{Buchalla:2018yce}. This more general description accommodates non-decoupling new physics, in which the heavy states acquire part of their mass from EWSB itself, as in composite Higgs models or in scenarios where the Higgs boson is a pseudo-Goldstone boson or a dilaton \cite{Agashe:2004rs,Contino:2010mh,Goldberger:2008zz}. Since any SMEFT Lagrangian can be rewritten in the HEFT language, SMEFT can be regarded as a restricted subset of HEFT, and the two descriptions are complementary rather than alternative: HEFT makes the fewest assumptions, while SMEFT imposes correlations that sharpen the predictions whenever its assumptions hold.

In what follows, the technical details of either formalism are not elaborated; instead, the focus is placed on the phenomenological consequences that both share for Higgs boson pair production.

\subsubsection{The effective coupling formalism}
\label{subsubsec:eff_couplings}
%
Whichever description is adopted, its effect on Higgs boson pair production can be expressed through a small number of effective couplings, which provide a common phenomenological language for the two formalisms. These couplings either modify interactions already present in the production amplitude or introduce new contact interactions, thereby altering both the production rate and the kinematic distributions.

In VBF HH production, the effective description only modifies interactions that already exist in the SM — the $HHH$, $HVV$ and $HHVV$ vertices —, rescaling them or introducing allowed tensor structures, so that no new contact interaction appears at leading order and the diagrams keep the same structure as in the SM.

In contrast, gluon fusion is sensitive to interactions that do not exist in the SM. Besides the anomalous trilinear and top quark Yukawa couplings, $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_t$, three contact interactions contribute at leading order, shown in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_diagrams}: $c_2$ couples a Higgs boson pair directly to a top quark pair, generating a triangle diagram without an intermediate Higgs boson; $c_g$ couples a single Higgs boson to two gluons, so that the gluons produce an off-shell Higgs boson that subsequently splits into the pair through the trilinear coupling; and $c_{2g}$ couples the Higgs boson pair directly to two gluons. Since the loops are dominated by the top quark, anomalous values of the bottom quark Yukawa coupling are neglected.

Therefore, the amplitude of $gg\to HH$ is the sum of five contributions, each multiplied by a fixed product of couplings. Consequently, the HH cross section, given by its squared modulus, is at leading order a polynomial in the five couplings with a finite number of terms — the five squared contributions and their ten interferences:
%
\begin{equation}
    R_{HH} = \frac{\sigma_{HH}}{\sigma_{HH}^{SM}} = \sum_{i=1}^{15} A_i\, f_i(\kappa_\lambda,\kappa_t,c_2,c_g,c_{2g}),
    \label{eq:RHH}
\end{equation}
where the $f_i$ are known monomials in the couplings and the $A_i$ are numerical coefficients determined from simulation \cite{Carvalho:2016rys}. In the absence of contact interactions, $c_2=c_g=c_{2g}=0$, Equation \ref{eq:RHH} reduces to the parametrization in $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_t$ of Equation \ref{eq:kl_kt_ggf}.

The set of five effective couplings $\kappa_{\lambda}$, $\kappa_t$, $c_2$, $c_g$ and $c_{2g}$ constitutes a common phenomenological language to which both EFT formalisms can be mapped under different considerations. In HEFT they are free and independent parameters, whereas in SMEFT, at leading order, they are generated by only four combinations of Wilson coefficients, so that their image is a four-dimensional region of the HEFT space: the two gluon contact couplings arise from the same operator, which fixes $c_{2g}$ in terms of $c_g$, and the top quark contact interaction $c_2$ cannot vary without simultaneously modifying the Yukawa coupling $\kappa_t$ \cite{Heinrich:2022huh}. This difference makes Higgs boson pair production one of the few processes capable of distinguishing between the two descriptions, since the couplings involving two Higgs bosons, $c_2$ and $c_{2g}$, do not contribute to single Higgs boson production at leading order. In HEFT they can therefore only be tested through HH production, whereas in SMEFT they are tied to the $t\bar{t}H$ and $ggH$ interactions, already strongly constrained by single Higgs boson measurements, so that the same measured distribution supports different physical conclusions depending on the description adopted.\medskip

Anomalous couplings not only enhance the HH production cross section, but also modify the kinematic properties of the events significantly. As in the simpler case of $\kappa_\lambda$, the five contributions interfere in a non-trivial way and give rise to a wide variety of signal topologies, so that interpreting a measurement in terms of these couplings requires predicting both the rate and the shape of the signal at every point of a five-dimensional space. This evidences the need to develop modeling tools for these effects in physics analyses. Since HEFT provides the most general description with the fewest assumptions, it is the one adopted in this thesis for that purpose: its formal construction for $gg\to HH$ production and the signal modeling techniques that make such an interpretation feasible are developed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}.

\section{Higgs boson pair searches at the LHC}
\label{sec:HH_searches}
%
After describing the phenomenology associated with the production of Higgs boson pairs, it si essential to understand how experimental searches for the HH signature are conducted. Proton-proton colliders offer a wide variety of accessible HH final states. Because the HH production cross-section is extremely small, searches focus preferentially  on final states with sufficiently large branching ratios (BR). However, each available decay channel poses specific experimental challenges; thus, the channel with the highest BR is not necessarily the most sensitive. Consequently, searches do not focus on a single leading channel. Instead, they are approached inclusively through multiple decay states. The larger number of production modes and the complexity of the final states involved in the experimental study of Higgs pair production — often involving high number of particles to reconstruct — makes it considerable challenging.

The following Section \ref{subsec:HH_BR} summarizes the main HH decay modes accessible at the LHC, along with their BR and experimental challenges. Next, Section \ref{subsec:Searches} reviews the main results of Higgs boson pair searches to date, which will serve to establish the context of where these searches currently stand and the contributions of the work presented in this thesis.


\subsection{Di-Higgs decay modes}
\label{subsec:HH_BR}
\noindent A rich phenomenology of final states are accessible at the LHC. The main challenge in measuring HH production in hadronic colliders is reconstructing the decay products in the detector and distinguishing them from the large background. Achieveng good sensitivity requires finding a balance between the production rates and the complexity of the final states. Thus, the choice of the decay channel for the HH system determines the trade-off between the BR and background contamination. The decay probability is directly related to the Higgs boson's coupling to the decay products and therefore favors third-generation fermions such as bottom quarks ($bb$) and tau leptons ($\tau\tau$), as well as massive vector bosons ($WW$ and $ZZ$), but also massless particles such as gluons ($gg$) and photons ($\gamma\gamma$) via fermionic loops. Note that decay into top quark pairs is highly suppressed by invariant mass conservation. Figure \ref{fig:HH_BR} lists the main possible combinations of HH final states, along with the corresponding branching ratio calculated assuming SM BR. Conversely, background complexity poses separate constraints on the sensitivity of searches, determined by the particles in the final state and the capabilities of experimental identification and reconstruction.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/chapter_I/HH_BR.png}
    \caption{Branching ratio (in \%) for the main combinations of decay products from the HH pair for a Higgs boson mass of 125 GeV. Values extracted from {\color{red}[ref] Hacer mi propio plot}}
    \label{fig:HH_BR}
\end{figure}

The sensitivity to HH production at the LHC is primarily driven by three main channels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $HH\rightarrow bbbb$ is the final state with the highest branching ratio ($\sim$33.9\%). However, significant background contamination from the dominant QCD multi-jet background affects the purity and sensitivity of $4b$ analysis. Consequently, it has little sensitivity to low $m_{HH}$ values, though it can benefir from large signal yields to probe regions up to $m_{HH} \approx 3$ TeV. Recent advances in jet tagging techniques have been fundamental to this channel's sensitivity, which now stands as one of the most competitive for HH searches~\cite{CMS:2022bbbb,ATLAS:2023bbbb}, particularly in the VBF~\cite{ATLAS:2024VBFbbbb} and VHH~\cite{ATLAS:2023VHH} production modes.

    \item $HH\rightarrow bb\tau\tau$ has a slightly smaller BR ($\sim$7.3\%), but benefits from higher purity due to the tau pair. Background for this channel comes from a combination of QCD multijet, Drell-Yan, and $t\overline{t}$ events contributions. The sizable BR and relatively low background provide an advantageous compromise, making it one of the most sensitive channels for setting upper limits on the HH production cross-section.

    \item $HH\rightarrow bb\gamma\gamma$ completes the three main decay modes, being the purest of them. Its low BR ($\sim$0.3\%) is counterbalanced by the clean photon signature. The main background contribution is the di-photom continuum, which is well-understood from single Higgs measurements, allowing for better background control.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This thesis focuses on searches in the $HH\rightarrow bb\tau\tau$ decay channel, an intermediate and particularly interesting scenario. As previously noted, experimental challenges vary significantly depending on the final state under consideration. In the case of $bb\tau\tau$, complexity increases due to the $\tau$ lepton unestable nature, which can decay into both leptons and hadrons in association with neutrinos. Therefore, searches must analyze different final states, and the complete reconstruction of the event is compromised by the presence neutrinos, since they impede the direct determination of the invariant mass of the system. This decay channel also suffers from irreducible background sources that are kinematically similar to the signal process. Thus, kinematic information must be fully exploited to effectively reduce the background contamination. For these reasons, the $bb\tau\tau$ channel is considered one of the most challenging at the LHC. However, the effort is rewarded with one of the best sensitivities for both resonant and non-resonant HH production hypotheses, as demonstrated by various phenomenological studies~\cite{Baglio:2012np,Dolan:2012rv,Barr:2013tda}. The specific properties of the $bb\tau\tau$ channel and the main background contributions are discussed in greater detail in Chapter \ref{ch:bbtt_analysis}, and throughout the entirety of this thesis.

\subsection{Experimental status of double Higgs searches}
\label{subsec:Searches}

\noindent The most widely used analysis historically at the LHC have been highlighted. However, the current scope is much broader, and the analysis landscape of the experimental collaborations is increasingly encompassing more final states. Thus, efforts are focused on covering the full range of main channels accessible at LHC, which is gradually becoming possible due to increased statistics, and substantial object reconstruction and analysis techniques improvements. Since searches across various decay channels complement each other — in both the SM and BSM scenarios —, the maximum sensitivity of HH measurements is achieved by combining the main decay channels.

The full set of proton-proton collisions at $\sqrt{s}=8$ TeV (Run I) and $\sqrt{s}=13$ TeV (Run II) has been analyzed for numerous HH final states. The Run I analyses established the fundamental methodology and provided the initial combined limits on HH production after collecting an integrated luminosity of $\sim$36 fb\textsuperscript{-1}. During Run II, the CMS experiment accumulated an integrated luminosity of approximately 138 fb\textsuperscript{-1}. This quantitative breakthrough, combined with significant advancements in detector and analysis strategies, enabled a remarkable expansion of the experimental scope. Searches were conduced in most main channels, and the combined Run II results achieved sensitivities far superior to those of Run I. This allowed exploration of regions of the trilinear self-coupling parameter space that were previously innaccessible, as well as reinterpretations in terms of EFT.

The results obtained by the CMS combination using the complete Run II dataset are shown in Figure \ref{fig:comb_upper_limits}, expressed in terms of upper limits on the HH cross section scaled to the SM value, which is defined as the signal strength $\mu_{HH}=\sigma_{HH}/\sigma^{SM}_{HH}$. In this formulation, the limits are obtained under the null hypothesis, which corresponds to the absence of a signal, i.e., $\mu_{HH}=0$. The statistical procedure used to construct these limits is described in detail in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_statistics}. The combination yields an observed (expected) upper limit of 3.5 (2.5) times the SM expectation at 95\% confidence level (CL), where the sensitivity is driven by the three main channels listed above. This improvement substantially surpasses the limits obtained in Run I, confirming progress achieved thanks not only to increased luminosity, but also to the optimization of reconstruction strategies and the use of advanced machine learning–based discrimination techniques. The ATLAS collaboration obtained similar results, with an observed (expected) upper limit of 2.9 (2.4) times the SM value at 95\% CL.

The start of Run III ($\sqrt{s} = 13.6$) signifies a new phase in the search for Higgs boson pairs. \section{Phenomenology of proton-proton collisions}   
\label{sec:pp_pheno}
% 
Both the results reviewed in the previous section and the improvement in sensitivity expected from Run III and the HL-LHC rely on an accurate prediction of the signal and the backgrounds. However, obtaining such a prediction requires more than the production mechanisms described in Section \ref{subsec:SM_production}, since the LHC does not collide elementary particles, but the protons that contain them. Although the interaction of interest takes place between the constituents of the proton — the quarks and gluons described in Section \ref{subsec:particle_content}, collectively referred to as partons — the way in which they are distributed within the proton cannot be calculated from first principles, and each of them carries only a fraction of the proton momentum. Furthermore, the hard interaction does not occur in isolation: the partons radiate before and after it, the quarks and gluons in the final state hadronize, and the remnants of the protons also interact with one another. As a result, the final state recorded by the detector contains much more than the decay products of the Higgs boson pair. Describing a proton-proton collision therefore requires separating the parts of the process that can be calculated in perturbation theory from those that must be extracted from data or modeled phenomenologically, a separation formalized by the factorization theorem~\cite{Collins:1989gx}.

The following subsections go through this description in the same order in which the prediction is built. First, Section \ref{subsec:pp_factorization} presents the factorization of the hadronic cross section into a partonic cross section, calculated to a given order in perturbation theory, and the parton distribution functions (PDFs) of the protons. Next, Section \ref{subsec:pp_event} describes the processes that complete the event beyond the hard interaction: initial- and final-state radiation, hadronization, and the underlying event. Finally, Section \ref{subsec:pp_simulation} summarizes the Monte Carlo simulation chain that reproduces all these steps, from the matrix-element generators to the simulation of the detector response. These elements form the basis of the simulated samples used to model the signal and the backgrounds in Chapter \ref{ch:bbtt_analysis}, and their uncertainties reappear there as theoretical uncertainties of the search.

\subsection{Factorization and parton distribution functions}
\label{subsec:pp_factorization}
%
Since hard interactions occur between the partons that make up the proton, the energy available in each interaction is not fixed. Each parton carries a fraction $x$ of the momentum of the proton to which it belongs, so the center-of-mass energy of the partonic interaction, $\sqrt{\hat{s}}=\sqrt{x_1x_2\,s}$, is lower than the nominal collision energy, $\sqrt{s}$, and varies from event to event. The probability of finding a parton of type $i$ with a momentum fraction $x$ in the proton is given by its parton distribution function (PDF), $f_i(x)$, which therefore determines how often the energy required to produce a given final state is reached.


The factorization theorem~\cite{Collins:1989gx} allows the cross section of a process $pp\to X$ to be written as the sum, over all pairs of partons $i,j$ that can initiate it, of the partonic cross section weighted by the PDFs of the two partons:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{pp\to X} = \sum_{i,j}\int_0^1 dx_1\,dx_2\; f_i(x_1,\mu_F)\,f_j(x_2,\mu_F)\;\hat{\sigma}_{ij\to X}(x_1x_2\,s,\,\mu_R,\,\mu_F),
    \label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
where $f_i(x,\mu_F)$ are the PDFs and $\hat{\sigma}_{ij\to X}$ is the cross section for the interaction between the two partons. This separation is possible owing to the behavior of the strong interaction described in Section \ref{subsec:particle_content}. In hard interactions, which involve high energies, $\alpha_s$ is small, and $\hat{\sigma}_{ij\to X}$ can be calculated from the Feynman diagrams of the process as a power series in $\alpha_s$, whose first term defines the leading-order (LO) prediction and whose subsequent terms — NLO, NNLO — incorporate the emission of additional partons and loop corrections, which are sizable in gluon-initiated processes. At the energies characteristic of the structure of the proton, however, $\alpha_s$ is large and this series is no longer applicable, so the PDFs cannot be calculated in this way. The scales $\mu_F$ and $\mu_R$ in Equation \ref{eq:factorization} determine, respectively, the boundary between the two regimes and the energy at which $\alpha_s$ is evaluated.

The PDFs are therefore extracted from global fits to measurements of other processes — deep inelastic scattering, Drell–Yan production, jet production, or $t\bar{t}$ pair production — and, being universal, can then be used in any prediction~\cite{NNPDF:2017mvq}. Their dependence on the scale, in contrast, can be calculated~\cite{Gribov:1972ri,Dokshitzer:1977sg,Altarelli:1977zs}. Figure \ref{fig:pdfs} shows the PDFs from the NNPDF3.1 set at two energy scales, $Q^2=10$ and $10^4$ GeV$^2$, the latter being on the order of those involved in Higgs boson production. Valence quarks dominate at high values of $x$, while gluons dominate at low values, increasingly so as the scale grows. Since producing a pair of Higgs bosons at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV requires $\sqrt{\hat{s}}\geq2m_H\approx250$ GeV, that is, typical momentum fractions of the order of $x\sim10^{-2}$, the process takes place precisely in the region dominated by gluons, which helps explain the predominance of gluon fusion described in Section \ref{subsec:SM_production}.

When the partonic cross-section series is truncated at a given order, the cross section of Equation \ref{eq:factorization} depends slightly on the values chosen for $\mu_R$ and $\mu_F$, which are set to a scale characteristic of the process. The uncertainty due to the missing higher orders is therefore estimated by varying both scales by a factor of two~\cite{deFlorian:2016spz}. This is combined with the uncertainty of the PDFs themselves — the band in Figure \ref{fig:pdfs} — and that of the value of $\alpha_s$. These are the scale and PDF$+\alpha_s$ uncertainties quoted for the cross sections in Section \ref{subsec:SM_production}, and the same variations, applied to the simulated samples, constitute the theoretical uncertainties of the search, described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}.



\subsection{Parton showers, hadronization and underlying event}
\label{subsec:pp_event}
%
Equation \ref{eq:factorization} predicts how often the final state of the hard interaction is produced, but it does not describe the complete event recorded by the detector. The quarks and gluons involved in the interaction emit gluons, both before the interaction — initial-state radiation (ISR) — and after it — final-state radiation (FSR) — and the emitted gluons can in turn radiate further or split into quark-antiquark pairs. The first of these emissions are part of the higher-order corrections to the partonic cross section described in Section \ref{subsec:pp_factorization} — an NLO calculation includes one additional emission, and an NNLO calculation two — but the rest must be described approximately by a parton shower, an algorithm included in the event generators of Section \ref{subsec:pp_simulation} that generates successive emissions probabilistically, each at a lower energy scale than the previous one, from the scale of the hard interaction down to energies of the order of $1$ GeV, where $\alpha_s$ becomes large and the perturbative description is no longer valid~\cite{Buckley:2011ms}. This radiation gives rise to the additional jets that accompany the hard interaction and, by carrying away part of the momentum of the initial partons, causes the particles produced in it — for example, the $Z$ boson in Drell–Yan events — to acquire a nonzero $p_T$, whose modeling in the simulation is corrected in this search, as detailed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_corrections}.

Below the $1$ GeV scale, the resulting quarks and gluons are no longer able to propagate freely. Instead, confinement, described in Section \ref{subsec:particle_content}, forces them to combine into color-neutral hadrons, in a process called hadronization. Since this is a non-perturbative regime, hadronization is described by phenomenological models whose parameters are fitted to data. Only color-charged particles take part in this process: leptons and photons, which do not interact strongly, do not hadronize. After the decay of unstable hadrons, the combined result of the parton shower and hadronization is a set of jets: collimated sprays of particles whose direction and energy approximately match those of the original quark or gluon, and which are the objects reconstructed in the detector (Section \ref{subsec:Jets}).

Finally, the hard interaction involves only one parton from each proton, but the rest of the proton does not disappear. When a color-charged parton is removed, what remains of each proton — the beam remnants — is no longer color-neutral and, for the same reason, also hadronizes, giving rise to particles emitted mostly along the beam direction. In addition, other partons from the same two protons may interact with each other in the same collision, usually with a lower momentum transfer, in what are known as multiple parton interactions. Everything that arises from the same proton-proton collision but not from the hard interaction constitutes the \textit{underlying event}\footnote{The underlying event should not be confused with pile-up, which arises from collisions of other proton pairs in the same bunch crossing (Section \ref{subsec:LHC_design}).}, which adds low-energy particles throughout the event, superimposed on the products of the hard interaction, and affects, for example, the isolation of leptons or the energy of the jets.

None of these stages can be calculated with the precision of the hard interaction: the parton shower is an approximation, and hadronization and the underlying event depend on models whose parameters are fitted to data. Their modeling therefore constitutes a source of uncertainty in its own right. In this search, it is evaluated by varying the parameters that control initial- and final-state radiation, which, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}, mainly affects the number and energy of the additional jets. All of these stages are combined in the Monte Carlo event generators described below.


\subsection{Monte Carlo event simulation}
\label{subsec:pp_simulation}
%
The steps described in the two previous sections are reproduced in practice using Monte Carlo (MC) event generators, programs that produce simulated events one by one, choosing the outcome of each step at random — the initial partons and their momentum fractions, the configuration of the hard interaction, each emission of the parton shower — with the probability assigned to it by theory~\cite{Buckley:2011ms}. Each simulated event thus contains the complete list of particles in the final state, just like a real event, and the ensemble of many such events reproduces the predicted distributions, so that simulation and data can be analyzed in exactly the same way.

This task is typically divided between two types of programs. Matrix-element generators compute the partonic cross section of Equation \ref{eq:factorization} at LO or NLO and use it to generate the hard interaction; among the most commonly used in CMS are \MGaMC~\cite{Alwall:2014hca} and \POWHEG~\cite{powheg1,powheg2,powheg3}. The resulting events are then completed by a general-purpose generator, such as \PYTHIA~\cite{Bierlich:2022pfr}, which adds the parton shower, hadronization, and the underlying event, and simulates the decay of unstable particles. Since the matrix element and the parton shower can generate the same emissions, the two must be combined so that no emission is counted twice. When the starting point is an NLO calculation, which already includes the first additional emission, this combination is called \textit{matching}~\cite{powheg2,Alwall:2014hca}. When several samples with different numbers of additional partons in the matrix element are combined — as in the simulation of Drell–Yan production in association with jets — it is called \textit{merging}, and relies on a scale that determines which emissions are described by the matrix element and which by the parton shower~\cite{Frederix:2012ps}. The parameters of the parton shower, hadronization, and the underlying event that are fitted to data are grouped into sets called \textit{tunes}: the CMS simulations for Run II and Run III use the CP5 tune of \PYTHIA~\cite{CMS:2019csb}, derived with the PDFs shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdfs}.

Finally, the generated particles are propagated through a detailed model of the detector using \GEANT~\cite{GEANT4:2002zbu}, which simulates their interactions with the various subdetectors and the signals these interactions produce. Additional interactions are superimposed on these events to reproduce the pile-up conditions, and the resulting events are reconstructed with the same algorithms as the data; both the detector and these algorithms are described in the following chapter.

Since each simulated event preserves the complete history of its generation, the parton or decay from which each particle originates is known — key information for measuring efficiencies, defining which events are signal, and training identification algorithms such as those of Chapter \ref{ch:taggers}. However, none of the stages of the simulation is perfect, and the remaining differences with respect to data must be corrected and covered by uncertainties, as is done in this search in Sections \ref{sec:bbtt_corrections} and \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}.\bigskip

This chapter has presented the theoretical framework in which this thesis is set: the SM and the BEH mechanism, which predicts the self-interaction of the Higgs boson; Higgs boson pair production, the most direct way to measure it and a probe of physics beyond the SM; the current status of its search at the LHC; and the elements used to build predictions for proton-proton collisions. All of this also determines the experimental requirements of the search. On the one hand, with a cross section of about $34$ fb at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV, approximately one Higgs boson pair is produced for every $10^{12}$ inelastic collisions, so the highest attainable energy and luminosity are required. On the other hand, the $bb\tau\tau$ final state requires identifying jets originating from $b$ quarks, reconstructing $\tau$ leptons in their various decay modes, and inferring the accompanying neutrinos from $p_T^\text{miss}$, all in an environment with dozens of simultaneous collisions. The following chapter describes the LHC and the CMS detector, which respectively provide and record these collisions, as well as the algorithms used to reconstruct the objects of this final state.
