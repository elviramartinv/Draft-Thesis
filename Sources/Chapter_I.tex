\chapter{Theoretical context}
\label{ch:theory}
\noindent The Standard Model (SM) of particle physics {\color{red} [ref]} is a renormalizable quantum field theory that describes the elementary particles and their fundamental interactions. Formulated in the early 1970s as a consolidation of theories and experimental discoveries from the previous decade, SM predictions have been well corroborated by numerous observations in collider experiments. The discovery of the Higgs boson is the final entry on this extensive checklist, theorized as a direct consequence of the Brout-Englert-Higgs (BEH) mechanism, which is responsible for the spontaneous electroweak symmetry breaking (EWSB).

Nevertheless, despite the excellent agreement the SM has yielded, a large body of evidence ranging from subnuclear to astrophysical phenomena suggests that its current formulation is incomplete. This makes a more comprehensive theory necessary to provide a complete picture. Exploring the scalar sector is a promising way to research physics Beyond the SM (BSM), where the connection between the Higgs boson self-interaction and the nature of the Higgs potential plays a fundamental role. Therefore, measuring the Higgs boson self-coupling constant, which is currently unconstrained by observation, could offer invaluable insights.

The present chapter discusses the importance of studying HH production in this context and aims to establish its theoretical basis. Section 1 provides a concise overview of the SM formulation. Section 2 focuses on double Higgs boson production and describes its phenomenology. In Section 3, the status of collider experiments and previous results obtained at the LHC are discussed.


\section{Structure of the Standard Model of particle physics}




\noindent The SM was initially proposed by S. Glashow, A. Salam, and S. Weinberg {\color{red} [ref]} and has been demonstrated to be the most compelling theoretical framework for synthesizing fundamental constituents and their interactions. It is a renormalizable quantum field theory (QFT) based on quantum mechanics and special relativity that satisfies gauge invariance. According to this framework, elementary particles are defined as indivisible entities devoid of internal structure, and correspond to excited states of underlying quantum field, which are mathematically described by QFT.

The SM also encompasses three of the four fundamental interactions. These are the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions, each connected to different intrinsic charges of the matter particles: the electromagnetic charge, the weak isospin, and the color charge. These interactions are described by the exchange of particle mediators, which arise as a consequence of local gauge invariance. The description of the gravitational force could not be included in the SM, which is a limitation of the model. However, it can be disregarded at the particle level scale since it is a force with such a weak intensity that its effect has practically no influence compared to the other forces.

The mathematical formulation of the SM is based on a Lagrangian density, $\mathcal{L}$, which condenses all the necessary information to describe the propagation and interactions of the particle fields. The Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ is defined as a scalar function of the fields $\phi_i$, their derivatives $\partial_\mu \phi_i$, and the space-time coordinates, $x^\mu$, and preserves invariance under local gauge symmetries. The invariance of the system under specific set of transformations constitutes the fundamental principle for the SM formalism. According to the postulate of Noether's theorem {\color{red} [ref]}, each global continuous symmetry of a physical system results in a conserved quantity. These quantities are the intrinsic charges of the matter particles associated with each interaction, and they manifest as a consequence of the symmetry of the SM Lagrangian under the continuous group $SU(3)_C \times SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$. Furthermore, as a direct consequence of local gauge symmetry, the introduction of vector fields is imperative, and their quantum excitations result in the particles that mediate the interactions: the strong interaction, invariant under $SU(3)_C$ and described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD); and the electroweak (EWK) force, unifying the $SU(2)_L \otimes U(1)_Y$ symmetries.

\subsection{Particle content}

According to the SM, the elementary particles can be classified depending on their spin. Two types of particles are distinguished: fermions with spin 1/2 and bosons with integer spin (0 or 1). The former arise from Lorentz spinor fields and constitute all known matter. In regard to the latter, a distinction is made between gauge bosons, corresponding to spin 1, which are associated with the vector fields and responsible for mediating interactions between fermions; and the Higgs boson, with spin 0, linked to a scalar field and responsible for the mechanism by which fermions and bosons acquire their mass. The particle content and their properties are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:SM_particles}, and discussed in detail next.

\noindent\textbf{Matter particles: Fermions}

\noindent Due to the relativistic nature of the SM, quantum excitations of spinorial fields that transform in the spin-1/2 representation of the Lorentz group give rise to half-integer spin particles called fermions. Experimental observations of fermions have demonstrated the existence of twelve fermions: six quark fields and six lepton fields, which will be detailed below. The 12 fermions are arranged into three generations of mass, with each generation consisting of two quarks with electric charges of $Q=+2/3$ and $Q=-1/3$ and two leptons with charges of $Q=-1$ and $Q=0$. The difference in mass between the families is related to their coupling strength with scalar fields. The first family, being the one with the lowest mass, constitutes stable matter, while the particles of the other families are predominantly unstable and decay rapidly. Each fermion has a corresponding antiparticle with the same spacetime characteristics (mass and spin) but opposite intrinsic quantum numbers (charges), meaning when a particle and its antiparticle interact, they annihilate each other.


\noindent\textbf{Quarks}
Each generation contains a quark doublet composed of an up-type quark $Q=+2/3$: up (u), charm (c) and top (t); and a down-type quark $Q=-1/3$ down (d), strange (s), bottom (b). The first-family quarks, up and down, are the lightest, with masses of 2.2 and 4.7 MeV, {\color{red} [ref]} respectively. Their counterparts in subsequent generations reach mass ranges of GeV in the second family, with masses of 1.27 GeV and 93.4 MeV for charm and strange, and of the order of hundreds of GeV in the third generation, composed of top and bottom, with masses of about 172.7 and 4.2 GeV, respectively.

Of all the particle content of the SM, only quarks are subject to all three forces. They are the only fermions that carry color charge connected to the strong force, in addition to their electric charge. The strong interaction, as described by quantum chromodynamics, leads to the phenomena known as QCD color confinement, thereby implying that quarks cannot exist as a free states, as observed for leptons, but can only be detected as bound states, called \textsc{hadrons}hadrons. Only color-neutral hadrons exist in nature, so the only possible states are mesons, composed of a quark-antiquark pair, and baryons, formed by three quarks. The top quark, however, is an exception, since its very high mass and therefore its high instability cause it to decay via electroweak interaction before it can form a bound state. At very high energies, the strong force weakens, causing quarks to behave like asymptotically free particles and allowing their interactions to be studied in proton colliders like the LHC.

\noindent\textbf{Leptons}
\noindent The other variety of fermions are known as leptons, which, devoid of color charge, interact solely through the weak and electromagnetic forces. Each family of lepton doublets consists of a charged lepton with electric charge $Q=1$: electron ($e$), muon ($\mu$) o tau ($\tau$); and the corresponding neutrino ($\nu$) with $Q=0$.

The three charged leptons have masses that differ by up to four orders of magnitude: the electron has a mass of 511 keV, the muon 105.7 MeV, and the tau 1.8 GeV {\color{red} [ref]}. Consequently, the electron is the only stable charged lepton, while muons have a mean lifetime of 2.2 $\mu s$ and the tau has a mean lifetime of 2.9 x 10-13 s. In collider experiments, the muon receives a Lorentz boost large enough to be detected as stable particles thanks to their high momentum and the size of the experiment. The tau, on the other hand, is highly unstable and can only be inferred from its decay products.

Neutral leptons, neutrinos, share the same lepton flavor as their corresponding charged leptons, finding $\nu_e$,$\nu_{\mu}$ and $\nu_{\tau}$. Because they are electrically neutral, neutrinos only interact with matter through the weak force, which makes direct detection in experiments unfeasible. Their indirect signature is revealed by the missing energy in the final state. In the classical formulation of the SM, the neutrino is assumed to be massless. This assumption, however, is contradicted by the observation of neutrino oscillations in 1998 {\color{red} [ref]}, implying that neutrinos are massive particles with upper limits of $m(\nu_e) < 1.1$ eV, $m(\nu_\mu) < 0.19$ MeV, and $m(\nu_\tau) < 18.2$ MeV. In order to account for the phenomenon of neutrino oscillations, the interplay between flavor and mass eigenstates is depicted by the Pontecorvo-Maki-Nakagawa-Sakata (PMNS) matrix, introduced as a revision to the SM.

\noindent\textbf{Bosons}

\noindent\textbf{Force-carrying particles: Gauge Bosons}

\noindent Gauge bosons are spin-1 particles responsible for mediating the fundamental interactions of the SM. They are the excitations of each gauge field associated with local symmetries, so the number of bosons mediator each force depends on the dimension of the gauge group, and their properties arise from the corresponding interaction's Lagrangian.

The symmetry gauge group $SU(3)_C$ is associated with the strong interaction and involves eight generators known as gluons (g). Gluons are massless and electrically neutral particles that carry color charge and therefore interact with other colored particles, meaning themselves and quarks. As a result of being colored particles, gluons cannot be directly observed as free particles, yet their existence is inferred from the hadronic jets produced by gluon emission, with the firs experimental evidence appearing in 1979 {\color{red} [ref]}.

The electroweak sector unified in the symmetry $SU(2)_L \otimes U(1)_Y$ consists of four generators. The photon ($\gamma$), a neutral, massless particle, is associated with the electromagnetic force. The other three generators are the two $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons, which are responsible for the charged and neutral currents of the weak force, respectively. The $W$ and $Z$ bosons were directly observed in 1983, and the two charged $W$ bosons with opposite charges $Q=\pm1$ have identical masses of 80.3 GeV while the mass of the neutral $Z$ boson is 91.2 GeV.

\noindent\textbf{Scalar Boson: Higgs Boson}

\noindent To endow the $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ bosons with mass, the $SU(2)_L \otimes U(1)_Y$ must be broken. This symmetry breaking process is realized through the Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism, which introduces a scalar field that preserves renormalizability and gauge invariance within the SM. The quantum excitation of this field is the Higgs boson, the only fundamental non-gauge spin-0 or scalar boson in the SM. It couples directly to all other particles, except for photons and gluons, imparting mass to them, as has been proven experimentally. Its description and properties are intimately linked to the EWSB mechanism, detailed in section {\color{red} [section EWSB]}.

\subsection{Fundamental interactions}

\noindent\textbf{Strong interaction}

\noindent The strong interaction is described by the theory of QCD, which is based on the local gauge invariance under the non-abelian group $SU(3)_C$. The subscript C denotes color, the conserved charge associated with the symmetry. As previously stated, each particle in the SM is characterized by a field $\psi(x)$, and since only quarks carry color charge, only quark fields have a non-trivial representation under this group. The interaction between these particles is described by the Lagrangian, which, in the case of quarks, corresponds to the Dirac Lagrangian density.

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dirac}} = \bar{\psi}(x)(i\slashed{\partial} - m)\psi(x)
\label{eq:dirac_lagrangian}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\psi$ is the bi-spinor quark field at the space-time coordinates $x$, $m$ is the quark mass, and $\not{\partial} = \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu$ is the Dirac operator, representing the contraction of the Dirac matrices $\gamma^\mu$ with the first order space-time derivatives $\partial_\mu$. The Dirac Lagrangian is defined to be invariant under global $SU(3)$ transformations, but loses its invariance under local $SU(3)$ transformations. Fermion fields transform in the following way under $SU(3)_C$ group:

\begin{equation}
\psi(x) \rightarrow e^{\left(i g_s \theta^a(x) \frac{\lambda_a}{2}\right)} \psi(x), \quad
\label{lagrangian_transformation}
\end{equation}

\noindent with \(\lambda_a\) being the eight Gell-Mann matrices that generate the group, \(\theta^a\) arbitrary constants, and \(g_s\) the strong coupling constant. To restore local gauge invariance, understood as the preservation of the invariance of the Lagrangian density \eqref{eq:dirac_lagrangian} under the transformation \eqref{lagrangian_transformation}, the derivative $\partial_\mu$ is redefined as covariant derivative \(D_{\mu}\):

\begin{equation}
D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i g_s \frac{\lambda_a}{2} G^a_\mu
\label{covariant_derivative}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the new gauge vector fields \(G^a_\mu\) correspond to the eight gluon fields, the gauge bosons mediators for the strong interaction. Incorporating the vector fields ensures that the covariant derivative transforms equivalently as the quark fields and, consequently, the Lagrangian density is promoted to be invariant under local gauge transformations.

To complete the Lagrangian density, a kinetic term for the gluon fields is included in the form:

\begin{equation}
-\frac{1}{4}G^{\mu\nu}_a G^a_{\mu\nu}
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(G^a_{\mu\nu}\) can be interpreted as the gluon field-strength tensor, and is defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
G^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu G^a_\nu - \partial_\nu G^a_\mu + g_s f^{abc} G^b_\mu G^c_\nu
\label{gluon_field}
\end{equation}

\noindent with \(f^{abc}\) symbols denoting the structure constants of the \(SU(3)\) gauge group. The final term of the strength field tensor is incorporated due to the non-abelian nature of $SU(3)_C$, resulting in gluon self-interactions. The full QCD Lagrangian finally reads:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{QCD}} = \bar{\psi}(x)(i\slashed{\partial} - m)\psi(x) - g_s \bar{\psi}(x) \gamma^\mu \frac{\lambda_a}{2} \psi(x) G^a_\mu - \frac{1}{4} G_a^{\mu\nu} G^a_{\mu\nu}
\label{QCD_Lagrangian}
\end{equation}

\noindent The first term is equivalent to the original Dirac Lagrange equation \eqref{eq:dirac_lagrangian}, and denotes the unperturbed propagation of the quark. The second term is attributed to the introduction of the covariant derivative and signifies the interaction of the quark with the vector field \(G_\mu\). The strength of the interaction is parameterized by the constant $g_s$, which is typically expressed through the redefinition: \(\alpha_s=g_s^2/4\pi\). The third term has been incorporated as a kinetic term of the vector field, leading to cubic and quartic self-interactions of the gluon fields, a general feature of non-abelian gauge theories.

In this formulation, the imposition of local gauge invariance under SU(3) transformations has yielded the introduction of gauge bosons, the gluons, and their interactions with fermionic fields, the quarks. The $SU(3)$ group implies the presence of eight gluons, which are distinguished solely by the color charge they carry. It is noteworthy that explicit mass terms in the form of \(G_a^{\mu\nu} G^a_{\mu\nu}\) for the gauge bosons in \eqref{QCD_Lagrangian} would result in the violation of gauge invariance of the Lagrangian.

\noindent\textbf{Electroweak interaction}

\noindent The electroweak (EWK) group provides a unified description of the electromagnetic and weak interactions. It is derived from the imposition of a local gauge invariance mechanism under the group $SU(2)_L \otimes U(1)_Y$.

% electromagnetic sector
The gauge group $U(1)_{em}$ is the abelian group associated with the electromagnetic sector, whose description is encapsulated in the theory of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED). Since QED is based on the same principles as QCD, the final expression of the Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}_{QED}\) can be derived from the Dirac Lagrangian \eqref{eq:dirac_lagrangian} as a starting point. The Dirac Lagrangian is consistent with global invariance under the group $U(1)_{em}$, yet is incompatible with local transformations. In order to promote invariance from global to local, it is necessary to replace the canonical derivative with a covariant derivative, \(D_\mu\), expressed as follows:

\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu - i e A_\mu
\label{derivative_electro}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the coupling constant is denoted by $e$ and corresponds to the unit of electric charge, while the term \(A_\mu\) represents the electromagnetic force carrier field, known as the photon field. In order to complete Lagrangian density, a kinetic term is introduced for the photon fields that accounts for the propagation of the photon, which depends on the field strength tensor \(F^{\mu\nu}\), defined as:

\begin{equation}
    F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu
\label{tensor_electro}
\end{equation}

\noindent where, in contrast to the non-abelian group of the strong interaction, QED does not predict self-interaction of the photon. The QED Lagrangian is thus expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{QED}} = \bar{\psi}(i\!\not{\partial} - m)\psi + e\,\bar{\psi} A^\mu \gamma_\mu \psi - \frac{1}{4} F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu}
\label{lagrangian_electro}
\end{equation}

In this formulation, the photon field emerges as a consequence of imposing local gauge invariance under $U(1)_{em}$, as well as its interactions with charged fermions, with the electric charge, $Q$, being the quantity conserved in the interaction. Similarly to the gluon field, it is imperative to reiterate the conclusion previously reached, which states that the incorporation of a hypothetical mass term for the photon would result in a violation of the invariance of the Lagrangian.

% Weak description sector
The non-abelian group $SU(2)_L$ is responsible for describing the weak interaction, which differs substantially from the other forces, therefore requires a more complex structure. Unlike the electromagnetic and strong interactions, the weak interaction universally couples to all fermions connecting different flavors of fermions through the Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa (CKM) mixing matrix. Additionally, the it is the only force observed to violate charge (C), parity (P), and combined charge-parity (CP) symmetries, all of which are conserved in the other interactions. The violation of parity {\color{red} [ref]} is indicative of the existence of \textit{chirality}, a fermion property according to which particles couple differently. Chirality is introduced into the theory as a Lorentz-invariant quantity corresponding to the eigenvalues \(\gamma^5=i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3\). This is used to define the left and right chirality projection operators as \(\frac{1-\gamma^5}{2}\) y \(\frac{1+\gamma^5}{2}\) respectively, giving rise to left (\(\psi_L\)) and right (\(\psi_R\) chirality fields. In the limit of a massless particle, chirality corresponds to helicity, which is defined as the normalized projection of the spin vector onto the spacial momentum vector.

The weak interaction affects only left-handed fermions (or right-handed anti-fermions), as reflected by the L subscript in the $SU(2)_L$ symmetry, which refers to the left-handed chirality of the fermions involved in the interaction. Gauge invariance under this group results in the presence of three gauge fields, \(W^i_\mu(i=1,2,3)\). Thus, each fermionic field in the SM is represented as a left-handed doublet (\(\psi_L\)) that participates in the weak interaction and two right-handed singlets (\(\psi_R,\psi'_R\)) that do not interact with the gauge fields \(W^i_\mu\) and are therefore excluded from the weak interaction:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
    \psi_L \equiv \frac{1 - \gamma^5}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\psi \\\psi'\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_L \\\psi'_L\end{pmatrix} \\
    \psi_R \equiv \frac{1 + \gamma^5}{2}\,\psi \\
    \psi'_R \equiv \frac{1 + \gamma^5}{2}\,\psi'
\label{fermionic_fields}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}


where \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) represents either the neutrino and charged lepton fields or the up- and down-type quark fields. Note that these two sectors are separate, and neither the strong nor the electroweak interactions can transform quark fields into lepton fields or vice-versa, since a direct lepton-quark coupling is not predicted in the SM.

The reason the weak interaction acts only on left-chirality doublets is explained by the conserved charged associated with the symmetry group $SU(2)_L$: the weak isospin \(I_3\). For left-handed fermions, weak isospin has a value of $I_3=+1/2$ for up-type fermions, and $I_3=-1/2$ for down-type-fermions, while right-handed fermions are associated with isospin $I_3=0$. According to this, it is notable that the right-handed neutrino (and the left-handed anti-neutrino) is sterile, because it does not carry charge for any of the interactions described by the SM: no electric charge, no weak isospin, and no color.

The weak Lagrangian $L_{WK}$ can be separated into left-handed and right-handed components, expressed as:
%decir que la dependencia de x está omitida? igual no ponerlo descompuesto, sino el total usando la 1.10 de Luca?

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{WK} = i\,\bar{\psi}_L \gamma_\mu D^\mu \psi_L + i\,\bar{\psi}_R \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi_R + i\,\bar{\psi}'_R \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi'_R - \frac{1}{4} W^i_{\mu\nu} W_i^{\mu\nu}
\label{weak_lagrangian}
\end{equation}

\noindent where as in the QCD Lagrangian, the last term corresponds to the kinetic term of the boson fields, and the covariant derivative, which is introduced to ensure gauge invariance, is described as follows:

\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i g_W\, T_i\, W_\mu^i
\label{weak_covariant_derivative}
\end{equation}

\noindent being \(g_W\) the weak coupling constant, \(W^i_\mu(i=1,2,3)\) are the three gauge fields, and \(T^i_\mu=\sigma_i/2\), with \(\sigma_i\) the Pauli's matrices. The tensor field \(W^i_{\mu\nu}\) associated to the weak force carrier is then defined as:

\begin{equation}
    W_{\mu\nu}^i = \partial_\mu W_\nu^i - \partial_\nu W_\mu^i + i g \epsilon_{ijk} W_{\mu j} , W_{\nu k}
\label{weak_strenth_tensor}
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(\epsilon_{ijk}\), knows as Levi-Civita tensor, signifies the structure constants of $SU(2)_L$. The last term of the strength field tensor appears as a direct consequence of non-abelian group, leading to cubic and quartic interaction terms between gauge fields.

It is essential to acknowledge that the Lagrangian lacks a mass term since the field transform differently based on their chirality, and consequently, a term \(m\overline{\psi}\psi=m(\overline{\psi_R}\psi_L+\overline{\psi_L}\psi_R)\) does not respect invariance under $SU(2)_L$ transformations. Thus, within this formulation, local gauge invariance is only preserved by the mass term being omitted. This would imply massless fermions and vector bosons, which is in stark contradiction with experimentally observed phenomena. Furthermore, in the formulation of the weak interaction, the physical fields \(W^{\pm}_\mu\) and $Z_\mu$ that give rise to the corresponding gauge bosons \(W^{\pm}\) and $Z$ do not correspond directly to the three gauge fields \(W^i_\mu(i=1,2,3)\).

% electroweak unification
The formalism that addresses the incongruity between theoretical and observable findings consists of unifying electromagnetic and weak interactions, wherein the group $SU(2)_L\otimes U(1)_Y$ becomes the fundamental symmetry of the system. In the context of the unification, the mechanism responsible for electroweak breaking is postulated to elucidate the process by which gauge fields are reorganized, thereby giving rise to the physical fields that are observed in interactions, as will be further explored in the upcoming discussion.

Before spontaneous symmetry breaking, the fundamental quantities conserved are the weak isospin $T_3$ and the weak hypercharge $Y$. The linear combination of these quantities yields the electromagnetic charge conserved after symmetry breaking, $Q$, according to the Gell-Mann-Nishijima expression:

\begin{equation}
    Q=T_3+\frac{Y}{2}
\label{GMN}
\end{equation}

\noindent which illustrates the correlation between the hypercharge group, $U(1)_Y$, and the electromagnetic sector $U(1)_{em}$. Therefore, the $U(1)_Y$ group emerges as the fundamental group of unification inspired by QED. The Lagrangian $L_Y$ can then be written as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_Y = i\,\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu D_\mu \psi - \frac{1}{4} B_{\mu\nu} B^{\mu\nu}
\label{Y_lagrangian}
\end{equation}

\noindent where:

\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i\, g_Y\, Y\, B_\mu
\label{Y_cov_der}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the covariant derivative of $U(1)_Y$, $g_Y$ is identified as the coupling constant and the strength tensor field $B_{\mu\nu}$ is introduced as a condition for gauge invariance conditions, described as follows:

\begin{equation}
    B_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu B_\nu - \partial_\nu B_\mu
\label{Y_strength_tensor}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $B_{\mu\nu}$ couples equally to the left- and right-handed components of the fermion fields, which, recalling expression \eqref{weak_covariant_derivative}, ultimately leading to the two covariant derivatives of the electromagnetic and weak interactions:


\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
D_{\mu,L} = \partial_{\mu}
+ i g\, T_i\, W^i_{\mu}
+ i g'\, \frac{Y}{2}\, B_{\mu} \\
D_{\mu,R} = \partial_{\mu}
+ i g'\, \frac{Y}{2}\, B_{\mu}
\label{WY_covariant_derivatives}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}


In light of the aforementioned elements, the final expression of the electroweak (EWK) Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_{EWK}$ introduced to guarantee local gauge invariance under the two fundamental symmetry $SU(2)_L\otimes U(1)_Y$ transformations, can be written as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{EWK} = \bar{\Psi}_L \left( i \slashed{D}_L \right) \Psi_L + \bar{\psi}_R \left( i \slashed{D}_R \right) \psi_R + \bar{\psi}'_R \left( i \slashed{D}_R \right) \psi'_R - \frac{1}{4} W^i_{\mu\nu} W^{i\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{4} B_{\mu\nu} B^{\mu\nu}
\label{EW_Lagrangian}
\end{equation}

\noindent which can be expressed as a combination of different contributions:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{EWK}=\mathcal{L}_{free} + \mathcal{L}_{CC} + \mathcal{L}_{NC} + \mathcal{L}_{kin}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The free term $\mathcal{L}_{free}$ describes the propagation of fermion fields in the absence of any interactions.

    \item The term $\mathcal{L}_{CC}$ refers to charged current interactions, which couples the fermionic fields $\psi_L$ and $\psi'_L$. The fields that mediate the interaction manifest as linear combinations of the gauge fields of the $SU(2)_L$ interaction, \(W^3_\mu\):

    \begin{equation}
        W^{\pm}_{\mu} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( W^{1}_{\mu} \mp i\, W^{2}_{\mu} \right)
    \end{equation}

    \noindent and corresponds to the physical $W^{\pm}_{\mu}$ fields associated with the charged $W^{\pm}$ bosons that interact with the fermions.

    \item A neutral current term $\mathcal{L}_{NC}$ interacting with neutral fields that can be expressed in terms of the physical field, $Z_{\mu}$, associated with the neutral boson $Z$, and the photon field, $A_{\mu}$, trough a linear superposition of the gauge fields \(W^3_\mu\) and $B_\mu$, according to:

    \begin{equation}
        \begin{pmatrix} A_\mu \\ Z_\mu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta_W & \sin\theta_W \\ -\sin\theta_W & \cos\theta_W \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} B_\mu \\ W^3_\mu \end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation}

    \noindent where $\theta_W$ is the Weinberg angle, a free parameter of the SM that parametrizes the mixing of gauge fields to create the observables of neutral current interactions, $Z_{\mu}$ and $A_{\mu}$. The Weinberg angle is defined in terms of the coupling constants for the weak and electromagnetic groups as follows:

    \begin{equation}
        \theta_W = \arctan\!\left( \frac{g_W}{g_Y} \right)
    \end{equation}

    \item The EWK Lagrange equation is finally complete with the addition of the kinetic terms $\mathcal{L}_{kin}$ for the gauge fields, whose behavior is determined by the field strengths $W^i_{\mu\nu}$ and $B_{\mu\nu}$ previously introduced at \eqref{weak_strenth_tensor} and \eqref{Y_strength_tensor}. Once more, the introduction of the kinetic terms corresponds to a rich self-interaction structurer of the gauge bosons: the existence of trilinear and quadrilinear self-couplings of the bosons $W^{\pm}$, $Z$ and $\gamma$ is predicted by the theory.

\end{itemize}

Up to this point, it has been assumed that bosons and fermions have no mass, since the addition of mass terms in the EWK Lagrangian would break gauge invariance. Therefore, direct boson and fermion mass terms are not permitted in the theory. However, this assumption is not supported by experimental observation, with the exception of the $\gamma$ boson and gluons. In order to account for the collected evidence while maintaining gauge invariance, the mechanism proposed in 1964 by R. Brout, F. Englert, and P. Higgs is relied on, as it provides an explanation of the mass acquisition through the spontaneous symmetry breaking of the electroweak symmetry.


\subsection{The Brout-Englert-Higgs-Mechanism}
\label{subsec:BEH}
%
Given that the EWK theory as it stands fails to account for the mass of electroweak bosons, there is a clear need to incorporate additional mass terms into the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_{EWK}$. The origin of this additional degree of freedom, corresponding to the mass term, is a direct consequence of the so-called electroweak symmetry breaking, which occurs when the potential of a system is invariant under symmetry transformations, but its individual ground states are not.

In SM, spontaneous symmetry breaking takes place through the Brout-Englert-Higgs (BEH) mechanism {\color{red} [ref]}. This mechanism elucidates the process by which the masses of gauge bosons and fermions are generated upon the breaking of the electroweak symmetry, $SU(2)_L\otimes U(1)_Y$, at low energies. According to Goldstone's theorem {\color{red} [ref]}, for each generator of a continuous global symmetry that breaks spontaneously, a scalar field associated with a massless spin-0 boson (Goldstone boson) appears. In a theory with local gauge symmetry, gauge bosons and Goldstone bosons recombine to form massive bosons, thereby giving rise to the Higgs mechanism. The breaking of EWK symmetry is therefore based on the introduction of a weak isospin doublet, whose potential has a non-trivial minimum, leading to spontaneous symmetry breaking. The new complex scalar $SU(2)_L$ doublet $\phi$, known as the Higgs field, is written as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \phi = \begin{pmatrix} \phi^{+} \\ \phi^{0} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} \phi_{1} + i\,\phi_{2} \\ \phi_{3} + i\,\phi_{4} \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\phi^+$ is a charge field with $I_3=+1/2$, $\phi^0$ is a neutral field with $I_3=-1/2$. It is imperative that the field be scalar in order to satisfy spacial isotropy; otherwise, the expected value on the vacuum would be frame-dependent. Furthermore, the expectation value in the vacuum must be constant to satisfy homogeneity. Given that the hypercharge of the field is $Y_{\phi}=1$, and according to expression \eqref{WY_covariant_derivatives}, the covariant derivative is described by:

\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu - ig\,W^i_\mu \frac{\sigma_i}{2} - \frac{1}{2} ig' B_\mu
\end{equation}

Consequently, the BEH Lagrangian of the complex scalar field $\phi$ can be written as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{BEH} = (D_\mu \phi)^\dagger (D^\mu \phi) - V(\phi^\dagger \phi)
\end{equation}

\noindent where the scalar potential $V(\phi^\dagger \phi)$ is selected with a specific form that induces spontaneous symmetry breaking, meaning the potential has multiple ground states with a vacuum expectation value that is different from zero. Therefore, the symmetry breaking occurs when the system selects one of these vacuum states as the minimum energy state. The potential that satisfies this condition is given by the following form:

\begin{equation}
    V(\phi^\dagger \phi) = -\mu^2\,\phi^\dagger \phi + \lambda\,(\phi^\dagger \phi)^2 \quad
    \label{higgs_potential}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu^2$ and $\lambda$ are the constants of the model. The possible values that the parameters $\mu^2$ and $\lambda$ can take to ensure the stability of the theory, i.e., ensuring that the potential is bounded from below, are outlined next. In the free case $\lambda=0$, the stability condition is verified only if $\mu^2<0$. On the alternative scenario where $\lambda>0$, the two possibilities $\mu^2>0$ or $\mu^2<0$ are allowed. In the case of $\mu^2<0$, the potential is a concave function with a minimum at the origin $V(\phi_0=0)=0$. This minimum is unique and symmetric under phase transformations, so the system is said to have exact symmetry. Conversely, if $\mu^2>0$, the potential adopts a \textit{Mexican hat} shape, as illustrated in \ref{fig:SM_particles}. In this scenario, the potential at $V(\phi_0=0)=0$ becomes an unstable local maximum, while the minimum is found for the doublet that satisfies:

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/chapter_I/higgs-champ.png}
    \caption{Shape of the Higgs potential \eqref{higgs_potential} for the case $\mu^2>0$: the origin becomes an unestable local maximum and the symmetry-breaking minimum at $|\phi_0|=v/\sqrt2$}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
    \phi^\dagger \phi = \frac{\mu^2}{2\lambda} \equiv \frac{v^2}{2}
\end{equation}

\noindent being $v$ the Vacuum Expectation Value (VEV). Because the Lagrangian is invariant under $SU(2)_L$ and $U(1)_Y$ phase transformations, the potential has an infinite number of degenerate minimum states:

\begin{equation}
    V(\phi_0)=-\frac{\lambda}{4}v^4
\end{equation}

\noindent whose solution correspond to $\phi_0=(v/\sqrt{2})e^{i\theta}$, which results in a circle centered at the origin. This degeneration of states with minimum potential is the underlying cause of symmetry breaking, since the application of a phase transformation in a specific point on the circle designated as the ground state results in another distinct minimum.

Under the assumption that the Higgs potential adopts the form given by $\mu^2,\lambda>0$, among all possible states, the ground state may be expressed, without loss of generality, as:

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\text{VEV}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\noindent This choice entails the breaking of both the $SU(2)_L$ and $U(1)_Y$ symmetries, while preserving the $U(1)_{em}$ symmetry, according to the following scheme:

\begin{equation}
    SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y\rightarrow U(1)_{em}
\end{equation}

\noindent From which it follows that three of the four generators are broken spontaneously, thus yielding three massless Goldstone bosons in accordance with the Goldstone theorem.

The spectrum of the particles can be determined by introducing small excitations to the Higgs field $\phi$ around the ground state, or the VEV. This field expansion around the minimum can be parametrized as:

\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = \exp\!\left( \frac{i\,\sigma_i}{2}\,\theta^i(x) \right) \frac{1} {\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v + H(x) \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $H(x)$ and $\theta^i(x)$ are four real fields that represent the small perturbations. The three real fields $\theta^i(x)$ are associated with the Goldstone bosons; however, these bosons are not observed in Nature. The freedom to perform $SU(2)_L$ gauge transformations on $\phi$ can be used to absorb the three Goldstone bosons:

\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v + H(x) \end{pmatrix}
    \label{phi_parametrization}
\end{equation}

After these transformations, any unphysical dependence on $\theta^i(x)$ is canceled, and the sole remaining field is the $H(x)$ field. This $H(x)$ field is associated with the massive boson, known as the Higgs boson, thereby completing the SM theory. Consequently, the BEH Lagrangian can be expressed as a function of the Higgs boson and ultimately assumes the following form:

\begin{align}
&\mathcal{L}_{\text{BEH}} = \tfrac12\,\partial_\mu H\,\partial^\mu H + \tfrac{g_w^2 v^2}{4} W^+_\mu W^{-\mu} + \tfrac12 \tfrac{g_w^2 + g_Y^2}{4} v^2 Z_\mu Z^\mu + \tfrac12 \mu^2 H^2
\label{eq:beh1}\\
&\tfrac{g_w^2}{2} v H\, W^+_\mu W^{-\mu} + \tfrac{g_Y^2}{2} v H\, Z_\mu Z^\mu
\label{eq:beh2}\\
&\tfrac{g_w^2}{2} H^2 W^+_\mu W^{-\mu} + \tfrac{g_Y^2}{4} H^2 Z_\mu Z^\mu
\label{eq:beh3}\\
&\tfrac{\mu^2}{v} H^3 + \tfrac{\mu^2}{4 v^2} H^4
\label{eq:beh4}
\end{align}

\noindent where it is demonstrated that the degrees of freedom associated with the $\theta^i(x)$ fields become longitudinal degrees of fredom, thereby giving rise to the massive terms of the $W^{\pm}$, $Z$ and $H$ bosons in \eqref{eq:beh1}:

\begin{equation}
    M_Z = \sqrt{\frac{g_{w}^{2} + g_{Y}^{2}}{2}}\, v \qquad M_{W^\pm} = \frac{g_{w} v}{2} = M_Z \cos\theta_W \qquad M_H = 2\sqrt{\lambda}v=\sqrt{2}\,\mu
\end{equation}

\noindent from where it can be inferred that the Higgs mass is not predicted in the SM, as $\lambda$ is a free parameter of the theory. The absence of terms involving the photon field, $A_{\mu}$, indicates the fact that the $U(1)_{em}$ symmetry is preserved, thereby confirming the photon's masslessness. The remaining terms in the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_{BEH}$ describe the interactions between bosons. The terms in \eqref{eq:beh2} and \eqref{eq:beh3} corresponds to the trilinear and quadrilinear interaction respectively between the $H$ and the gauge bosons, $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$, that can be written under the forms:

\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{V} = \frac{2}{v}\, M_{V}^{2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lambda_{2V} = \frac{1}{v^{2}}\, M_{V}^{2}
\end{equation}

\noindent where in this context $V$ is used to denote \textit{vector} and indicates the vector bosons, $W^{\pm}$ or $Z$.

Meanwhile, \eqref{eq:beh4} illustrates the trilinear and quadrilinear self-interactions of the Higgs $H$, defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
 \lambda_{HHH} = \lambda_{HHHH} = \frac{M_H^{2}}{v^{2}}
    \label{higgs_self_coupling}
\end{equation}

According to these definitions, the Higgs potential reveals a more convenient formulation:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    V(H) & = 2\mu^{2} H^{2} + \frac{\mu^{2}}{v} H^{3} + \frac{\mu^{2}}{4v} H^{4} = \\
& = \frac{1}{2} M_H^{2} H^{2} + \lambda_{HHH} v H^{3} + \lambda_{HHHH} H^{4}
    \label{higgs_pot}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The presence of the Higgs self-interaction terms in $\mathcal{L}_{BEH}$ \eqref{eq:beh4} implies that double and triple Higgs production is predicted in the SM, while \eqref{higgs_self_coupling} and \eqref{higgs_pot} evidences that it is directly related to the parameters of the scalar potential and entirely determined by the Higgs mass at the VEV. Measuring the double Higgs production process, therefore, represents a crucial test of validity of the SM and the EWSB.


From the potential form \eqref{higgs_potential} it is also noteworthy that the constant term that appears, while not affecting the SM, contributes to vacuum energy, which is associated with the cosmological constant that determines the curvature of the Universe. The value predicted by the SM for this constant is incompatible with astronomical observations, thus constituting a dilemma that necessitates a proper quantum theory of gravity or a mechanism to reduce the Higgs field vacuum energy density.

At this stage, the free parameters of the BEH mechanism are the VEV, $v$, and the mass of the Higgs, $M_H$. The former corresponds to the energy scale of the electroweak symmetry, which can be estimated from the relation between the mass of the $W^{\pm}$ boson and the Fermi constant, $G_F$, that is precisely determined from the muon lifetime:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{G_F}{\sqrt{2}} = \left( \frac{g}{2\sqrt{2}} \right)^{2} \frac{1}{M_W^{2}} \;\rightarrow\; v = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,G_F}} \approx 246\,\text{GeV}
\end{equation}

Thus far, the mechanism that creates the mass terms of vector and scalar bosons has been detailed. However, the assumption that fermions are massless has persisted until now. To understand how their mass is generate, it is necessary to introduce the Yukawa Lagrangian density as an additional term to the SM Lagrangian. As the Yukawa interaction couples the left and right chiral fields, it can be expressed using the notation in \eqref{fermionic_fields}:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Yukawa}} = -y_f \left( \bar{\Psi}_L \phi\, \psi'_R + \bar{\psi}'_R \phi^\dagger \Psi_L \right) - y'_f \left( \bar{\Psi}_L \sigma_2 \phi^{*}\, \psi'_R + \bar{\psi}'_R \sigma_2 \phi^{*\,\dagger} \Psi_L \right)
    \label{yukawa_lagrangian}
\end{equation}

\noindent being $y_f$ and $y_f'$ the coupling constants for the two components of the fermion $SU(2)_L$ doublets: the component $I_3=-1/2$ couples to the Higgs field $\phi$, while the conjugated field $\phi^*$ is introduced to allow the coupling of the component $I_3=+1/2$. After EWSB, $\phi^*$ takes the form:

\begin{equation}
    \phi^{*} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} v + H \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
    \label{phi_conjugated}
\end{equation}

By injecting the scalar doublet $\phi$ parametrized as in \eqref{phi_parametrization}, and the newly introduced $\phi^*$ in \eqref{phi_conjugated}, the Yukawa Lagrangian can be written more conveniently as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Yukawa}} = -\sum_f M_f \left( \bar{\psi}_L \psi_R + \bar{\psi}_R \psi_L \right)\left( 1 + \frac{H}{v} \right)
\end{equation}

\noindent where the fermionic mass term $M_f$ explicitly appear. In this formulation, the fermions mass generation is explained by the Yukawa interaction of the Higgs field with the fermions, and it is proportional to the strength of the Yukawa couplings $y_f$ and to the VEV $v$ according to:

\begin{equation}
    M_f = y_f\,\frac{v}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{equation}

In essence, the BEH mechanism resolves the inconsistency of the electroweak theory by introducing a new Higgs boson scalar field with a non-zero VEV, thereby providing bosons and fermions with mass. Following the symmetry breaking, the scalar field generates Goldstone bosons, which are absorbed by the $W^{\pm}$ and $Z$ gauge fields and become their longitudinal degree of freedom, resulting in their mass. Additionally, the Higgs boson couples to the left- and right-handed components of the fermionic fields in a Yukawa interaction, determining the masses of the fermions trough a purely quantum-relativistic mechanism. The interaction between the Higgs boson, vector bosons and fermions are governed by the corresponding gauge and Yukawa couplings, together with the VEV $v$, which constitute the fundamental free parameters of the theory. After EWSB, these parameters establish the masses of all SM particles, revealing that the Higgs interactions with them are found to be proportional to their masses. Consequently, the Higgs boson decays preferentially into the heaviest particles that are kinematically accessible, as will be detailed subsequently.

\subsection{Phenomenology of the Higgs boson}
\label{subsec:H_phenomenology}
%
Despite the theoretical robustness of the proposed BEH mechanism in 1964, experimental confirmation did not occur until July 4, 2012, when the CMS and ATLAS collaborations at the LHC claimed the discovery of a new particle compatible with the Higgs boson predicted by the SM {\color{red} [ref]}. The discovery was accomplished independently by the two experiments utilizing data collected during LHC Run I at center-of-mass energies of $\sqrt{s}=7$ and $\sqrt{s}=8$ TeV. The experimental observation of the Higgs boson led to the confirmation of the BEH mechanism as a part of the SM, and the measurement of its mass, with a value of approximately $m_H=125$ GeV, allowed the final free parameter of the theory to be determined.

In the wake of the recent discovery, the scientific community is now directing its efforts toward characterizing the new particle, with the aim of furthering the exploration of the EWSB. A thorough investigation is necessary to make precise measurements of the Higgs properties: measurements of the Higgs couplings via exclusive production modes and decay channels, its spin-parity, and its differential production cross section must be performed and contrasted with the predictions of the SM. The following paragraphs provide an overview of the results of more than a dozen years of research at the LHC on the characterization of the Higgs boson phenomenology.

Several mechanisms contribute to the production of the Higgs boson. The dominant mechanism at the LHC ($\sqrt{s} = 13$ TeV) is gluon-gluon fusion (ggF), which proceeds through a heavy quark loop and has a cross section of about 49 pb. This is followed by vector boson fusion (VBF) production, which has a cross section of 3.8 pb, about 10 times smaller. In the VBF mechanism, two vector bosons interact to produce a Higgs boson, which in the final state appears alongside two high-momentum quarks, very close to the direction of the incident particles — the experimental signature that characterizes the process. The next mechanism is associated production with a single vector boson ($VH, V=W^{\pm},Z$), with a cross section of 2.3 pb. The study of the rare VH and VBF production mechanisms allows for a deeper dive into the Higgs boson's coupling to vector bosons. Lastly, there is associated production with heavy quarks: top pair ($t\overline{t}H$), bottom pair ($b\overline{b}H$, or single top ($tH$), which benefits from the Higgs boson's enhanced coupling with the heaviest fermions. The cross sections for these latter processes are around one order of magnitude smaller than the previous mechanisms. The $t\overline{t}H$ mechanism is of particular interest because it enables the direct determination of the top Yukawa coupling, $y_t$, as opposed to the indirect determination from ggF.

\noindent \textbf{Decay modes}

\noindent The observed mass value of the Higgs boson is a genuine natural phenomenon: it lies at the intersection of all significant decay modes, preventing any single mode from dominating and enabling an exceptionally rich range of experimental channels. Thus, at a mass of 125 GeV, the Higgs boson decays into a variety of SM particles. The probability of its decay into each final state is described by the branching fractions, or branching ratios (BR), which are shown as a function of Higgs boson mass in the Figure \ref{fig:H_br}. The SM Higgs boson couples to vector bosons ($V$) with an amplitude proportional to the gauge boson mass squared $m^2_V$, and to fermions ($f$) with amplitude proportional to the fermion mass $m_f$:

\begin{equation}
    k_V \sim \frac{m_V^2}{v^2} \qquad k_f \sim \frac{m_f}{v}
\label{H_couples}
\end{equation}

Consequently, Higgs coupling is enhanced for heavy particles. As presented in Figure \ref{fig:H_br}, the decay probabilities indicate that for a Higgs boson with a mass of 125 GeV, the most probable decay modes are $H\rightarrow bb$ and $H\rightarrow WW$, followed by $H\rightarrow gg$ and $H\rightarrow \tau\tau$, which involve vector bosons and third-generation fermions. Higgs decays into massless particles do not occur directly; rather, they require quantum loop-induced processes, which are in fact a key tool for investigating physics at energies higher than those directly reachable.

\noindent \textbf{Experimental status}

\noindent The Run I discovery of the Higgs boson was performed inclusively for all production modes, and led by the combination of high-resolution $H\rightarrow ZZ \rightarrow 4l$ and $H\rightarrow\gamma\gamma$ channels, which allowed for a precise determination of $m_H$. After the discovery claim, the analysis of the angular distributions in the $H\rightarrow ZZ \rightarrow 4l$ decay channel confirmed the spin $J$ and parity $P$ of the newly discovered particle, $J^P=0^+$, ruling out all alternative hypothesis at 99.9\% confidence level. Combining various decay channels also allowed for precise measurements of decay rates and coupling strengths, all consistent with SM predictions. Continued research into Higgs boson properties has advanced thanks to the LHC's exceptional performance, the robustness of the experiments, and the development of advanced experimental techniques. The latest findings on Higgs boson couplings, as measured by the CMS collaboration, are summarized in Figure \ref{fig:H_coupling}. Thus far, all observations confirm that the couplings with the $W$ and $Z$ gauge bosons scale quadratically with their masses and that the couplings with third-generation charged fermions and muons depend linearly on their masses.

Thus far, the exploration of Higgs boson properties has revealed that it behaves in a manner consistent with the SM. Although constraints on its couplings partially depend on assumptions about beyond SM contributions and still allow for possible deviations, the scalar boson discovered 12 years ago is now precisely characterized and compatible with a SM Higgs boson. Experimentally, it has been confirmed that the Higgs mechanism breaks degeneracy between the three fermion families by coupling proportionally to their mass. However, there is still a crucial element missing from the full picture: the next fundamental stage in validating the EWSB mechanism is determining the Higgs boson's coupling to itself. The self-coupling value can be derived in the SM from \eqref{higgs_self_coupling}, $\lambda_{HHH}\approx0.13$, fully determined by $m_H$ and $v$.

Experimentally, the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ parameter is accessible at leading order (LO) from Higgs boson pair production rates, and at next-to-leading order (NLO) from cross-section measurements of single Higgs boson production, thanks to radiative NLO electroweak corrections arising from the trilinear coupling. This underscores the key importance of searches for HH production. Furthermore, the experimental determination of HH production has profound implications for BSM theories that introduce additional effective terms into the H potential, given that the kinematic and HH production rates are highly sensitive to new physics. Deviations from SM predictions may indicate the presence of extensions in the scalar sector, ranging from models with additional doublets or singlets to frameworks such as compositeness or supersymmetry. Therefore, directly determining the self-coupling value from HH production constitutes an essential closure test of the BEH mechanism and ultimately probes SM consistency.

\subsection{Limitations of the Standard Model}
\label{subsec:SM_limitations}
The relevance of characterizing SM consistency is supported by a substantial body of theoretical considerations and experimental evidence that underscores the existence of physics beyond the SM that has yet to be discovered. Despite the remarkable success in theoretically describing all observed particle fields, the SM falls short in addressing fundamental unsolved problems in Nature, such as the matter-antimatter asymmetry observed in the Universe, nor the existence of a particle species compatible with the dark matter, which is observed from its gravitational effects. Moreover, the present SM formalism is capable of accounting for only three of the fundamental interactions, excluding the gravitational force. In addition to these limitations, the SM is unable to provide an explanation for the experimentally observed neutrino oscillations, which require leptonic masses and mixing that are not covered by the SM theory.

In the electroweak sector, the SM fails to explain three main theoretical elements that are closely related to the physics of the Higgs boson. Firstly, the current theory does not provide an answer to the question of the origin of the three families of fermions, which are identical in all respects except for their coupling to the Higgs boson, spanning several orders of magnitude. Secondly, the hierarchy problem arises from the value ascribed to the Higgs boson mass. Given the Planck scale ($\sim10^{19}$ GeV), the Higgs boson mass should assume high values, due to quadratically divergent radiative corrections that tend to push the mass towards values much larger than the observed $m_{H}$, suggesting the existence of a mechanism to stabilize it and avoid unnatural fine-tuning. Finally, the validity of the theory up to the Planck scale can only be assured if the scalar potential is bounded from below, thereby guaranteeing vacuum stability. In view of the observed values of $m_H$ and $m_t$, the SM is inclined towards a state of metastability in the scalar potential \cite{EliasMiro:2011aa}, which suggests that the stability of electroweak vacuum may be questionable at very high energies. At such scales, the behavior of the Higgs potential is also relevant for cosmology since certain scenarios propose that the Higgs field could have acted as the inflaton in the early Universe \cite{Bezrukov:2007ep,Bezrukov:2013fka}. The question of vacuum stability and the viability of Higgs-driven inflation are dependent on the shape of the scalar potential, which is determined by the running value of the BEH potential parameter $\lambda$. This parameter is intimately connected to the Higgs boson self-coupling, which is the primary subject of investigation in this thesis.

In this regard, the Standard Model is naturally conceived as a part of a broader theoretical framework that extends beyond it, operating below the Planck scale and providing a resolution to the aforementioned shortcomings of the SM. Consequently, the existence of beyond the SM physics may offer solutions to the limitations of the current theory, while preserving its remarkable success in explaining the phenomena observed in collider experiments.

When measuring possible deviations from SM predictions, it is possible to distinguish between two different approaches depending on the energy scale of the new physics, If BSM physics operates at energies accessible to collider experiments, the new states or particles can be directly produced and experimentally tested. In this case, the presence of new physics would manifest as a new resonance that enhance the SM process cross section for a given value of the invariant mass $m_X$. Conversely, if the energy scale of the new physics is beyond the current experimental reach, it si possible to detect the effects of this new physics indirectly though hypothetical deviations from the SM predictions in the process cross section or kinematics. A graphical representation of these two approaches can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:BSMsearches}. In indirect searches, new results can be interpreted using effective Lagrangians with higher-dimensional operators designed to capture potential BSM effects. In this context, the SM becomes an Effective Field Theory (EFT), which serves as a low-energy approximation of a more comprehensive theory. This approach is not dependent on a specific BSM model, making it a highly versatile tool for exploring unknown aspects of BSM physics.

\section{Higgs boson pair production}
\noindent Higgs boson pair production (HH) represents one of the primary goals of the LHC program in the coming decades precisely because it provides direct access to the Higgs sector. The HH production rate depends directly on the trilinear self-coupling, $\lambda_{HHH}$, which, according to Equation \eqref{higgs_pot}, determines the structure of the Higgs potential. Therefore, the study of HH production is directly linked to the $\lambda_{HHH}$ parameter, whose crucial role has been strongly justified in this context.

Conversely, the trilinear coupling is not the only interaction that contributes to Higgs boson pair production. In addition to the Higgs self-coupling, which is often considered the primary driver of HH searches, HH production provides a means of testing other SM parameters. Specific production modes involve the Yukawa coupling to the top quark, $y_t$, as well as the trilinear and quadrilinear couplings with vector bosons, $\lambda_V$ and $\lambda_{2V}$. The produciton mechanisms of an on-shell Higgs boson pair, as detailed in this section, are characterized by different interactions from which the contribution of the trilinear coupling must be disentangled from other effects.

Di-Higgs production is a very rare process in particle colliders: its SM cross-section for proton-proton collisions at center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{13}$ TeV is expected to be $\sigma_{HH} \sim 32.7$ fb, three orders of magnitude smaller than single Higgs production. Precise measurements of couplings involved in HH production are still challenging to perform directly; the trilinear self-coupling is expected to be determined with precision of approximately 30\% by the end of the High Luminosity LHC phase (expected in 2041) {\color{red}[ref]}. Sec. \ref{subsec:SM_production} details all production mechanisms that contribute to SM HH production. Moreover, effects of physics BSM can already be probed with the current LHC configuration (see \ref{sec:LHC}): small deviations of SM values can lead to significant variations in cross-section rates and kinematics of HH production. An overview of these effects is given in Sec. \ref{subsec:BSM_production}.


\subsection{Di-Higgs production in the SM}

The six main mechanisms as a function of collision energy are shown in Figure \ref{fig:HH_xsec}. Ath the LHC, operating at an energy of 13 TeV, the two dominant production mechanisms are fusion of gluons (ggF) and vector bosons (VBF). While experimental research has predominantly focused on these two modes, advancements in experimental apparatus and techniques are gradually adding other production mechanisms to the analysis landscape. The following section provides an in-depth description of the two primary HH production modes, ggF and VBF, and a brief overview of additional production modes that occur at LHC energies.

\noindent \textbf{Gluon fusion $gg\rightarrow HH$}

\noindent Gluon gluon fusion (ggF) is the dominant HH production mode at LHC. Gluons, massless particles, do not interact directly with the Higgs boson; instead, third-generation heavy quarks mediate the interaction through loops. The dominant contribution to the loop comes from the top quark in 99\% of cases, followed by the b quark in the remaining 1\%. Figure \ref{fig:HH_ggF_diagrams} shows the first-order Feynman diagrams involved in the process: in the triangle diagram (\ref{fig:HH_triangle}), an off-shell H boson splits into two Higgs bosons, and in the box diagram (\ref{fig:HH_box}), the two Higgs bosons are radiated from the heavy quarks. Thus, the triangle contribution depends linearly on the Higgs self-coupling parameter $\lambda_{HHH}$ and the Yukawa coupling $y_t$, while the box diagram only depends quadratically on $y_t$.

\noindent As detailed below and shown in Figure \ref{fig:HH_xsec}, SM HH production at the LHC is a very rare process, with a production rate $\mathcal{O}(10^3)$ times smaller than single Higgs production. Consequently, HH experimental searches — and those covered in this thesis — have primarly focused on the dominant mechanisms, ggF and VBF. Since the gluon fusion is the leading production mode, ggF searches provide the highest sensitivy to the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$; on the other hand, VBF HH production provides additional handles for measuring $\lambda_{HHH}$ and allows for exploration of the VVHH interaction.

It has been underscored that the total cross-section of each production mode stems from the interference of its corresponding diagrams. Thus, small SM HH cross-sections result from processes such as destructive interference between the triangle and box diagrams of the ggF mechanism (see Figure \ref{fig:ggf_contributions}). This phenomenon can be interpreted as meaning that the total HH cross-section depends heavily on the value of the Higgs self-coupling, and small deviations from the SM $\lambda_{HHH}$ value can significantly alter the interference, leading to large enhancements in the HH cross-section. Therefore, the focus shifts from the initial conception of HH production as a test of the SM to its interpretation as a probe of BSM physics, as discussed in more detail in the following section.

\subsection{BSM Di-Higgs production: Towards Effective Field Theories}
\label{subsec:BSM_production}
%
The limitations of the SM discussed in Section \ref{subsec:SM_limitations} motivate the search for new physics, and Higgs boson pair production is a particularly suited probe for it. Owing to its direct connection with the scalar potential, any new physics that modifies the scalar sector is expected to leave an imprint on this process, either through new particles that couple to the Higgs boson or through modified interactions among the particles involved in its production. Accordingly, the two complementary approaches illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:BSM_approaches} — direct searches for new states and indirect searches for deviations from the SM predictions — translate into two classes of signatures: resonant and non-resonant Higgs boson pair production.

In the resonant scenario, when BSM physics operates at energies accessible by the collider experiments, a new particle $X$ with a mass $m_X > 2m_H$ is produced directly and decays into a pair of Higgs bosons, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:HH_resonant}, so that the signal appears as a localized excess in the invariant mass spectrum of the pair on top of the non-resonant SM production. Such states arise naturally in many extensions of the SM, including extensions of the scalar sector — the singlet model, the two-Higgs-doublet model or the minimal supersymmetric SM —, which predict additional spin-0 bosons, and models with warped extra dimensions, which predict spin-0 radions and spin-2 Kaluza–Klein gravitons {\color{red}[refs]}. Despite their different theoretical motivations, these models typically predict the existence of a CP-event scalar spin-0 or spin-2 particles, with an intrinsic width negligible compared to the detector resolution, leading to similar experimental signatures. The presence of a common and clear signature allows these models to be probed simultaneously using a model-independent way by scanning a broad range of mass hypotheses, from the kinematic threshold up to several TeV. The search for such resonances in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state with the Run 2 dataset, which covers masses between $250$ and $3000$ GeV \cite{CMS-PAS-B2G-24-011}, relies on the jet assignment algorithm described in Chapter \ref{ch:taggers}, whose adaptation to that search was part of this thesis. Resonant production is otherwise not covered further in this work.

In the non-resonant scenario, which is the focus of this thesis, the new physics lies at an energy scale beyond the direct reach of the LHC, and its effects appear instead at lower energies as modifications of the interactions involved in Higgs boson pair production. These modifications alter both the production rate and the kinematic properties of the pair, so that the experimental signature is not a localized excess but a deformation of the distributions predicted by the SM. Their description is addressed here in three steps of increasing generality. First, the couplings of the SM are rescaled by coupling modifiers, the parametrization in which the results of this thesis are expressed. Second, effective field theories are introduced as the systematic framework that generalizes this approach. Finally, the effective couplings that this framework yields for gluon fusion production are presented, which constitute the basis of the signal modeling developed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}.




In this regard, HH production serves as a probe for BSM physics. While testing the SM only requires computing the cross-section and comparing them with experimental data, exploring the possibility of alternative theories demands understanding how these cross sections vary outside the SM framework. Two scenarios for Higgs pair production arise in BSM physics studies: possible deviations from the SM predictions can be probed either withing a specific theoretical framework or through a model-independent effective field theory (EFT) approach. \subsubsection{Couplings modifiers}
%
In the SM, the value of the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ is entirely determined by the values of $v$ and $m_H$, as shown in Equation \ref{higgs_self_coupling}. To remain sensitive to possible BSM effects, it is instead treated as a free parameter, and its deviations from the SM prediction are parametrized through the self-coupling modifier
%
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_{\lambda} = \frac{\lambda_{HHH}}{\lambda_{HHH}^{SM}},
    \label{eq:kappa_lambda}
\end{equation}
which follows the approach of the $\kappa$-framework, in which each coupling of the Higgs boson is rescaled by a multiplicative factor with respect to its SM value \cite{LHCHiggsCrossSectionWorkingGroup:2013rie}. This is the same framework in which the couplings of Figure \ref{fig:H_coupling} are measured, and expressing the results in terms of $\kappa_\lambda$ allows double and single Higgs boson production — the latter sensitive to $\lambda_{HHH}$ through electroweak loop corrections — to be interpreted consistently and combined. The modifiers of the top quark Yukawa coupling, $\kappa_t = y_t/y_t^{SM}$, and of the couplings to vector bosons, $\kappa_V$ and $\kappa_{2V}$, are defined analogously.

% This coupling rescaling approach is usually referred to as the $\kappa$-framework. The framework is also sensitive to single Higgs measurements affected by deviations from $\lambda_{HHH}$ SM value, due to loop corrections influencing several of these processes. Expressing the experimental results in terms of the coupling modifier allows for the consistent interpretation of both single and double Higgs production, opening the possibility of establishing combined constraints on the value of the trilinear Higgs boson self-coupling.

Modifications in the value of $\lambda_{HHH}$ predicted by the SM have a profound impact on the HH total cross section, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:HH_xsec_vs_kl} for all HH production mechanisms covered in \ref{subsec:SM_production}.

\end{figure}

The interference between the diagrams that contribute to HH production results in a minimum of the cross section that is not centered at $\kappa_{\lambda} = 0$, but rather at a shifted value. Gluon fusion production, in particular, shows strong dependence of its cross section on the value of $\kappa_{\lambda}$. Including the top quark Yukawa coupling modifier, $\kappa_t$, the ratio HH ggF production cross section to its SM prediction can be parametrized at leading order in terms of the square of the amplitude of the box and triangle diagrams~\cite{Carvalho:2016rys}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\sigma_{HH}}{\sigma_{HH}^{SM}} = 0.28\kappa_{\lambda}^2\kappa_t^2-1.37\kappa_{\lambda}\kappa_t^3+2.09 \kappa_t^4
    \label{eq:kl_kt_ggf}
\end{equation}
%
which shows a minimum at $\kappa_{\lambda}/\kappa_t = 2.45$.

A similar approach can be followed for the VBF production, whose cross section depends on $\kappa_{V}$, $\kappa_{2V}$ and $\kappa_{\lambda}$. %
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sigma(\kappa_V, \kappa_{2V}, \kappa_\lambda) \sim\;&
0.9\, \kappa_V^{2}\, \kappa_\lambda^{2}
+ 31.4\, \kappa_V^{4}
+ 16.5\, \kappa_{2V}^{2} \\
& - 8.6\, \kappa_V^{3}\, \kappa_\lambda
+ 5.5\, \kappa_V\, \kappa_{2V}\, \kappa_\lambda
- 44.0\, \kappa_V^{2}\, \kappa_{2V}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
%
where, in fact, the sensitivity to the quartic coupling $\kappa_{2V}$ is much greater than that to modifications of the trilinear self-coupling, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:VBF_modifications}. Furthermore, the $\kappa_{2V}$ coupling is particularly relevant for providing information on the deep structure of the Higgs sector and the nautre of EWSB since, in many BSM models, the standard relation $\kappa_{2V}=\kappa_{V}^2$ no longer holds.

\subsubsection{Effective Field Theories}
%
Up to now, in non-resonant indirect searches, the self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ has been treated as a free parameter, and deviations from the SM prediction are parametrized via coupling strength modifiers within the $\kappa$-framework. This approach enables the simultaneous exploration of a wide range of BSM scenarios, and allows the results to be reinterpreted within specific models. A generalization of this approach with a systematic method is provided by the effective field theories (EFTs): the EFT formalism permits the coherent and gauge-invariant incorporation of possible corrections to SM predictions. Within this framework, modifications to the Higgs self-coupling and other relevant interactions are organized into a expansion of higher-dimensional operators. Thus, EFTs offer a practical theoretical framework for interpreting experimental results and potentially connecting them to comprehensive UV models. Furthermore, the EFT approach is not dependent on a specific BSM model, thereby making it a versatile tool for investigating unknown aspects of BSM physics.

Accordingly, new physics at a scale $\Lambda \gg v$ can be described by incorporating additional fields into the SM Lagrangian that only act at extremely short distances and high energies, meaning their internal structure can be disregarded within the current LHC range. In practice, this entails describing physics at scales below $\Lambda$ by integrating the heavy fields of an extended renormalizable theory that interact only at high energies. Therefore, from a \textit{bottom-up} perspective, $\Lambda$ can be interpreted as the scale up to which only SM fields propagate, while from a \textit{top-down} approach, it corresponds to the scale of the new physics. The resulting effective theory is not renormalizable; however, this is not problematic since an EFT only describes the low-energy manifestation of a more fundamental, renormalizable theory defined at higher scales. Consequently, this procedure generates higher-dimensional, non-renormalizable operators that capture the residual effects of those fields in the low-energy regime.

There is not a single way to formulate such an effective description. The criterion used to expand the SM Lagrangian, and the symmetries imposed on the new theory and its fields, lead to the definition of different EFTs. In the context of Higgs boson pair searches, two main descriptions are used in the literature, and they differ precisely in the assumption they make about the nature of the observed scalar \cite{Alasfar:2023xpc}. According to the SM, the physical Higgs boson and the three Goldstone bosons absorbed by the $W$ and $Z$ bosons form a single $SU(2)_L$ doublet, as described in Section \ref{subsec:BEH}. However, the only experimentally established facts are the existence of those three Goldstone bosons and of a light neutral scalar with a mass of about $125$ GeV and couplings compatible with the SM predictions, while whether this scalar is the fourth component of the same doublet or an electroweak singlet remains a hypothesis rather than a measurement. The Standard Model EFT (SMEFT) adopts the first possibility, assuming that the Higgs boson belongs to a doublet and organizing the new physics effects in a linear, gauge-invariant expansion in higher-dimensional operators, whereas the Higgs EFT (HEFT) adopts the second, treating the Higgs boson as an electroweak singlet and realizing the electroweak symmetry non-linearly, which allows for a very general parametrization of the deviations in its interactions. Both lead to internally consistent, gauge-invariant Lagrangians, but they differ in their assumptions and in the correlations they impose among the couplings of the Higgs boson.

SMEFT retains the Higgs boson as part of an $SU(2)_L$ doublet, so that the electroweak symmetry is realized linearly, exactly as in the SM. Its expansion is therefore organized according to the canonical dimension $D$ of the operators, each additional order being suppressed by a further power of the scale $\Lambda$ of the new physics:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SMEFT}}
    = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}^{(D\leq4)}
    + \sum_{D>4}\sum_i \frac{c_i}{\Lambda^{D-4}}\, \mathcal{O}_i^{(D)},
    \label{eq:eft_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}_i^{(D)}$ are operators of dimension $D$ built from the SM fields and invariant under $SU(3)_C\times SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$ local transformations, $\Lambda$ is the energy scale at which the SM loses validity, and $c_i$ are the Wilson coefficients that parametrize the new physics. Once a complete BSM model is specified, it can be reduced to its low-energy manifestation to express the coefficients $c_i$ in terms of its fundamental parameters, which makes Equation \ref{eq:eft_lagrangian} a model-independent bridge between measurements and theory. The only operator of dimension five violates lepton number and has no impact on Higgs boson phenomenology, so that the leading effects arise at $D=6$, where a complete and non-redundant set of operators is provided by the Warsaw basis \cite{Grzadkowski:2010es}.

The doublet structure has a direct consequence for Higgs boson pair production. In SMEFT, the Higgs field enters the operators only through the combination $\phi^\dagger\phi$, so that a single operator generates the vertices with one and with two Higgs bosons at once. At leading order, the interactions relevant to gluon fusion are governed by five such operators — those modifying the kinetic term and the potential of the Higgs field, the top quark Yukawa interaction, and the contact interaction between the Higgs field and two gluons — and the couplings they generate are therefore correlated. This makes the description more restrictive but also more predictive, since it connects HH production with single Higgs boson and electroweak precision measurements through a common set of parameters. In exchange, SMEFT is only adequate when the new physics decouples, i.e., when the heavy states acquire their mass from a mechanism unrelated to EWSB, so that their effects vanish as their masses increase. Moreover, its predictions depend not only on the Wilson coefficients but also on the scale $\Lambda$ and on the order at which the expansion is truncated, and they remain valid only as long as $c_i E^2/\Lambda^2 \ll 1$, where the relevant energy $E$ is, in HH production, the invariant mass $m_{HH}$ \cite{Heinrich:2022huh}.

HEFT adopts instead the more agnostic hypothesis where the physical Higgs boson is treated as an electroweak singlet, independent of the Goldstone bosons, and the electroweak symmetry is realized non-linearly. The Higgs field can then appear in interaction terms of arbitrary canonical dimension, so that the expansion is no longer ordered by $D$ but by the loop order at which each interaction can first be generated, and the couplings involving one and two Higgs bosons become independent parameters \cite{Buchalla:2018yce}. This more general description accommodates non-decoupling new physics, in which the heavy states acquire part of their mass from EWSB itself, as in composite Higgs models or in scenarios where the Higgs boson is a pseudo-Goldstone boson or a dilaton \cite{Agashe:2004rs,Contino:2010mh,Goldberger:2008zz}. Since any SMEFT Lagrangian can be rewritten in the HEFT language, SMEFT can be regarded as a restricted subset of HEFT, and the two descriptions are complementary rather than alternative: HEFT makes the fewest assumptions, while SMEFT imposes correlations that sharpen the predictions whenever its assumptions hold.

In what follows, the technical details of either formalism are not elaborated; instead, the focus is placed on the phenomenological consequences that both share for Higgs boson pair production.

\subsubsection{The effective coupling formalism}
\label{subsubsect:eff_couplings}
%
Whichever description is adopted, its effect on Higgs boson pair production can be expressed through a small number of effective couplings, which provide a common phenomenological language for the two formalisms. These couplings either modify interactions already present in the production amplitude or introduce new contact interactions, thereby altering both the production rate and the kinematic distributions.

In VBF HH production, the effective description only modifies interactions that already exist in the SM — the $HHH$, $HVV$ and $HHVV$ vertices —, rescaling them or introducing allowed tensor structures, so that no new contact interaction appears at leading order and the diagrams keep the same structure as in the SM.

In contrast, gluon fusion is sensitive to interactions that do not exist in the SM. Besides the anomalous trilinear and top quark Yukawa couplings, $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_t$, three contact interactions contribute at leading order, highlighted in Figure \ref{fig:ggf_EFT}: $c_2$ couples a Higgs boson pair directly to a top quark pair, generating a triangle diagram without an intermediate Higgs boson; $c_g$ couples a single Higgs boson to two gluons, so that the gluons produce an off-shell Higgs boson that subsequently splits into the pair through the trilinear coupling; and $c_{2g}$ couples the Higgs boson pair directly to two gluons. Since the loops are dominated by the top quark, anomalous values of the bottom quark Yukawa coupling are neglected.

Therefore, the amplitude of $gg\to HH$ is the sum of five contributions, each multiplied by a fixed product of couplings. Consequently, the HH cross section, given by its squared modulus, is at leading order a polynomial in the five couplings with a finite number of terms — the five squared contributions and their ten interferences:
%
\begin{equation}
    R_{HH} = \frac{\sigma_{HH}}{\sigma_{HH}^{SM}} = \sum_{i=1}^{15} A_i\, f_i(\kappa_\lambda,\kappa_t,c_2,c_g,c_{2g}),
    \label{eq:RHH}
\end{equation}
where the $f_i$ are known monomials in the couplings and the $A_i$ are numerical coefficients determined from simulation \cite{Carvalho:2016rys}. In the absence of contact interactions, $c_2=c_g=c_{2g}=0$, Equation \ref{eq:RHH} reduces to the parametrization in $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_t$ of Equation \ref{eq:kl_kt_ggf}.

The set of five effective couplings $\kappa_{\lambda}$, $\kappa_t$, $c_2$, $c_g$ and $c_{2g}$ constitutes a common phenomenological language to which both EFT formalisms can be mapped under different considerations. In HEFT they are free and independent parameters, whereas in SMEFT, at leading order, they are generated by only four combinations of Wilson coefficients, so that their image is a four-dimensional region of the HEFT space: the two gluon contact couplings arise from the same operator, which fixes $c_{2g}$ in terms of $c_g$, and the top quark contact interaction $c_2$ cannot vary without simultaneously modifying the Yukawa coupling $\kappa_t$ \cite{Heinrich:2022huh}. This difference makes Higgs boson pair production one of the few processes capable of distinguishing between the two descriptions, since the couplings involving two Higgs bosons, $c_2$ and $c_{2g}$, do not contribute to single Higgs boson production at leading order. In HEFT they can therefore only be tested through HH production, whereas in SMEFT they are tied to the $t\bar{t}H$ and $ggH$ interactions, already strongly constrained by single Higgs boson measurements, so that the same measured distribution supports different physical conclusions depending on the description adopted.\\

Anomalous couplings not only enhance the HH production cross section, but also modify the kinematic properties of the events significantly. As in the simpler case of $\kappa_\lambda$, the five contributions interfere in a non-trivial way and give rise to a wide variety of signal topologies, so that interpreting a measurement in terms of these couplings requires predicting both the rate and the shape of the signal at every point of a five-dimensional space. This evidences the need to develop modeling tools for these effects in physics analyses. Since HEFT provides the most general description with the fewest assumptions, it is the one adopted in this thesis for that purpose: its formal construction for $gg\to HH$ production and the signal modeling techniques that make such an interpretation feasible are developed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}.

\section{Higgs boson pair searches at the LHC}

\noindent After describing the phenomenology associated with the production of Higgs boson pairs, it si essential to understand how experimental searches for the HH signature are conducted. Proton-proton colliders offer a wide variety of accessible HH final states. Because the HH production cross-section is extremely small, searches focus preferentially  on final states with sufficiently large branching ratios (BR). However, each available decay channel poses specific experimental challenges; thus, the channel with the highest BR is not necessarily the most sensitive. Consequently, searches do not focus on a single leading channel. Instead, they are approached inclusively through multiple decay states. The larger number of production modes and the complexity of the final states involved in the experimental study of Higgs pair production — often involving high number of particles to reconstruct — makes it considerable challenging.

The following Section \ref{subsec:HH_BR} summarizes the main HH decay modes accessible at the LHC, along with their BR and experimental challenges. Next, Section \ref{subsec:Searches} reviews the main results of Higgs boson pair searches to date, which will serve to establish the context of where these searches currently stand and the contributions of the work presented in this thesis.


\subsection{Di-Higgs decay modes}
\label{subsec:HH_BR}
\noindent A rich phenomenology of final states are accessible at the LHC. The main challenge in measuring HH production in hadronic colliders is reconstructing the decay products in the detector and distinguishing them from the large background. Achieveng good sensitivity requires finding a balance between the production rates and the complexity of the final states. Thus, the choice of the decay channel for the HH system determines the trade-off between the BR and background contamination. The decay probability is directly related to the Higgs boson's coupling to the decay products and therefore favors third-generation fermions such as bottom quarks ($bb$) and tau leptons ($\tau\tau$), as well as massive vector bosons ($WW$ and $ZZ$), but also massless particles such as gluons ($gg$) and photons ($\gamma\gamma$) via fermionic loops. Note that decay into top quark pairs is highly suppressed by invariant mass conservation. Figure \ref{fig:HH_BR} lists the main possible combinations of HH final states, along with the corresponding branching ratio calculated assuming SM BR. Conversely, background complexity poses separate constraints on the sensitivity of searches, determined by the particles in the final state and the capabilities of experimental identification and reconstruction.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/chapter_I/HH_BR.png}
    \caption{Branching ratio (in \%) for the main combinations of decay products from the HH pair for a Higgs boson mass of 125 GeV. Values extracted from {\color{red}[ref] Hacer mi propio plot}}
    \label{fig:HH_BR}
\end{figure}

The sensitivity to HH production at the LHC is primarily driven by three main channels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $HH\rightarrow bbbb$ is the final state with the highest branching ratio ($\sim$33.9\%). However, significant background contamination from the dominant QCD multi-jet background affects the purity and sensitivity of $4b$ analysis. Consequently, it has little sensitivity to low $m_{HH}$ values, though it can benefir from large signal yields to probe regions up to $m_{HH} \approx 3$ TeV. Recent advances in jet tagging techniques have been fundamental to this channel's sensitivity, which now stands as one of the most competitive for HH searches~\cite{CMS:2022bbbb,ATLAS:2023bbbb}, particularly in the VBF~\cite{ATLAS:2024VBFbbbb} and VHH~\cite{ATLAS:2023VHH} production modes.

    \item $HH\rightarrow bb\tau\tau$ has a slightly smaller BR ($\sim$7.3\%), but benefits from higher purity due to the tau pair. Background for this channel comes from a combination of QCD multijet, Drell-Yan, and $t\overline{t}$ events contributions. The sizable BR and relatively low background provide an advantageous compromise, making it one of the most sensitive channels for setting upper limits on the HH production cross-section.

    \item $HH\rightarrow bb\gamma\gamma$ completes the three main decay modes, being the purest of them. Its low BR ($\sim$0.3\%) is counterbalanced by the clean photon signature. The main background contribution is the di-photom continuum, which is well-understood from single Higgs measurements, allowing for better background control.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This thesis focuses on searches in the $HH\rightarrow bb\tau\tau$ decay channel, an intermediate and particularly interesting scenario. As previously noted, experimental challenges vary significantly depending on the final state under consideration. In the case of $bb\tau\tau$, complexity increases due to the $\tau$ lepton unestable nature, which can decay into both leptons and hadrons in association with neutrinos. Therefore, searches must analyze different final states, and the complete reconstruction of the event is compromised by the presence neutrinos, since they impede the direct determination of the invariant mass of the system. This decay channel also suffers from irreducible background sources that are kinematically similar to the signal process. Thus, kinematic information must be fully exploited to effectively reduce the background contamination. For these reasons, the $bb\tau\tau$ channel is considered one of the most challenging at the LHC. However, the effort is rewarded with one of the best sensitivities for both resonant and non-resonant HH production hypotheses, as demonstrated by various phenomenological studies~\cite{Baglio:2012np,Dolan:2012rv,Barr:2013tda}. The specific properties of the $bb\tau\tau$ channel and the main background contributions are discussed in greater detail in section \ref{} of Chapter \ref{}, and throughout the entirety of this thesis.

\subsection{Experimental status of double Higgs searches}
\label{subsec:Searches}

\noindent The most widely used analysis historically at the LHC have been highlighted. However, the current scope is much broader, and the analysis landscape of the experimental collaborations is increasingly encompassing more final states. Thus, efforts are focused on covering the full range of main channels accessible at LHC, which is gradually becoming possible due to increased statistics, and substantial object reconstruction and analysis techniques improvements. Since searches across various decay channels complement each other — in both the SM and BSM scenarios —, the maximum sensitivity of HH measurements is achieved by combining the main decay channels.

The full set of proton-proton collisions at $\sqrt{s}=8$ TeV (Run I) and $\sqrt{s}=13$ TeV (Run II) has been analyzed for numerous HH final states. The Run I analyses established the fundamental methodology and provided the initial combined limits on HH production after collecting an integrated luminosity of $\sim$36 fb\textsuperscript{-1}. During Run II, the CMS experiment accumulated an integrated luminosity of approximately 138 fb\textsuperscript{-1}. This quantitative breakthrough, combined with significant advancements in detector and analysis strategies, enabled a remarkable expansion of the experimental scope. Searches were conduced in most main channels, and the combined Run II results achieved sensitivities far superior to those of Run I. This allowed exploration of regions of the trilinear self-coupling parameter space that were previously innaccessible, as well as reinterpretations in terms of EFT.

The results obtained by the CMS combination using the complete Run II dataset are shown in Figure \ref{fig:comb_upper_limits}, expressed in terms of upper limits on the HH cross section scaled to the SM value, which is defined as the signal strength $\mu_{HH}=\sigma_{HH}/\sigma^{SM}_{HH}$. In this formulation, the limits are obtained under the null hypothesis, which corresponds to the absence of a signal, i.e., $\mu_{HH}=0$. The statistical procedure used to construct these limits is described in detail in Section \ref{sec:stats}. The combination yields an observed (expected) upper limit of 3.5 (2.5) times the SM expectation at 95\% confidence level (CL), where the sensitivity is driven by the three main channels listed above. This improvement substantially surpasses the limits obtained in Run I, confirming progress achieved thanks not only to increased luminosity, but also to the optimization of reconstruction strategies and the use of advanced machine learning–based discrimination techniques. The ATLAS collaboration obtained similar results, with an observed (expected) upper limit of 2.9 (2.4) times the SM value at 95\% CL.

The start of Run III ($\sqrt{s} = 13.6$) signifies a new phase in the search for Higgs boson pairs. The early part of Run III (2022-2024) is already delivering new and exciting results, and the full set (2022-2026) is on the way. With an expected luminosity of around 300 fb\textsuperscript{-1}, and improved analysis strategies, this ongoing data-taking period promises to significantly improve sensitivity and substantially strengthen current limits.
