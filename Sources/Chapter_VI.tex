\part{The bb\texorpdfstring{$\tau\tau$}{ττ} analysis}
\chapter{Search for nonresonant HH production in the bb\texorpdfstring{$\tau\tau$}{ττ} final state}
\label{ch:bbtt_analysis}
% Entradilla
Among the channels available for the production of Higgs boson pairs at the LHC, the final state in which one of the Higgs bosons decays into a pair of $b$-quarks and the other into a pair of $\tau$ leptons holds a privileged position, since it offers a favorable trade-off between the expected number of signal events and the accompanying background. Consequently, it is one of the channels most systematically explored in the successive searches conducted by CMS. 

The experimental signature of this process is not unique, since the $\tau$ lepton is unstable, which gives rise to different reconstructible final states resulting from the $\tau\tau$ pair. As listed in Table \ref{tab:tau_decays}, the $\tau$ lepton decays predominantly into hadrons and a neutrino, with a total branching fraction of $64.8\%$, and leptonically into an electron or a muon accompanied by two neutrinos, with branching fractions of $17.8\%$ and $17.4\%$ respectively, denoted $\tau_h$, $\tau_e$, and $\tau_\mu$ correspondingly. Only the three pairings in which at least one tau decays hadronically are considered signal channels: the fully hadronic $\tau_h\tau_h$ and the semileptonic $\tau_\mu\tau_h$ and $\tau_e\tau_h$, which together account for $87.6\%$ of the total branching fraction. Apart from the channels in which the signal is expected, the purely leptonic pairings, which have a smaller branching fraction, are used in the analysis as control regions since they are overwhelmed by background production.

The analysis presented in this chapter describes the strategy followed in the search for non-resonant production of Higgs boson pairs in this final state, considering the two relevant production mechanisms: gluon-gluon fusion, sensitive to the trilinear self-coupling $\lambda_{HHH}$ via interference between triangle and box diagrams, and vector boson fusion, which provides complementary sensitivity to the coupling of two Higgs bosons to two vector bosons. The analysis is performed using proton-proton collision data collected by CMS during the first years of LHC Run 3 — 2022, 2023, and 2024 — corresponding to an integrated luminosity of $172$ fb$^{-1}$. The search presented here builds on the work carried out on the complete Run 2 dataset \cite{}, but introduces significant developments that take advantage of the Run 3 improvements at various levels. 

The structure is therefore the same as that of the strategy followed in Run 2. The definition of the three resolved channels based on the decay mode of the $\tau\tau$ pair, the reconstruction of the two Higgs boson candidates, and the general signal extraction scheme are retained. As a result, this is not a completely new strategy, but rather a comprehensive revision of the established one, to which substantial modifications are introduced in virtually every step. First, the phase space covered is expanded to incorporate boosted configurations, both in the $bb$ pair and in the $\tau\tau$ pair, which entails the addition of a fourth signal channel dedicated to boosted $\tau_h\tau_h$. The trigger strategy is extended with the introduction of new triggers, including parking strategies that allow events below conventional thresholds to be recorded. The assignment of jets to the reconstructed candidates relies on the two algorithms developed in the previous chapter: \textsc{HH-BTag}, retrained for Run 3 conditions, and \textsc{VBFjTag}, introduced here for the first time in an analysis of this final state. Furthermore, new methods are developed for estimating fake $\tau$-leptons from multi-jet events, as well as a new DNN for signal extraction in different regions.

The complete analysis workflow, from the datasets used to the extraction of results, is summarized schematically in Figure \ref{fig:bbtt_flow}. Following the analysis workflow, the data collected by CMS and the Monte Carlo simulated events undergo the same trigger selection, designed to isolate the objects of interest. Identification criteria and kinematic requirements that define the physical objects in the final state are then applied to the events that pass this selection. From these events, the Higgs boson candidate that decays into the $\tau\tau$ pair, the one that decays into the $b\bar{b}$ pair, and — when present — the jets characteristic of vector boson fusion topology are successively reconstructed. A selection based on the reconstructed invariant masses removes the region lacking signal contribution, and the surviving events are divided into mutually exclusive categories. In each category, the discrimination between signal and background is performed by a deep neural network whose output distribution constitutes the observable on which the statistical fitting is performed, from which the results are extracted.

\section{Data and simulated samples}
\label{sec:bbtt_samples}
The analysis is based on a comparison between the data recorded by CMS and the Standard Model prediction for the processes that contribute to this final state. The prediction is derived from simulated MC samples, produced using state-of-the-art generators, which are used to model both the signal and the backgrounds. The only exception is the contribution from events in which a quark or gluon jet is misidentified as a hadronic $\tau$ lepton: generating enough such events is computationally prohibitive, and the misidentification rate is in any case not modeled with the required precision, being highly sensitive to detector effects. This contribution is therefore estimated directly from the data.

\subsection{Datasets and data-taking periods}
%
The analyzed data correspond to proton-proton collisions at $\sqrt{s} = 13.6$ TeV recorded by CMS with a bunsh crossing interval of 25 ns during the years 2022, 2023, and 2024. Only events occurring during data-taking periods in which all subdetectors were operational and functioning correctly, as determined by the centralized certification procedure of the collaboration, are considered. The resulting available integrated luminosity amounts to $34.7$, $27.7$, and $110$ fb$^{-1}$ for 2022, 2023, and 2024, respectively, for a total of $172$ fb$^{-1}$.

The recorded events are distributed across primary datasets, which are defined by the trigger paths that lead to their storage, and are in turn subdivided into eras — identified by a letter — that delimit intervals with homogeneous data-acquisition conditions. This analysis uses the \texttt{Muon} and \texttt{EGamma} datasets, which contain events selected by the isolated lepton and lepton plus $\tau_h$ triggers; \texttt{Tau}, associated with the double $\tau_h$ triggers; \texttt{JetMET}, which provides access to events selected by triggers based on jets and missing transverse momentum; and the \texttt{ParkingHH} and \texttt{ParkingVBF} parking sets, introduced during Run 3 and described in Section \ref{sec:triggers}.

During the 2022 and 2023 eras, incidents occurred in the detector that led to separate simulation campaigns. Consequently, the data-acquisition periods are named after the affected subsystem. In 2022, the incident affected one end of the electromagnetic calorimeter, resulting in the periods 2022preEE and 2022postEE; in 2023, a region of the barrel pixel detector was compromised, separating the periods 2023preBPix and 2023postBPix. In 2024, no equivalent incident occurred, so the data from that year are treated as a single period. Table \ref{tab:datataking_periods} lists the five resulting periods along with their integrated luminosity and the incident that caused each division. Furthermore, the 2022 and 2023 data were subsequently reprocessed to incorporate the corrections and algorithms available from the latest simulation campaigns, ensuring that all five periods are analyzed using a homogeneous reconstruction chain. The analysis is performed independently for each of these five periods, and the results are combined during the final stage of statistical fitting.

\subsection{Signal samples}
%
Higgs boson pair production via gluon-gluon fusion (ggF) is simulated using the \POWHEG v2.0 generator at NLO in QCD, while the production via vector boson fusion (VBF) is simulated using \MADGRAPH5\_aMC@NLO 2.9.13 at LO. Parton showering and hadronization are simulated with \PYTHIA{}. The samples are normalized to the SM cross sections at $\sqrt{s} = 13.6$ TeV for a Higgs boson mass of $125$ GeV: $34.13$ fb for ggF, and
$1.874$ fb for VBF. In both cases, additional samples are generated with anomalous values of the coupling modifiers, which make it possible to cover the dependence of the cross section and of the kinematics of the $HH$ system on these parameters.

Although optimized for the SM search, the analysis is not restricted to the SM hypothesis. The results are also interpreted as constraints on the coupling modifiers $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_{2V}$, which requires a prediction of the signal at any point of the coupling space. The prediction cannot be obtained by simulating one sample per hypothesis, but rather it can be built from a small set of samples thanks to a structural property of the process. The amplitude of the process is a finite sum of contributions whose coefficients depend on the couplings but whose kinematics do not. As a consequence, the cross section is a polynomial in the couplings with a finite number of terms, and this holds not only for the total rate but also for any differential distribution. The full dependence on the couplings can therefore be determined from a small number of simulated samples, as shown below for each production mode.\\

For ggF production, whose LO diagrams are shown in Figure \ref{fig:HH_ggF_diagrams}, the amplitude is the sum of a box contribution, proportional to $\kappa_t^2$, and a triangle contribution, proportional to $\kappa_t\kappa_\lambda$, so that the cross section can be decomposed into three terms,
%
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_\text{ggF}(\kappa_\lambda, \kappa_t)
    = \kappa_t^4\, |A_{\Box}|^2
    + \kappa_t^2\kappa_\lambda^2\, |A_{\triangle}|^2
    + \kappa_t^3\kappa_\lambda\, I_{\Box\triangle},
    \label{eq:ggf_poly}
\end{equation}
%
namely the box and triangle contributions and their interference. The three unknown components are determined from three samples generated at $\kappa_\lambda = 1$, $2.45$, and $5$, with $\kappa_t = 1$. 

For VBF production, whose diagrams are shown in Figure \ref{fig:HH_VBF_diagrams}, three topologies interfere, with coupling dependences $\kappa_V^2$, $\kappa_V\kappa_\lambda$, and $\kappa_{2V}$, respectively, so that the cross section contains six terms:
%
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_\text{VBF}(\kappa_V, \kappa_{2V}, \kappa_\lambda)
    = \kappa_V^4\,a_1 + \kappa_V^2\kappa_\lambda^2\,a_2 + \kappa_{2V}^2\,a_3
    + \kappa_V^3\kappa_\lambda\,a_4 + \kappa_V^2\kappa_{2V}\,a_5
    + \kappa_V\kappa_\lambda\kappa_{2V}\,a_6 .
    \label{eq:vbf_poly}
\end{equation}
%
These six components are determined from samples, generated at the points $(\kappa_V, \kappa_{2V}, \kappa_\lambda) = (1, 1, 1)$, $(1, 0, 1)$, $(-0.962, 0.959, -1.43)$, $(-1.21, 1.94, -0.94)$, $(-1.60, 2.72, -1.36)$, and $(-1.83, 3.57, -3.39)$.\\


By applying Equations \ref{eq:ggf_poly} and \ref{eq:vbf_poly} to each of the generated samples, a system of linear equations can be written, $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = K\,\mathbf{A}$, where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ contains the cross sections of the samples, $\mathbf{A}$ the unknown components, and each row of the matrix $K$ the monomials of one sample. With as many samples as unknown components, the system can be solved, $\mathbf{A} = K^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}$, and the components obtained no longer depend on the couplings. The cross section at any other point $\boldsymbol{\kappa}$ then follows by multiplying them by the monomials evaluated at that point, so that it is expressed as a linear combination of the simulated samples:
%
\begin{equation}
    \sigma(\boldsymbol{\kappa}) = \mathbf{f}^{\,T}(\boldsymbol{\kappa})\, K^{-1}\,
    \boldsymbol{\sigma},
    \label{eq:matrix_inversion}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{f}(\boldsymbol{\kappa})$ is the vector of monomials evaluated at the target point. Since the decomposition holds bin by bin, the same combination is applied to the histograms of the final discriminant in every category, and the signal model is constructed in this way directly at the level of the statistical fit.\\ 

This approach is known as the \textit{matrix inversion method}, and its general formalism, the conditions that the set of simulated points must satisfy for the inversion to be numerically stable, and its extension to the higher-dimensional parameter spaces of effective field theories, which constitutes one of the original contributions of this thesis, are developed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}.

\subsection{Simulated background samples}
%
The SM processes that can reproduce the signature of the signal fall into two groups. Irreducible backgrounds lead to exactly the same final state, with two genuine $b$ quarks and two genuine $\tau$ leptons. The main one is top quark pair production, in which each top quark decays into a $b$ quark and a $W$ boson, and both $W$ bosons decay leptonically, either directly into an electron or a muon or through a $\tau$ lepton. It is followed by Drell--Yan production, $Z/\gamma^*\to\tau\tau$, when the lepton pair is accompanied by $b$ jets from gluon splitting. Single Higgs boson production also contributes despite its small cross section, in particular through $ZH$ production with $Z\to b\bar{b}$ and $H\to\tau\tau$. Reducible backgrounds, instead, only reproduce the final state when an object is misidentified: a light-flavor jet taken for a $b$ jet, an electron or a muon taken for a $\tau_h$ or, above all, a quark or gluon jet taken for a $\tau_h$. This is the case of QCD multijet production, which dominates in the $\tau_h\tau_h$
channel, of $W$ boson production in association with jets, and of the $t\bar{t}$ and Drell--Yan decays in which one of the objects of the final state is not genuine. Representative diagrams of the three main processes are shown in Figure \ref{fig:bkg_feynman}.

\section{Trigger strategy}
\label{sec:triggers}
%
% One of the strongest development lines of the Run-3 analysis -> own section.
The selection criteria applied by the trigger system during data acquisition determine which events are recorded, setting the upper limit of the acceptance of the analysis. The trigger strategy builds upon the one used in the published Run 2 search and extends it in three directions. First, it includes the incorporation of paths that require jets in addition to leptons, which allows for lowering the lepton thresholds in exchange for requiring additional hadronic activity. Second, the \textit{parking} strategy is adopted, which involves writing events directly to tape during the final stages of an LHC fill, when the instantaneous luminosity has dropped sufficiently to allow for more permissive thresholds without saturating the bandwidth; two such streams are exploited here, one devoted to the vector boson fusion topology from 2023 onwards and one to the fully hadronic $HH$ final state from 2024. Finally, dedicated paths for boosted topologies that did not exist in Run 2 are introduced. The trigger strategy thus constitutes one of the strongest developments with respect to the Run 2 analysis.

The available paths and their requirements are listed in Table \ref{tab:triggers}. To maximize efficiency, the paths are combined in a logical OR. However, since some paths overlap significantly, when this overlap complicates the derivation of corrections, they are orthogonalized by inverting the selections associated with the others, so that each event is assigned to only one path.

The online trigger requirement is carried over to the offline selection, requiring that the reconstructed objects be matched to the objects that fired the path and that they exceed more restrictive $p_T$ thresholds than those applied online, so that the selection lies on the plateau of the efficiency curve rather than in the turn-on region, where efficiency varies rapidly and modeling is less reliable. The specific values for each path are detailed, along with the selection criteria for each object type, in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_objects}.

\subsection{Baseline trigger paths}
%
The core of the selection strategy is based on the trigger paths inherited from Run 2: the isolated single-lepton triggers, the cross-trigger paths that require a lepton with lower momentum accompanied by a $\tau_h$, and the double $\tau_h$ path for the fully hadronic final state. The cross-trigger strategy enables a substantial lowering of the lepton threshold, although this comes at the cost of limiting the angular acceptance to the region covered by the Level 1 trigger system for the $\tau_h$. This is because the $\tau_h$ is the most difficult to identify online, thereby constraining the selection. Its identification in the HLT reproduces, in a simplified form, the \textit{hadrons-plus-strips} algorithm described in Section \ref{sec:Tau_MisID}, followed by a machine-learning discriminator that, in 2022 and 2023, is a version of \textsc{DeepTau} adapted to the latency constraints of the trigger, and which, in 2024, changes to \textsc{ParticleNet}; both are online versions of the algorithms presented in Chapter \ref{ch:Tau}. This change leads to an improvement in discrimination capability that allows for lowering the thresholds of the double $\tau_h$ path while maintaining the recording rate, and the 2024 deployment additionally extends the angular coverage of the paths involving $\tau_h$ leptons.

\subsection{New trigger paths in Run 3}
% 
The new additions to Run 3 broaden the acceptance of the baseline analysis strategy by addressing the regions of phase space that it covered less thoroughly. The first change from Run 2 came in 2022 and consists of the \textit{DiTau+Jet} path, where the additional jet requirement allows for a lower threshold on both $\tau_h$ in a region of the spectrum where reconstruction efficiency varies rapidly. In 2024, a new addition was introduced into the $HH$ parking stream that also affects both $\tau_h$ and requires four central jets, at least one of which must satisfy a $b$-quark tagging requirement and another a $\tau_h$ tagging requirement, both evaluated online. As it significantly overlaps with the previous paths, the new trigger introduced in 2024 is employed in a completely orthogonal region of phase space, obtained by inverting all the offline selections associated with the double $\tau_h$ and DiTau+Jet triggers.

The gain in signal selection efficiency provided by each path relative to the Run 2 strategy — which is based solely on the double $\tau_h$ path — is shown in Figure \ref{fig:trigger_gain}, as a function of the invariant mass of the $HH$ system at the generator level and for two coupling hypotheses, $\kappa_\lambda=1$ and $5$. The DiTau+Jet path provides a $13\%$ gain integrated over the entire spectrum, and this gain increases to $17\%$ in the low invariant mass region, which is precisely the region most sensitive to variations in the trilinear self-coupling\footnote{The gains reflect the increase in the number of selected signal events. However, the number of background events that pass the selection also increases, so the numbers do not translate into an equivalent improvement in sensitivity}. For 2022 and 2023, this amounts to approximately $10$ fb$^{-1}$ of additional luminosity. The hadronic parking path contributes an additional $15\%$, with an opposite dependence: its gain is lower near the production threshold and increases toward higher masses, where the jets of the events are more energetic and more readily satisfy the $H_T$ and multiplicity requirements. The two are therefore complementary, and their orthogonalized combination shows the joint gain across the entire range. The comparison between the two coupling hypotheses also shows that the improvement is independent of the point in parameter space under consideration, since the gain curves are essentially identical in both figures. The change, however, stems from the $m_{HH}$ spectrum of the signal — superimposed in light gray in the figure — which, for the case $\kappa_\lambda = 5$, shifts toward the production threshold, so that the signal is concentrated precisely in the region where the new triggers contribute the most.\\

Dedicated to the VBF topology, new triggers have been introduced that specifically exploit this distinctive signature. Although the Run 2 search already included a path of this kind, which required two low-momentum $\tau_h$ candidates accompanied by two jets with high invariant mass and which is retained in Run 3, a much broader set is now available. The Run 3 VBF triggers, mostly recorded through the parking stream, were introduced from 2023 onwards and select the VBF jets either on their own, in what is referred to as the \textit{inclusive} path, or in association with a muon, an electron, or one or two $\tau_h$.

The choice of which paths to use is made independently for each final state based on two criteria: the gain in signal efficiency relative to the baseline strategy, and the overlap with paths already used, since a path whose gain is entirely contained within that of another path does not introduce new events and only adds complexity. The result is that in the final state $\mu\tau_h$, only the VBF$+\mu$ path is retained, while in $e\tau_h$ both the inclusive path and the VBF$+e$ path are selected, and in $\tau_h\tau_h$, the inclusive path is retained along with those requiring one or two $\tau_h$. The gain is maximal for all channels in the low invariant mass region, reaching around $20\%$ in $\mu\tau_h$, $30\%$ in $e\tau_h$, and nearly $75\%$ in $\tau_h\tau_h$. This hierarchy reflects the fact that single-lepton and cross-lepton paths already effectively cover the semileptonic final states, whereas in the fully hadronic case, the baseline requires two $\tau_h$ above $35$ GeV, leaving much greater room for improvement.\\

Finally, Run 3 incorporates paths that provide access to the boosted region, where the $\tau\tau$ decay products are so collimated that the double $\tau_h$ path thresholds are no longer efficient. There are two new triggers. The first is based on the missing transverse momentum computed without the muon contribution, $p_T^{\text{miss,no-}\mu}$, taking advantage of the fact that in the boosted regime the missing transverse momentum is greater than in the resolved one, where it is largely compensated for by the two $\tau$ leptons being emitted in nearly opposite directions. The second requires a large-radius jet with a minimum \textit{soft-drop} mass\footnote{Soft-drop is a jet grooming algorithm that removes soft, wide-angle radiation from a large-radius jet by iteratively declustering it and discarding the softer branch whenever the two subjets fail a momentum-sharing condition. The logical OR of the two remains above $90\%$ across the entire range, and a comparison between the two periods shows that the decline of the large-radius jet path is more pronounced in 2024, without this significantly affecting the combination in the region relevant to the analysis. For simplicity, the two triggers are applied in orthogonal and mutually exclusive regions separated by a threshold of $200$ GeV on the offline-reconstructed missing transverse momentum, which results in a loss of efficiency of only about $3$ percentage points.

\section{Physics object preselection}
\label{sec:bbtt_objects}
%
The reconstruction of the objects involved in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state — muons, electrons, hadronic $\tau$ leptons, and jets — is performed using the candidates from the Particle Flow algorithm introduced in Section \ref{sec:POR}. Therefore, this section is limited to listing the identification criteria and the kinematic regions that define each object at the analysis level. It should be noted that the transverse momentum thresholds for reconstructed objects are directly related to the trigger strategy employed. Hence, they are not set here but are inherited by each event from the path that selected it: the objects that reconstruct the final state must be geometrically matched to the cone within $\Delta R < 0.5$ with the objects that activated the trigger, and they must exceed a threshold
%
\begin{equation}
    p_{T}^{\text{offline}} \geq p_{T}^{\text{online}} + \delta,
    \label{eq:trigger_matching}
\end{equation}
%
where $p_{T}^{\text{online}}$ is the online threshold listed in Table \ref{tab:triggers} and $\delta$ is a margin chosen such that the selection operates on the plateau of the efficiency curves and not on their turn-on region, where the efficiency depends critically on the exact value of the threshold. This margin is determined by the resolution with which each type of object is reconstructed online versus offline, and it is set at $1$ GeV for electrons, $2$ GeV for muons, and $5$ GeV for hadronic $\tau$ leptons\footnote {The thresholds on the purely hadronic legs of the triggers are the same as those applied online, with the exception of the VBF+DiTau path, in which the two jets are required to have $p_{T} > 140$ $(160)$ and $60$ $(70)$ GeV for 2022-23 (24). For the $p_{T}^{\text{miss}}$ path, whose turn-on curve is notably slower, $p_{T}^{\text{miss}} > 200$ GeV is required.}. Consequently, the preselections described below are deliberately loose in $p_{T}$.

\subsection{Muons and electrons}
%
Muons are selected within the acceptance of the muon chamber system, $|\eta| < 2.4$, requiring a \textit{tight} identification working point and a relative isolation, calculated within a cone of radius $\Delta R = 0.4$, of less than $0.15$. Electrons, on the other hand, are restricted to the coverage of the tracker, with $|\eta| < 2.5$, and are identified using a multivariate discriminant that combines identification and isolation information, operating at an efficiency of $80\%$. In both cases, the trajectory must be compatible with the primary vertex of the event, with transverse and longitudinal impact parameters less than $0.045$ and $0.2$ cm, respectively; and in the 2022postEE period, reconstructed electrons in the endcap region of the electromagnetic calorimeter affected by the power loss described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples} are also discarded. A third-lepton veto is applied on top of this selection, so that events containing any additional muon or electron that satisfies a relaxed version of the above criteria are rejected, which suppresses the contribution from Drell–Yan and $t\bar{t}$ dilepton events.

\subsection{Hadronic tau leptons}

\subsection{Jets and heavy-flavor identification}
%
The jets are reconstructed by grouping Particle Flow candidates using the anti-$k_T$ algorithm, with a distance parameter of $R = 0.4$ for ordinary jets and $R = 0.8$ for large-radius jets intended for boosted topologies. Of these, those that overlap with the already selected $\tau$ candidates are discarded to avoid double-counting the same energy deposits, as are low-momentum jets reconstructed in the transition region $2.5 < |\eta| < 3.0$, where the resolution degrades significantly. The $b$-jet candidates are sought among jets with $p_T > 20$ GeV and $|\eta| < 2.5$, while vector boson fusion jet candidates are accepted up to $|\eta| < 4.7$, taking advantage of the complete coverage of the forward hadronic calorimeter. The decision of which jets to include in the reconstruction of each candidate is made using the algorithms developed in Chapter \ref{ch:taggers}, as described in the following section.

\section{Event selection and categorization}
\label{sec:bbtt_flow}
%
The criteria defining each of the analysis objects separately have been established. However, the analysis rests on the combinations they form among themselves so that the event is assembled candidate by candidate, following a specific order. First, the pair $\tau\tau$ is reconstructed, since it determines the channel to which the event is assigned. Next, the candidate $H\to b\bar{b}$ is constructed, taking care to filter out the jet selection with respect to the $\tau$ candidates once they have been selected. Finally, the jets remaining after the two Higgs boson candidates have been formed are examined for the topology characteristic of vector boson fusion.

Once the candidates have been identified, the analysis proceeds to the definition of the signal regions. Events are first distributed among mutually exclusive categories, defined by the topology in which the event was reconstructed and by the final state of the $\tau\tau$ pair. Within the resolved categories, a requirement on the plane defined by the two reconstructed masses then removes the region where no signal is expected. It is therefore essential to address how the mass of each candidate is reconstructed, as will be done in each of the corresponding sections. These categories represent the regions from which the signal is ultimately extracted and, therefore, the way they are defined has a direct impact on the final results of the analysis. The following sections walk through each of these steps in the same order in which they are applied.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$H\to\tau\tau$}{H->tautau}: the channel-defining pair}
% FINAL STATES ASSIGNMENT AND SELECTION
%
After the preselection outline<d in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_objects} has been applied, the channel to which an event belongs is determined by its light lepton content. Events with one muon and no electrons are assigned to the $\tau_\mu\tau_h$ channel, those containing one electron and no muons to the $\tau_e\tau_h$ channel, and those containing neither to the $\tau_h\tau_h$ channel. Events involving two light leptons — namely, $\mu\mu$, $ee$, and $e\mu$ — are kept separately and reserved as control regions, since the signal contribution from the purely leptonic decay of the $\tau\tau$ pair is buried under the two main backgrounds in the analysis: Drell-–Yan dominates in $ee$ and $\mu\mu$, while $e\mu$ is dominated by the dileptonic decay of $t\bar{t}$ pairs, due to the fact that the $Z$ bosons cannot contribute directly, by lepton flavor conservation. Events that do not alignt with the expected configurations are discarded.

Channel assignment therefore relies entirely on the light leptons, which have been previously selected using strict identification and isolation criteria. The $\tau_h$ leptons, on the other hand, are deliberately preselected using the loosest working point available with respect to jets, allowing for the construction of the control regions on which the estimation of the misidentified jet background described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_ff} is based. It therefore remains to be determined which $\tau_h$ accompanies the light lepton in the channel that the latter defines, since a single event often admits several compatible pairs from which a choice must be made.

The ambiguity is resolved by  ordering all possible pairs and keeping the first one. The ordering is done leg by leg; in semileptonic channels, the light lepton always occupies the leading position, while in $\tau_h\tau_h$ the two permutations of the candidates are considered. The pairs are initially sorted by the isolation of the leading leg, and if that value is the same, priority is given to the pair with the higher transverse momentum. If they also match in momentum — which indicates that they share the leading candidate, as will be the case for semileptonic channels — the isolation of the second leg is used, and as a last resort, its transverse momentum. In the case of the $\tau_h$ candidates, this isolation is the \textsc{DeepTau} score relative to jets, which is the same variable to which the working point defining the signal region will later be applied. The ordering thus retains the pair least likely to originate from a misidentified jet and which, therefore, is most likely to survive that subsequent tightening of the signal region.

The boosted channel, $b\tau_h\tau_h$, is treated as an extension of the purely hadronic channel and is considered only for events in which no resolved pair could form. The priority here is always the resolved channel, i.e., if both two $\tau_h$ from the standard algorithm and two candidates from the dedicated reconstruction for boosted topologies are reconstructed in the same event, the event is assigned to the resolved $\tau_h\tau_h$. Otherwise, and provided that the event contains no light leptons, the boosted candidates are paired if their angular separation lies between $0.05$ and $0.8$, a range that requires them to be close enough to be contained within a large-radius jet, but not so close that both are reconstructions of the same object. The jet containing them is then identified. If multiple jets satisfy this condition, the one with the highest transverse momentum is selected, whereas if none exist, the pair is discarded. A \textsc{ParticleNet} discriminant is then evaluated on that associated jet, which quantifies on a scale from zero to one the compatibility of the jet with containing an $H\to\tau\tau$ decay versus multi-jet production. Pairs whose jet does not exceed a minimum value of $0.1$ are discarded, \textcolor{red}{below which the efficiency of the corresponding trigger path is not reliably described by the simulation.}\\

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$H\to\tau\tau$}{H->tautau} mass reconstruction}
%
After selecting the candidate pair, it is essential to determine the method for obtaining its invariant mass, which is not trivial for the $\tau\tau$ system. Every $\tau$ decay contains at least one neutrino that escapes detection and carries away an unmeasured fraction of the momentum, making it unfeasible to obtain the invariant mass of the $\tau\tau$ system directly from the visible decay products. The mass estimated solely from the visible products appears shifted well below $125$ GeV and has a considerable width, which is insufficient for the analysis.

In Run 2, the issue was addressed using \textsc{FastMTT} \cite{fastmtt}, a likelihood-based algorithm that evaluates the compatibility between a mass hypothesis and the measured momenta of the decay products, complemented by the missing transverse momentum of the event. The analysis in Run 3 replaces this procedure with a neural network trained to directly output the neutrino momenta, so that the mass is obtained by summing the reconstructed four-momenta. The network receives as input the categorical variables describing the event — such as the channel, the decay mode, and the charge of each $\tau$ — along with the four-momenta of the $\tau$ candidates, the $b$-jet candidates, and the systems they form; the missing transverse momentum with its covariance matrix; and the jet tagging scores. Training is performed separately for each period using signal samples and Drell–-Yan, $t\bar{t}$, and $t\bar{t}H$ background samples. Boosted events, which are too infrequent to train on, are evaluated as if they were $\tau_h\tau_h$.

The enhancement resulting from this change in strategy can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:htautau_mass}, which compares, in the $\tau_\mu\tau_h$ channel, the mass reconstructed directly from the visible products, the likelihood method, and the regression developed in Run 3. The figure illustrates that the visible mass places the signal around $70$ GeV, with a width such that it completely overlaps with the Drell–Yan peak. The likelihood method recovers the correct scale and moves the signal to the vicinity of $125$ GeV, but its width remains comparable to the distance separating it from the background. The regression, on the other hand, concentrates the signal into a narrow peak around the Higgs boson mass, clearly separated from the Drell-–Yan peak. In quantitative terms, the improvement in resolution is approximately $50\%$ compared to the likelihood method, and the background rejection capability increases by between $30$ and $40\%$, depending on the channel. The masses of the purely leptonic control channels, which do not play a role in signal extraction, continue to be obtained using the likelihood method introduced in Run 2.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$H\to bb$}{H->bb} candidate: resolved and boosted topologies}
%
Once the $\tau\tau$ pair has been identified, the $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidate is sought among the preselected jets that do not overlap with any of the previously selected $\tau$ candidates. In this final state, it is common to find more heavy-flavor jets than the two being sought, originating from radiation in the initial state of the signal itself or from background events such as the $t\bar{t}$ pair. Therefore the objective is to determine which of the jets of the event tagged as originating from a $b$ quark actually come from the decay of the Higgs boson.\\

In the final state of candidate $b\bar{b}$, the resolved and boosted topologies are again considered. In the resolved topology, the assignment is performed by the algorithm trained for this purpose, \textsc{HH-BTag}, which was previously presented in Section \ref{sec:HHBTag}. This algorithm combines each jet $b$-tagging score with its kinematics and its relative position with respect to the other objects in the event, as well as with global event variables, and returns for each jet the probability that it originates from the candidate. For each event, the two jets that receive the highest scores are selected as those that constitute the candidate. 

Originally developed for Run 2 analysis, the \textsc{HH-BTag} algorithm has been reoptimized for Run 3, and in all cases has demonstrated a significant improvement in the purity of the selected pair compared to the selection carried out by available standard algorithms. For all production mechanisms, signal scenarios, and years, the \textsc{HH-BTag} algorithm achieves purity values ranging from $94$ to $98\%$, compared to the $91$–$95\%$ achieved by the best standard tagging discriminant used alone. Hence, the use of the algorithm significantly and consistently enhances performance for HH production scenarios for all $\tau\tau$ final states. Details on the architecture, the retraining performed for Run 3 conditions, and the comparison with standard algorithms can be found in Chapter \ref{ch:taggers}.\\

This reconstruction is based on the assumption that the two $b$ quarks give rise to two distinguishable jets, which no longer holds when the Higgs boson is produced with high transverse momentum. The angular separation between the two quarks is bounded from below by $2 m_{H} / p_{T}(H)$, a limit reached when both quarks share the momentum equally and from which the separation deviates as the momentum distribution becomes asymmetric. Thus, below a transverse momentum of approximately $300$ GeV for the Higgs, it is possible to reconstruct the two jets separately, whereas above approximately $600$ GeV, the $0.4$-radius algorithm is no longer able to reconstruct them as distinct objects. In the regime between these two values, the two quarks partially overlap, and the event can be described in both ways simultaneously.

Accordingly, in order to cover the collimated regime, the large-radius jet containing the two quarks with the highest \textsc{ParticleNet} score compared to multijet production is selected as the candidate. Both the resolved and boosted reconstructions are attempted for all events, so that in the intermediate region both reconstructions can be successful. This underscores the order of priority among categories described below, according to which the boosted category is examined first because it is the least populated. Otherwise, if it were evaluated last, its events would have already been absorbed by the resolved categories.

\subsubsection{$H\to bb$ mass reconstruction}
%
In contrast to the case of the $\tau\tau$ system, the mass of the $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidate is derived entirely from visible observables. In the resolved topology, the reconstructed mass is simply the invariant mass of the system formed by the two selected jets. In the boosted topology, it corresponds to the mass of the large-radius jet. Nevertheless, the peak of the distribution appears slightly below the expected $125$ GeV, since the semileptonic decays of the $B$ hadrons produced in the hadronization of $b$ quarks contain neutrinos that also escape detection, carrying away part of the momentum of the jet. The effect, however, is a residual shift of the peak, not a degradation comparable to that of the $\tau\tau$ system; therefore, it is not corrected.

\subsection{The VBF jets assignment}
%
In the final step of the object selection procedure, the VBF jets are assigned. Once the two Higgs boson candidates have been reconstructed, an examination is conducted to determine whether the jet configuration of the event is consistent with the jets characteristic of vector boson fusion production. The problem is solved using the same approach as the one adopted for the assignment of the $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidate, leaving the decision to the \textsc{VBFjTag} algorithm introduced in Section \ref{sec:VBFjTag}, used here for the first time in an analysis and which replicates the architecture and training strategy of \textsc{HH-BTag}.

The algorithm incorporates input variables specific to this problem, such as the isolation of the jet from the other jets in the event and its centrality, quantities that leverage the characteristic properties of VBF jets. Moreover, the network uses information about which jets have been selected to form the $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidate, which in Section \ref{sec:VBFjTag} proved to be a useful input variable for the model. Therefore, while the score is evaluated over all preselected jets — extending the set to $|\eta| < 4.7$ — the pair is chosen only from among those that have not already been used, where the jets with the highest scores constitute the candidate pair.\\

However, the identification of a candidate pair in a given event is not sufficient to classify it as resulting from the vector boson fusion production. Instead, additional kinematic requirements must also be met, as described below, along with the rest of the categorization scheme.

\subsection{Event categorization}
% poner en algun sitio que está heredado del run 2 (y en qué puntos difiere), ya que en la linea472 si que estoy poniendo eso, pero esta descontextualizado
The steps described establish the presence of two Higgs boson candidates in the event and identify the objects most likely to originate from them, thereby describing the event in terms of the candidate $H\to\tau\tau$, which determines the channel; the $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidate, reconstructed in the resolved topology, in the boosted topology, or in both simultaneously; and, when present, the jet pair consistent with the vector boson fusion signature.

However, this description alone is not sufficient to distinguish the signal from the background, since background events can yield the same readings as signal events. Furthermore, given the low cross section for Higgs pair production in comparison to that of the accompanying processes, the events are largely dominated by the background. This low cross section is also the reason why it is not feasible to extract the signal by tightening the selection criteria, as this would significantly reduce the signal, weaken the control of the background, and limit the scope of the analysis to different signal hypotheses. Signal extraction therefore aims to preserve all information by dividing the events into mutually exclusive categories, which allow the phase space to be partitioned into regions with different signal-to-background ratios. In this manner, the overall sensitivity is enhanced precisely because the statistical fitting can exploit the contrast between categories, both in terms of composition and in the behavior of the discriminating variables.

These categories are defined on the aforementioned reconstructed objects, and the conditions of each category serve as classification criteria, since an event that does not satisfy the conditions of one category is then examined for the next. Accordingly, the different channels associated with the final state of the pair $\tau\tau$ constitute one criterion for classifying events. The other criterion is the topology in which the event was reconstructed, first evaluating whether the $b\bar{b}$ system is collimated — in which case it would be assigned to the boosted $b\bar{b}$ category —; subsequently, if the event is not identified within the boosted category, it is examined to see if it exhibits the characteristic of vector boson fusion — the VBF category —; and finally, the remaining events are distributed among resolved categories, which are further subdivided according to the quality of the jets, and to which a cut is applied in the $(m_{H\tau\tau}, m_{Hbb})$ plane. The scheme is largely inherited from the Run 2 analysis, where the introduced differences stem from the added phase space and the algorithms that become available in Run 3.

The order of precedence used in defining the categories ensures that each event falls into only one of them. This sequence, shown in Figure \ref{fig:category_flow}, is not arbitrary but is designed according to the expected number of events in each category, so that the less populated categories are given preference to prevent the more populated regions from absorbing events that might overlap in phase space.

\section{Corrections to simulated events}
\label{sec:bbtt_corrections}
%
The processes that populate the regions defined in the previous section, along with the generators used to simulate them, were listed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples}. Although the simulated samples are reconstructed using the same algorithms as the data, the simulation is unable to accurately reproduce either the conditions under which the data were collected or the detector response to each object. There are also known shortcomings in the generator that affect the description of the $p_T$ in the two dominant backgrounds of the analysis, Drell–Yan and $t\bar{t}$, and which must be corrected. Therefore, correction factors or scale factors are introduced into the simulated events to improve the agreement between the data and the simulation. The scale factors can modify the total yield of specific samples, either by weighting each event according to its characteristics, or by modifying the four-momentum of particular objects.

In many cases, these corrections are derived centrally in CMS and are common to all analyses. However, there are others that depend on trigger paths, objects, or phase spaces specific to this search, and which are in consequence derived and validated within the analysis itself. All correction factors applied to the simulated events are described below, and their associated uncertainties are described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}.


%The processes that populate the regions defined in the previous section, along with the generators used to simulate them, were listed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples}. However, the method for obtaining the expected number of events in each region has not yet been explained. This method does not simply amount to normalizing these samples to the integrated luminosity, but rather requires further considerations depending on the process and the origin of the $\tau_h$ candidate. As previously noted, events in which both $\tau$ leptons are genuine are taken directly from the simulation. However, the simulation of the two main contributions, Drell–Yan and top quark pair production, shows known discrepancies with respect to the data, necessitating corrections to the simulation. Additionally, the contribution from those events in which a hadronic jet has been erroneously identified as $\tau_h$ — which will simply be referred to as \textit{fakes} hereafter — cannot be reliably described using MC simulation, and therefore, it is estimated directly from the data.

\subsection{Object-level corrections}
\label{subsec:bbtt_object_corrections}
%
The corrections included in this subsection do not depend on the simulated physical process, but solely on the objects present in the event and the conditions under which the event was recorded. These corrections therefore apply equally to all simulated samples, including the signal. Most of the corrections introduced here correct an efficiency — whether of a trigger path or an identification criterion — via a scale factor defined as the ratio between the efficiency measured in the data and that obtained in simulation. These efficiencies are commonly measured using the tag-and-probe method, which exploits a well-known resonance, typically the $Z$ boson, to define, based on its decay products, the \textit{tag} — which must satisfy tight selection criteria that ensure the purity of the sample — and the \textit{probe} — used to measure the efficiency of the criterion under study without biasing it. The remaining corrections affect the energy scale and resolution of the reconstructed objects. %All of them arise as an event progresses through the analysis: first, the pileup conditions under which it was recorded; then, its selection by the trigger; and finally, the reconstruction and identification of each of the objects in its final state.

%
\subsubsection{Pile-up reweighting}
%
The pile-up profile is set in advance for each simulation campaign based on the expected running conditions of the LHC. However, the profile actually recorded differs from this, and since the pile-up profile influences the detector response and the reconstruction of all objects, it is necessary to correct this discrepancy. Following the procedure recommended for Run 3, each simulated event is weighted according to the ratio of the distribution of the number of interactions in the data to that in the simulation.

\subsubsection{Trigger efficiency scale factors}
%
Trigger paths apply equally to data and simulation, but their efficiency may differ significantly between the two, requiring a correction using scale factors evaluated event-by-event and defined as the ratio of the efficiencies measured in data to those measured in simulation. For the single-lepton paths and for the lepton and $\tau_h$ legs of the cross paths, these factors are derived from centrally provided measurements, as a function of the $p_T$ of the object, and, for $\tau_h$, of its decay mode. Their combination, however, depends on the strategy of each analysis. \subsubsection{Jets and \texorpdfstring{$p_T^\text{miss}$}{pTmiss}}
%
The jet energy is calibrated with the standard CMS jet energy corrections, which account for the pile-up contribution, the non-linearity of the detector response, and residual differences between data and simulation. Moreover, the Run 3 data show a significant deterioration in the jet energy resolution compared to the simulation. This discrepancy is corrected by degrading the resolution of the simulated jets. Accordingly, if the reconstructed jet can be matched to a jet built from generated particles, its four-momentum is scaled according to the $p_T$ difference between the two; otherwise, a random smearing is applied. All these corrections are propagated to $p_T^\text{miss}$, which is computed from the momenta of
the reconstructed objects and would otherwise be inconsistent with the corrected jets.

\subsubsection{Identification of \texorpdfstring{$b$}{b} jets}

\subsection{Process-specific corrections}
\label{subsec:bbtt_process_corrections}
%
The corrections described so far address the response of the detector and are common to all simulated samples. The simulation of the two dominant backgrounds, however, is also limited by the description of the physics process itself, which no object-level correction can account for. In both cases the shortcoming concerns the $p_T$ spectrum of the produced heavy particle, which is shaped by QCD radiation and by higher-order effects that the generators describe only approximately. In Drell--Yan production, the simulation does not correctly reproduce the $p_T$ of the $Z$ boson, and the corresponding correction is measured in data, in control channels with two light leptons of the same flavor where this process is practically the only one present. In top quark pair production, the $p_T$ of the top quark is corrected instead by means of a theoretical calculation at higher order than that achieved by the generator, without any measurement in data.

\section{Estimation of the jet\texorpdfstring{$\to\tau_h$}{ -> tau h} background}
\label{sec:bbtt_ff}
%
The contribution from events in which a hadronic jet is misidentified as a $\tau_h$, referred to simply as \textit{fakes} hereafter, does not correspond to a specific physical process, but rather comes from QCD multi-jet production, where the misidentified jet can originate from either a quark or a gluon; from top quark pair production, where it comes from the quarks in the decay; and from $W$ boson production in association with jets, where it comes from radiation in the initial or final state. 

The decision to model the \textit{fakes} contribution using data-driven methods is justified by two factors that render MC simulation inaccurate. The first factor is statistical and stems from the low probability that a quark or gluon jet will be identified as a $\tau_h$, ranging from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-3}$. Combined with the requirement for two additional jets that satisfy the $b$-tagging criteria, this would entail generating an unmanageably large number of simulated events to populate the signal regions with sufficient statistics. The second factor is the imperfect modeling of this misidentification in the simulation, primarily due to its dependence on detector effects. To address both limitations, a data-driven strategy is adopted, with a method that evolves from the one adopted in the Run 2 search, briefly recalled first.


\subsection{From the ABCD method to fake factors}
%
In Run 2 the method used addressed only the first of these, while misidentified candidates from the other two backgrounds remained within the simulated prediction of their respective processes and were therefore subject to the limitations described above. The estimation was performed using the ABCD method, which measures this contribution in regions where it is dominant and then transfers it to the signal region. The regions in which the measurement is taken are constructed by separately and jointly inverting the condition on the relative charge of the two $\tau$ candidates and the identification criterion for the $\tau_h$ candidate. Under this approach, the shape of the distribution was inferred directly from one of the defined regions, and its normalization from the ratio of the other two. The procedure thus assumed that a single transfer factor and a single template sufficient to describe a background that is actually a mixture of processes with different misidentification rates\footnote{The validity of these assumptions was the subject of a specific study conducted as part of this thesis, which is included in Appendix~\ref{app:abcd_validation}.}. \textcolor{red}{lo pongo en un apendice? lo pongo aquí? no lo pongo?}

\subsection{Derivation and application of the fake factors}
%
The \textit{fake factors} method adopted in Run 3 is based on the same principle of measuring the misidentification rate in sideband regions and transferring it to the region of interest. However, the data-driven approach is no longer limited to QCD multi-jet production but is extended to all three main processes. Consequently, the simulation of $t\bar{t}$ and $W$+jets is restricted to events in which the $\tau_h$ candidate corresponds to a generated particle, and those originating from a jet are now estimated entirely from the data, so that this contribution is not counted twice. For each process, a region where it dominates is defined, and the ratio of the number of candidates that satisfy the identification criterion of the signal region to those that do not is measured in that region. This ratio is known as the \textit{fake factor} and allows the number of events with a misidentified jet that contaminate the signal region to be predicted from the candidates that fail the criterion. The procedure is independent for each of the three contributing processes — which have different misidentification rates — and is parameterized based on the kinematics of the candidate rather than averaged over it, as was done in Run 2.\\


The method is based on four regions, represented in Figure~\ref{fig:ff_regions}: the signal region itself and three sideband regions, each enriched in one of the processes. The sideband regions are constructed using the selection described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_flow}, by relaxing or inverting some of the conditions that were deliberately left unapplied:
%
\begin{itemize}
    \item The QCD multi-jet region requires that the two $\tau$ candidates have the same charge to eliminate contributions from processes involving genuine $\tau\tau$ pairs, such as the signal, thereby achieving a purity of $99\%$. Furthermore, since its composition is particularly sensitive to topology and to the tagging requirements of the $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidate — in multi-jet events, tagged jets can be light, charm, or $b\bar{b}$ gluon-splitting pairs, which significantly alters the flavor composition and, with it, the misidentification rate — it is defined separately for each category of the analysis.
    \item The $t\bar{t}$ region is obtained by inverting the elliptic selection in the plane of the two reconstructed masses, a mechanism that serves to eliminate the signal and thus allows the requirement for opposite charges in the signal region to be maintained. A purity of $95\%$ is achieved.
    \item The $W$+jets region is obtained by selecting candidates with the same charge and imposing $p_T^\text{miss} > 60$ GeV and $m_T > 30$ GeV, with the aim of selecting the leptonic decay of the $W$ boson and excluding events containing jets tagged at the loose working point. Since these cuts also select any event involving neutrinos, including the signal and processes with a genuine $\tau\tau$ pair, it is again required that the candidates have the same charge. The purity achieved in this case is reduced to $80\%$, which is due to the fact that this process lacks a unique signature that distinguishes it from the others.
\end{itemize}

Each of the four regions is further subdivided into two depending on whether the second candidate in the pair — $\tau_h$ in all cases — passes the \textit{Medium} working point of the jet discriminator. In both halves, the contribution correctly predicted by the simulation, i.e., the genuine $\tau_h$s and the light leptons erroneously identified as $\tau_h$s, is subtracted from the data, leaving only misidentified jets.

Next, the number of data events in both halves is counted in each of the three sideband regions, and the \textit{fake factor} of the corresponding process, $FF_\text{MJ}$, $FF_{t\bar{t}}$, and $FF_{Wj}$, is calculated as the ratio of the number of events that exceed the \textit{Medium} WP to the number of events that do not. It is this factor that ultimately allows estimation of the number of jets that would pass the identification criterion, which cannot be isolated from the rest, based on the events that fail the criterion, which can be counted directly.

Once the fake factor is obtained, it is applied to half of the region that meets the same criteria as the signal region, except that it does not exceed the \textit{Medium} WP, and which is called the \textit{template} region. Therefore, multiplying the number of events in the template region by the \textit{fake factor} yields the prediction of misidentified jets in the signal region. However, since it is not possible to distinguish which process produces each jet in the template, a different factor cannot be applied to each one. Instead, the fractions of $t\bar{t}$, $W$+jets, and QCD multi-jets that make up the region are estimated: the first two are from the simulation as a function of the $p_T$ of the candidate, and the third is deduced as the remainder, resulting in a single combined \textit{fake factor},

\end{equation}

\section{Systematic uncertainties}
\label{sec:bbtt_systematics}
%
Each element that comprises the prediction described in the previous sections is associated with uncertainty, and the resulting sources of uncertainty fall into three distinct categories. Theoretical uncertainties reflect the limited knowledge of the cross sections and branching fractions used to normalize the simulated samples, as well as the effect that these same variations have on the fraction of events that survive selection. Experimental uncertainties stem from the measurement of the integrated luminosity and the calibration of the reconstructed objects: the object- and process-level corrections in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_corrections} are known with limited precision, and these are further compounded by the uncertainties affecting the estimation of misidentified jets in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_ff}, which depends on the calibration of the objects used to define their regions. Statistical uncertainties, finally, reflect the finite number of available events, both in the simulated samples and in the regions used for that estimation.

The following subsections review these sources, which are grouped according to the part of the prediction they affect, and their distribution among the three families is discussed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_results} when examining their impact on the final result. All of them are incorporated into the statistical model as nuisance parameters, which can modify either only the normalization of the affected process or also the shape of its distributions. The treatment of these parameters in the likelihood function is described in Section \ref{sec:stat_framework}\textcolor{red}{tengo que escribir esa seccion y ver si la referencio aqui, mi idea es que vaya en el capitulo II}.

\subsection{Luminosity and theoretical uncertainties}
%
As introduced in Section \ref{sec:???lumi}, the number of events that each simulated process contributes to the prediction is given by the product of the integrated luminosity, the production cross section, the decay branching fractions of the Higgs bosons involved, and the acceptance defined as the fraction of generated events that survive the selection. The first three factors scale the yield of the process without altering the shape of its distributions, while the last factor depends on the kinematics of the events and can modify it. Therefore, these factors correspond to sources of uncertainty independent of the correction applied to the simulation, and they affect all processes derived from the simulation.

\subsubsection{Integrated luminosity} 
%
The integrated luminosity for each year is derived from van der Meer scans and the stability of the response of the detectors used to monitor it, and is known to an accuracy of 1.4\% in 2022 \cite{CMS-PAS-LUM-22-001}, 1.3\% in 2023 \cite{CMS-DP-2024-068}, and 1.7\% in 2024 \cite{CMS-DP-2026-003}. Since these measurements share some systematic sources of uncertainty, the uncertainties are partially correlated with one another. Because luminosity is the factor by which all simulated samples are normalized, this uncertainty applies to the signal and to all backgrounds taken from the simulation, but not to the estimate of misidentified jets in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_ff}, which is obtained directly from the data.

\subsubsection{Cross sections and branching fractions} 
The cross sections used to normalize the simulated samples are fixed-order calculations, and their uncertainty is evaluated by varying the different inputs to the calculation: the renormalization and factorization scales, the set of parton distributions, and the value of $\alpha_s$.\\


Regarding the production of Higgs boson pairs via gluon fusion, the variation in renormalization and factorization scales, combined with the ambiguity in the top quark mass scheme, amounts to $^{+6}_{-23}\%$ \cite{Grazzini:2018bsd,Baglio:2020wgt}, while the choice of the set of parton distributions and the value of $\alpha_s$ together contribute 2.3\%. In production via vector boson fusion, the same sources result in variations of 0.05\% and 2.7\%, respectively \cite{Dreyer:2018qbw,Dreyer:2020xaj}. Given that the variation in the scales is contingent on the value of $\kappa_\lambda$, it is necessary to reevaluate it when interpreting the result under different assumptions.\\


For the simulated backgrounds, the recommended uncertainties for each process are used, ranging from 0.5 to 6\% \cite{LHCHWG-xsec-13p6TeV}, with two exceptions: the Drell–Yan process, whose normalization is not derived from its theoretical cross section but from the data-driven correction described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_corrections}; and the production of a Higgs boson in association with a $b\bar{b}$ pair, which enters precisely the $b$-tagged categories in which the signal is searched for, and whose predicted rate there is considerably less well known than that of the other single-Higgs processes \cite{bbH-modelling}. An additional normalization uncertainty of $100\%$ is therefore assigned to this contribution.\\

%whose predicted rate is affected by a large theoretical uncertainty arising from the treatment of the $b$-quark parton density and from the choice of flavor scheme in the calculation \cite{bbH-modelling}.


Finally, the $H\to b\bar{b}$ and $H\to\tau\tau$ decay branching fractions are known with a precision of $^{+1.2}_{-1.3}\%$ and $^{+1.7}_ {-1.6}\%$ \cite{deFlorian:2016spz}, and their uncertainty is assigned to all processes, including the signal, involving at least one Higgs boson.


\subsubsection{Acceptance} 
%
The theoretical variations considered above in the production process do not merely modify the inclusive cross section of each process. By altering the momentum distribution of the initial partons and the probability of emitting additional radiation, they also change the kinematics of the events and, consequently, the fraction of events that satisfy the selection criteria. This effect on the acceptance is independent of the change in the cross section and is therefore evaluated separately.

Accordingly, in addition to the renormalization and factorization scales and the set of parton distributions, a third source is introduced here: the variation in the parameters governing the parton shower simulation. This source was not mentioned in the previous section because it is applied to events that have already been generated and does not alter their total number, i.e., the total cross section. However, it does modify the number and hardness of the additional jets, and thereby affects the category into which each event is ultimately classified.

For each process and each category, the acceptance under each variation is recalculated as the ratio of the number of selected events to the number of generated events. In this way, the change in the inclusive cross section, which was previously accounted for in the previous section, is canceled out in the ratio and is not counted twice.

As a result, there are three sources of uncertainty in the acceptance — one for each type of variation — which are evaluated at each bin of the distribution that is subsequently fit, such that they can modify the shape of the distribution and not just the total yield of the category. These are calculated for the Drell–Yan process, $t\bar{t}$, single Higgs boson processes, and the signal. For the rest of the simulated backgrounds, grouped together as a minor contribution, their effect is negligible compared to the uncertainty in their own normalization. Of the three, the one associated with parton distributions is negligible, the one associated with scales remains around 1\%, and the one associated with the parton shower is the largest.\\ 


%que modifica la distribución de cada proceso, y que son las escalas de renormalización y factorización y las distribuciones de partones que ya aparecían como fuente de incertidumbre para la sección eficaz inclusiva, con el añadido de los parámetros de la lluvia de partones. Éstas se asignan a los procesos que contribuyen en cada categoría, y se evalúan intervalo a intervalo. Las asociadas a las distribuciones de partones resultan ser despreciables y las de las escalas se mantienen en torno al 1\%, mientras que las de la lluvia de partones son las mayores de las tres.\\


Uncertainties in luminosity, cross sections, and decay fractions multiply all events in the process they affect by the same factor. They are incorporated into the fit simply by specifying the allowed range of variation and rescaling the nominal distribution. The acceptance uncertainties, by contrast, affect each bin differently, and a distribution is constructed for each direction of variation by reweighting the simulated events with the weights provided by the generator for each variation, without repeating the reconstruction.

\subsection{Uncertainties in the object-level corrections}
%
The object-level corrections in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_object_corrections} are applied equally to all simulated processes, including the signal, so that their uncertainties affect the entire part of the prediction derived from the simulation. The majority of these corrections are taken from the CMS central recommendations, along with the uncertainties provided by the groups responsible for each object.


Components that do not stem from any central recommendation are accompanied by an uncertainty estimated along with the measurement itself. In the case of the 2024 QuadJet path, the $15\%$ lack of closure observed in the factorization validation is added to the measurement uncertainty. The identification factors for the $b\tau_h\tau_h$ channel candidates also carry an independent uncertainty. And in the tagging of $b$-jets, uncertainty is associated with the two components derived internally: the tagging efficiencies measured in the simulation — which, in 2024, translate the \textsc{UParT} scale factors by working points into a weight per event — and the dedicated calibration of large-radius jets misidentified as $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidates.\textcolor{red}{[CONFIRMAR lo del QuadJet]}\\

For each of these sources, two alternative distributions are constructed in addition to the nominal one by shifting the corresponding correction by one standard deviation upward and another downward. The method for obtaining them depends on the nature of the correction. When the correction is a weight — such as the scale factors for triggering, reconstruction, identification, isolation, and tagging — it suffices to refill the same histograms with the shifted weight. When it modifies the energy or momentum of the objects — the energy scales and resolutions of jets, electrons, and $\tau_h$ — the event itself may migrate between categories and change the input variables of the final discriminator, so it is necessary to repeat the entire analysis chain with the shifted correction. In these cases, the variation also propagates coherently to $p_T^\text{miss}$. None of these corrections is the same for all events, so both methods modify both the normalization and the shape of the distributions.\\

\subsection{Uncertainties in the process-specific corrections}
%
The uncertainties associated with the corrections to the two dominant backgrounds in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_process_corrections} are evaluated within the analysis itself, since neither stems from a central recommendation, and both are propagated as weights.\\


%Sus incertidumbres se evalúan por tanto dentro del propio análisis, y ambas se propagan como pesos, puesto que ambas correcciones lo son.
The Drell–Yan correction is the product of two factors, each of which contributes its own sources of uncertainty. The first factor is the fit of the ratio between the data and the simulation as a function of the $p_T$ of the pair of muons, and gives rise to the uncertainty in its free parameters. Rather than treating each parameter separately, they are grouped according to the fitting regime to which they belong, resulting in two independent sources — one associated with the Gaussian regime and the other with the linear regime. To construct each of these, the transition point resulting from the nominal fit is taken, and the content of the bins falling within the regime under consideration is shifted by $1.5$ times its statistical uncertainty, both upward and downward. The fit is repeated while leaving the bins of the other regime unchanged. The functions obtained in this way define the two variations and correspond to the bands shown in Figure \ref{fig:dy_fit}. \textcolor{red}{explicar mejor esto, a mi no me queda muy claro asi..}

The second factor is the additional normalization, measured over five bins of the number of central jets, $n_\text{jets} = 2, 3, 4, 5, \geq 6$, and three bins of the number of jets tagged as originating from a $b$-quark, $n_{b\,\text{jets}} = 0, 1, \geq 2$. This gives rise to the statistical uncertainty of the measurement itself, grouped into three independent sources — one for each bin of $n_{b\,\text{jets}}$ — and within each source, the five bins of $n_\text{jets}$ shift collectively.\\

On the other hand, the correction to the $p_T$ of the top quark is not a measurement but a calculation, and there is no independent estimate of its accuracy. It is therefore assigned an uncertainty equal to the magnitude of the correction itself. The upward variation is obtained by applying the factor twice, and the downward variation by disregarding it entirely, so that the nominal value lies midway between the two. This uncertainty affects only the top quark pair production samples, and not the single top quark production samples, which do not carry the correction.


\subsection{Uncertainties in the jet$\to\tau_h$ estimate}
%
Since the estimate in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_ff} does not originate from the simulation, none of the above uncertainties apply to it. Rather, as previously noted, these uncertainties stem from the calibration of the objects used to define their regions — which is incorporated into the values of the measured factors — and from the finite number of events in the regions in which the method is measured and applied.

The so-called \textit{fake factors} are obtained by counting events in regions bounded by cuts on reconstructed objects, such that a variation in the calibration of those objects shifts the measured value. To quantify this, the factors are derived again by shifting the energy scales and resolutions of the $\tau_h$, electrons, and jets — that is, under the same object energy shifts listed in Table \ref{tab:object_systematics}. The effect is found to be smaller than the statistical uncertainty of the method itself, except for the $t\bar{t}$ component. Instead of including a source for each of these variations, a simplified envelope is therefore adopted for each process, which grows linearly with the $p_T$ of the candidate: from $20\%$ at $20$ GeV to $100\% $ at $200$ GeV for $t\bar{t}$, and from $1\%$ to $5\%$ in that same range for $W$+jets and for multi-jet QCD. The three shift together and constitute a single source.

Additionally, the three \textit{fake factors} and the fractions with which they are combined in Equation \ref{eq:fake_factor} are measured over a finite number of events in the auxiliary regions. The resulting statistical uncertainty propagates by shifting the factors and fractions in a consistent manner, which constitutes a second source.

Finally, the statistics for the template region determine the number of events to be extrapolated. The prediction in each bin of the final distribution is derived as the product of the number of events observed in the template and the average \textit{fake factor} for that bin. These counts are frequently just a few events, or even zero, which is a regime in which the Gaussian approximation does not describe the Poisson distribution and in which an empty bin would be assigned zero uncertainty. Therefore, the Garwood interval \cite{Garwood:1936} at the $95\%$ confidence level is used\footnote{A $95\%$ confidence interval overestimates the statistical uncertainty of the template region by approximately a factor of two. This choice is deliberately conservative, as this is the first application of this method in the search.}, which respects the asymmetry of the distribution and is also defined for zero counts, and is applied to the prediction by multiplying it by that same average factor.\\

The first two sources are propagated as weights, since the \textit{fake factor} is applied as such to the events in the template region. The third, on the other hand, is not constructed event by event, but directly on the final distribution, bin by bin.



\subsection{Statistical uncertainties of the simulated samples}
%
Each process derived from the simulation is constructed from a finite number of generated events, so the content of each bin in the distribution being fitted is an estimate that is subject to statistical fluctuation. This uncertainty does not admit the treatment of the previous ones. Instead, the predicion in each bin is left free within its own statistical uncertainty, constrained by a term that accounts for the number of simulated events behind it. This is the procedure of Barlow and Beeston \cite{Barlow:1993dm}, and it amounts to adding as many parameters to the fit as there are bins in the distribution. The statistical limitation of the misidentified-jet estimate, which does not come from the simulation, has already been described in the previous subsection.\\\\


All sources of uncertainty affecting the prediction have been thoroughly addressed. The manner in which they are incorporated into the fit is described in Section \ref{sec:stat_framework}, and the correlation assigned to them across categories and data collection periods is described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_statistics}. The weight of each source of uncertainty on the measurement can only be determined once the fitted has been made, and this is examined in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_results}.\\

One these are in place, the prediction is complete, and can be compared with the data under appropriate conditions. This is the verification described in next Section \ref{sec:bbtt_controlplots}, where the band accompanying the prediction in its figures is the combination of the sources just described. This allows for the assessment of both whether the corrections in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_corrections} and the estimate in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_ff} reproduce the observations, and whether they do so within the margin assigned to them.

\section{Signal extraction}
\label{sec:bbtt_extraction}
%
Combining relevant information into a single number is a classification problem in which the decision of how much the properties of an event resemble those of the signal or background is made. The problem is addressed by employing a neural network trained on simulated events from both classes, capable of returning a continuous score whose distribution clusters signal events toward one end and background events toward the other. It should be noted that this score does not serve as an additional threshold in the analysis; therefore, no event is discarded based on it, but rather it serves solely as the observable whose shape is subject to adjustment. This approach is well-founded to ensure that background-dominated bins are not wasted but rather contribute to the measurement by constraining the normalization of the processes that populate them, while the high-score bins — where the signal is concentrated — are what provide sensitivity to the analysis.

Signal extraction relies on two separately trained classifiers, each optimized to one of the two production topologies. The classifier dedicated to vector boson fusion is applied exclusively in the VBF category, while the one tuned to gluon fusion is applied to the remaining nine categories, ensuring that in every category the events are scored by the network matched to the topology selected there.\\

The following subsections describe the architecture and the variables input to each of the classifiers, as well as the procedure used to train and validate them, and the mapping used to convert their output into the distribution that is ultimately fitted.


\subsection{Architecture and input variables}
%
Each classifier consists of three networks: two that generate variables from the inputs, and a third that classifies based on the variables produced by the first two.\\

The first is a \textit{Lorentz Boost Network} \cite{LBN}, whose objective is to process the input variables so that the network does not have to learn the relevant invariant combinations from scratch, but is instead directly provided with a set of them that have physical meaning. The \textit{Lorentz Boost Network} is designed to process the quadrimoments of the reconstructed objects and learn two distinct linear combinations: one that forms a set of new quadrimoments and another that acts as a reference system. From the transformed quadrimoments, it extracts quantities which are then passed on to the rest of the model. During the training process, the coefficients of both combinations are adjusted to enable the network to identify the relevant kinematic structures. This approach offers a distinct advantage by enforcing an architecture that can only construct quantities with kinematic meaning, rather than allowing the network to freely combine the components $(p_x, p_y, p_z, E)$.

The neutrino momentum regression network described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_flow} operates in parallel to facilitate the reconstruction of the mass of the $\tau\tau$ system. The outputs of both networks are concatenated with high-level input variables, and the resulting vector feeds into the third network. \textcolor{red}{preguntarle a Filip. En el AN no pone que use la red de regression para la red VBF, pero en sus presentaciones sí que pone que comparten arquitectura las dos redes...?}

The latter is a densely connected network \cite{DenseNet} in which each layer receives as input the outputs of all preceding layers, so that the discriminative power of the intermediate variables remains accessible at any point in the network and is not diluted as the network becomes deeper. It ends with a layer of three nodes with \textit{softmax} activation, which normalizes the three outputs to sum to one.\\

While the architecture described is common to both classifiers, they differ in the set of samples used to train each one, en las variables que reciben y en lo que representa su salida de señal. The classifier for gluon fusion is trained using simulated backgrounds and signal samples produced by that mechanism, without imposing any category selection, resulting in a single network covering the nine categories in which it is subsequently used. By contrast, while the samples used for the vector bosons fusion include those from both signal production mechanism, the classifier is trained on the VBF category, de forma que está optimizado para separar la señal from the backgrounds en la región VBF. Both classifiers produce three outputs, associated with the signal, the Drell–Yan production, and the production of top quark pairs, and in both cases the one used as an observable in the fit is the one associated with the signal.\\

\subsubsection{Input variables of the gluon fusion classifier}
%
Esta red recibe el momento transverso, la pseudorrapidez, el ángulo acimutal y la masa de los dos candidatos $\tau$, de los dos candidatos a jet de $b$ y del jet de radio grande, junto con los cuadrimomentos de los sistemas que estos forman  — los dos candidatos a bosón de Higgs y el candidato $HH$ —  y el momento transverso perdido. A ello se añaden las salidas de los algoritmos de etiquetado: los discriminantes frente a jets de $b$ y de encanto de cada jet, y la puntuación de \textsc{HH-BTag} presentada en el Capítulo \ref{ch:taggers}, con la que el trabajo desarrollado en la primera parte de esta tesis entra directamente en la extracción de la señal. Por último, un conjunto de variables categóricas identifica el canal del suceso, el modo de desintegración y la carga de cada candidato $\tau$, y si el candidato $H\to b\bar{b}$ se ha reconstruido en la topología resuelta o en la fusionada.

\subsubsection{Input variables of the vector boson fusion classifier}
%
La red dedicada a la señal VBF se sirve de la misma información que para el gluon fusino classifier, pero se le suman los cuadrimomentos de los dos jets característicos de esa topología junto con su discriminante entre quark y gluon. Así mismo, se emplean en este caso high-level variables construidas to condense information that the network would have difficulty extracting on its own from the low-level variables, y optimizadas para la topología VBF. These include the rapidity gaps between the two $b$-jet candidates and between the two characteristic jets of the VBF topology; four Fox–Wolfram moments \cite{FoxWolfram}, which characterize the angular distribution of the event’s hadronic activity; and the variable
%
\begin{equation}
    M_{\chi} = m_{bb\tau\tau} - \left(m_{bb} - m_H\right) - \left(m_{\tau\tau} - m_H\right),
    \label{eq:mchi}
\end{equation}
%
which corrects the reconstructed mass of the $HH$ system by subtracting the deviations of the masses of the two candidates from the nominal value of the Higgs boson, thereby compensating for the effect of the experimental resolution on both.

Finally, it should be noted that to determine the set of high-level variables ultimately used, the starting point was a comprehensive list of variables that previous studies had identified as discriminants in searches of this type, and an iterative procedure was applied to that list. In this way, the network is trained, and the loss in performance caused by permuting the values of each variable separately — averaged over several iterations — is evaluated, in order to discard the least influential variables before retraining, in a similar way as described in \ref{permutation_feature}. \subsection{Training and performance}
% 
Ha quedado descrita la arquitectura que fija la forma de los dos discriminantes. El siguiente paso consiste en entrenar la red para fijar los coeficientes con los que se combinan las entradas en cada paso. El entrenamiento de una red ya se describió en la Sección \ref{subsec:training} para los algoritmos del Capítulo \ref{ch:taggers}, y aquí se sigue un procedimiento similar, común a las dos redes.\textcolor{red}{preguntarle a Filip si es igual el training en las dos redes} 


El conjunto simulado se reparte en función del número de suceso y se entrena un modelo por cada parte, de manera que cada uscesos acaba evaluado por una red que no lo ha visto. En este caso, el conjunto se divide en cinco subconjuntos, y se entrena una red por cada uno de ellos utilizando los otros cuatro, reservando dentro de cada entrenamiento una carta parte de los sucesos para validación. A ello se le añade un promedio sobre inicializaciones, de forma que cada parte se entrenan varias redes partiendo de valores iniciales distintos para sus parámetros, y la puntuación final se obtiene como la media de sus predicciones, de modo que el resultado deja de depender de una inicialización concreta \cite{CMS-PAS-HIG-25-008}.
