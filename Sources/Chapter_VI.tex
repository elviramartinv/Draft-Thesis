\chapter{Results and combination with the Run 2 dataset}
\label{ch:bbtt_results}
%
With the expected signal and backgrounds distributions fully specified, the next step is to convert its comparison with the data into a measurement. However, the low cross section of the signal being sought means that this comparison cannot be made directly. The excess that would result from the production of Higgs boson pairs is small when compared to the uncertainty on the backgrounds that accompany it. Under these conditions, a comparison between an observed number and a predicted number is not meaningful. Instead, the question is how much signal the data can accommodate once the background is allowed to be adjusted within the limits of its uncertainties.

Accordingly, the statistical model treats each source of systematic uncertainty as an additional degree of freedom in the fit, which can readjust normalizations and reshape distributions within the limits allowed by each uncertainty. However, deviating from the nominal value comes at a cost, so it is done only to the extent that the data require it. Therefore, in the observable fitted, the low-score bins — populated almost exclusively by background — serve as the anchoring points for the fit, while the high-score bins are the only ones that respond appreciably to the presence of a signal. Given that the fifty signal regions share processes and calibrations, the readjustment must be consistent across all of them, so the fit is performed simultaneously on the entire set.

Given the relative signal size and the analyzed 172\,fb$^{-1}$ of data collected during the considered Run 3 periods, it follows that the expected sensitivity of the analysis is not sufficient to observe the production of Higgs boson pairs at the rate predicted by the standard model (SM). Therefore, the result is not a direct measurement of the signal, but rather an upper limit on how much signal might have gone undetected, along with the resulting constraint on the couplings that govern the process under study. These results also show how much this margin has narrowed with respect to previous analyses, and to what extent the Run 3 conditions — new triggers and improved identification algorithms such as those of Chapter \ref{ch:taggers} — have done more than merely accumulate data.

For this reason the analysis does not stop at the Run-3 data, but exploits all the data available in this final state. This measurement is combined with the published Run-2 measurement in the same final state, which requires determining which uncertainties are common to both analyses and which are not. It is also worth placing the result in the context of searches for Higgs pair production in other final states, since the $bb\tau\tau$ search is just one of several that target the same process, each with its own balance between the branching fraction and the background to be suppressed. Likewise, a comparison with results from the ATLAS experiment provides a broader picture of where this measurement stands and what actually limits it.\medskip

The present chapter is structured as follows. The construction of the statistical model that meets the aforementioned requirements is the subject of Section \ref{sec:bbtt_statistics}, which introduces the statistical formalism on which the measurement rests together with the decisions specific to this study. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_results} first presents the post-fie distributions of the discriminant and then the results derived from it, examining the factors that limit the measurement. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_combination} combines the obtained result with the published measure on the Run 2 dataset at the level of the statistical model and without reprocessing those data. Finally, Section \ref{sec:bbtt_comparison} compares the result with the other searches for the same process, \textcolor{red}{and Section \ref{sec:bbtt_prospects} concludes the chapter by.....}



\section{The statistical model of the search}
\label{sec:bbtt_statistics}
%
The comparison between the prediction and the data is performed using a binned maximum likelihood fit, carried out simultaneously across the fifty signal regions of the analysis. In each region the observable is the output of the classifier assigned to it, using the adaptive binning described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction}, so that the total likelihood is the product of the contributions from all bins in all regions. The statistical treatment follows the frequentist approach common to all CMS analyses, developed jointly with the ATLAS Collaboration for the Higgs boson searches~\cite{ATLAS:2011tau}, and its implementation is based on \textsc{Combine}~\cite{CMS-combine}, the statistical analysis and combination tool used at CMS. The formalism on which it rests is introduced in the following subsections together with its application to this search.


The fit is determined by three decisions,  each of which is addressed in one of the subsections below. The first is the measurement itself, i.e., how the likelihood is constructed, which parameters are left free and how the dependence of the signal on them is modeled, which is the subject of Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_likelihood}. The second concerns how the systematic uncertainties discussed in the previous chapter enter the fit. This includes the way in which each nuisance parameter modifies the prediction and the scheme by which they are correlated across periods, categories, and channels, as well as how the templates are handled before reaching the fit, and is covered in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_nuisances}. Finally, Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_teststat} addresses the third aspect, which consists of describing the test statistic from which each result is constructed and how both the observed and the expected results are obtained from it.


\subsection{The fit and its parameters of interest}
\label{subsec:bbtt_likelihood}
% 
% de qué está hecha la predicción en cada bin y de dónde viene cada parte
In each bin of each signal region, the expected number of events is the sum of the contributions from all the processes that populate that region. As previously noted, the simulation provides the signal in its two production modes and the backgrounds from $t\bar{t}$, Drell--Yan, single top quark and $tW$, $W$+jets, diboson and triboson production, and single Higgs boson production, which in this search constitutes an irreducible background. The contribution from jets misidentified as $\tau_h$ is extracted from the data using the method described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_ff}. This distinction has consequences that must be considered in the fit, since the object calibrations enter as nuisance parameters of their own for the simulated processes, whereas their effect on the estimate obtained from the data was quantified during its derivation and was absorbed into the associated envelopes.


Since the events that populate each bin are produced independently of one another, the number observed in each of them, $n_i$, follows a Poisson distribution whose mean is the expected number of events, written as the sum of a signal contribution $s_i$, scaled by the parameter of interest $\mu$, and a background contribution $b_i$. Both depend on the set of nuisance parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ that encode the uncertainties of the prediction. Moreover, the number of events observed in different bins are statistically independent, so that the probability of observing all of them simultaneously, which defines the likelihood function, is the product of the Poisson probabilities of all bins $i$ of all signal regions, written as
\begin{equation}
    L(\mu,\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{i} \frac{\left(\mu\, s_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + b_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^{n_i}}{n_i!}\, e^{-\left(\mu\, s_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + b_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)} \;\prod_{k} p(\tilde{\theta}_k\,|\,\theta_k),
    \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
where the second product contains a constraint term for each nuisance parameter $\theta_k$, which carries the external information available on it, $\tilde{\theta}_k$, and whose form is described in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_nuisances}~\cite{CMS-combine}. The statistical uncertainty of the data therefore requires no parameter of its own, since the Poisson probabilities of the first product already describe how the observed number of events fluctuates around its expected value. The fit consists in finding the values of $\mu$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ that maximize this function, which in practice is done by minimizing $-2\ln L$ — which turns the product into a sum that is numerically more manageable and whose variations around the minimum, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_teststat}, can be directly interpreted in terms of confidence intervals.


% qué está fijo y qué es libre
Each contribution enters the fit with the normalization and shape assigned to it by the prediction built in the previous chapter, and can only deviate from them through the nuisance parameters associated with their uncertainties. None is left free, so the only parameter that the fit determines without any constraint term in Equation \ref{eq:likelihood} is the parameter of interest, which is defined based on the result to be interpreted. In this regard, two alternatives are considered: one for measuring the strength with which the signal appears in the data — which constitutes the natural parameter when the goal is to measure or set a limit on the production rate — and another for constraining the parameters that govern the process — where the coupling modifiers themselves constitute the parameter of interest.\medskip


%ya que el ajuste no dispone de ningún grado de libertad con el que abosrber un fondo mal modelado más alla de lo que su propia incertidumrbe consiente. 

The signal strength, $\mu$, is defined as the factor by which the cross section predicted by the SM must be multiplied to reproduce the observed cross section. In the fit, $\mu$ multiplies the expected number of signal events in each bin of each signal region, thereby modifying the height of the signal distribution but not its shape. Its value is continuous and is determined by the fit, with $\mu = 1$ and $\mu = 0$ merely marking the SM hypothesis and the absence of signal, respectively.

This parameter is used to derive two measurements, one that is inclusive of both production modes and another that is exclusive to vector boson fusion. This split reflects the marked asymmetry between the two modes, as the cross section for gluon fusion is nearly twenty times larger than that for VBF. Consequently, the inclusive measurement is barely sensitive to the VBF contribution, which is tied to the dominant mode and, therefore, it must be measured separately so that the data can constrain it.

The inclusive measurement uses a single $\mu$ for both production modes. By scaling both templates by the same factor, the ratio between them is fixed at the value predicted by the SM, so that the only thing the fit determines is the overall scale of their sum. The exclusive measurement replaces that parameter with another that multiplies only the templates for vector boson fusion. It is the dedicated VBF category of Section \ref{sec:bbtt_flow} that provides the necessary sensitivity by isolating a region where this production mode accounts for a much larger fraction of the total than it does in the rest of the analysis.\medskip


In the second alternative, a coupling modifier replaces $\mu$ as the parameter of interest and is fitted directly to the data, while the other couplings are held at their SM values; this yields confidence intervals for $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\kappa_{2V}$\footnote{The same procedure applies to $\kappa_t$ and $\kappa_V$, for which this search does not improve upon the constraints provided by single-Higgs-boson production measurements. Therefore, they are not reported here.}. This treatment is possible because the parametrization described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples} allows the signal prediction at any point in coupling space to be expressed as a linear combination of the simulated samples, and it does so bin by bin rather than just for the total cross section, so that each signal template is a known analytic function of the couplings $s_i(\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{\theta})$, which replaces the term $\mu\,s_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ of Equation \ref{eq:likelihood}. Unlike $\mu$, a coupling does not merely scale the height of the signal distribution. A change in $\kappa_\lambda$ shifts the invariant mass spectrum of the $HH$ system and thereby modifies the distribution of the observable that is fitted.



The same parametrization can also be used the other way around. The couplings are held fixed at a point in coupling space, and the parameter of interest is again $\mu$, which provides the limit on the cross section as a function of the coupling. A value of the coupling is excluded when the limit obtained with it falls below the cross section that the theory predicts at that same value, and the uncertainty on the boundary of the resulting interval follows from the bands of the limit itself. By scanning over a grid in two of them, the exclusion contours in the plane they define can be determined.\medskip


Once the parameter of interest has been defined in each case, the model is fully determined except for its remaining parameters, which carry the uncertainties and on which the burden of covering any residual difference between the prediction and the data falls entirely. The way in which they act on the prediction, the scheme by which they are correlated and the treatment their templates receive before entering the fit are addressed in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_nuisances}.

\subsection{The nuisance parameters of the fit}
\label{subsec:bbtt_nuisances}
% constraints
Each source of uncertainty is represented in the likelihood of Equation \ref{eq:likelihood} by a nuisance parameter $\theta_k$, defined so that $\theta_k = 0$ reproduces the nominal prediction and $\theta_k = \pm1$ corresponds to a shift of the source by one standard deviation. The external knowledge available on each source enters through its constraint term, a Gaussian distribution of unit width centered at the nominal value, $p(\tilde{\theta}_k\,|\,\theta_k) \propto e^{-(\theta_k-\tilde{\theta}_k)^2/2}$ with $\tilde{\theta}_k = 0$, which penalizes any deviation from it~\cite{CMS-combine}. However, a nuisance parameter does not represent a fixed uncertainty, but rather a quantity whose value is determined simultaneously by the external information that constrains it and by the fit data themselves. Therefore, when the data are more sensitive to a given source, they can be determined with greater precision than external calibration from which its uncertainty was derived — as typically occurs in bins dominated by background, where the number of events is large. In that case, the actual uncertainty of the parameter is reduced after the fit, and the nuisance parameter is said to be constrained. 

% como actúa cada nuisance
The way in which a nuisance parameter acts on the prediction depends on whether its source modifies only the normalization of the affected process or also its shape, as indicated in the \textit{Type} column of Table \ref{tab:systematics_summary}. In the first case, the expected yield of the process is multiplied by a factor $(1+\delta_k)^{\theta_k}$, where $\delta_k$ is the relative uncertainty of the source. This corresponds to a log-normal distribution of the yield, which, unlike a Gaussian one, never allows it to become negative, regardless of the size of the uncertainty; when the upward and downward variations differ, two different values of $\delta_k$ are used on each side of the nominal value. In the second case, the two shifted templates constructed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics} provide the content of each bin at $\theta_k = \pm1$, and the content at any intermediate value is obtained bin by bin by interpolating between these two templates and the nominal one, using a smooth polynomial for $|\theta_k| < 1$ that preserves the continuity of the prediction and of its first derivatives, and a linear extrapolation beyond~\cite{CMS-combine}.

The treatment of each of the sources detailed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics} depends on its origin, which determines the three decisions that remain to be made, that is, which of them share parameters across regions; what modifications the shifted templates undergo before entering the fit; and how the limited statistical precision of the prediction itself is incorporated.\medskip


% correlación
Each source of uncertainty affects the prediction in more than one signal region, and it is therefore necessary to determine whether its effect on one region is tied to its effect in the others, since the model may either use a single parameter that shifts them all at once or assign independent ones. Accordingly, a source is correlated when it has a common origin across all affected regions — as is the case with theoretical predictions or calibrations derived jointly for the entire dataset — and is uncorrelated when it is measured independently in each period, or when it stems from a finite number of events — in which case fluctuations in one region bear no relation to those in another. The fifty signal regions differ both in category and in data-taking period, and what distinguishes them as far as these uncertainties are concerned are the conditions under which the data were recorded and the calibrations derived for them. The division is therefore made between periods, with a single parameter shared by the ten categories of a given period and independent parameters from one period to the next.

Once the correlation scheme has been fixed, the two shifted templates for each source — constructed in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics} by shifting the corresponding correction in both directions — undergo some corrections before the fit to ensure that all variations behave consistently under interpolation.

\subsection{Extraction of the results}
\label{subsec:bbtt_teststat}
%
With the model complete, all the results are extracted from a single test statistic, constructed from the ratio between the maximum value of the likelihood when the parameter of interest is fixed at a given value and its absolute maximum,
\begin{equation}
    q(\mu) = -2\ln\frac{L(\mu,\hat{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_\mu)}{L(\hat{\mu},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})},
    \label{eq:test_statistic}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ are the values that maximize the likelihood, and $\hat{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_\mu$ are those of the nuisance parameters that maximize it when $\mu$ is held fixed. This procedure, known as \textit{profiling}, readjusts the nuisance parameters at each value of the parameter of interest so that they describe the data as well as possible, and thus propagates the uncertainties they represent to the result. By construction, $q(\mu)$ vanishes at the best-fit value and grows as $\mu$ moves away from it, providing a measure of the incompatibility between the data and the hypothesis that the parameter of interest takes that value; the same definition applies when the parameter of interest is a coupling modifier. The practical importance of this statistic is that, when the number of events is sufficiently large, its distribution approaches a $\chi^2$ distribution with as many degrees of freedom as parameters of interest, regardless of the values of the nuisance parameters~\cite{Wilks:1938dza,Cowan:2010js}. This asymptotic approximation, used for all the results of this search, allows them to be computed analytically without generating pseudo-experiments.

Consequently, the signal strength is the value $\hat{\mu}$ that maximizes the likelihood, and its $68\,\%$ confidence interval is bounded by the two values at which $q(\mu)$ reaches unity, a threshold that rises to $3.84$ for the $95\,\%$ confidence level. Confidence intervals for the couplings are derived in the same way from the values that the statistic takes along a scan, while in a two-dimensional scan the thresholds become $2.30$ and $5.99$ and delimit the excluded regions in the plane spanned by the two couplings. 

The width of the interval on $\mu$ reflects all the uncertainties of the model at once, and the contribution of each of them is obtained by removing it. To this end, the scan is repeated with the nuisance parameters of a given group fixed at their best-fit values, so that they can no longer move and the interval becomes narrower. Since independent contributions add in quadrature, the uncertainty attributable to that group is the quadratic difference between the width of the full interval and that of the reduced one. When all the nuisance parameters are fixed, the only remaining source of variation is the Poisson fluctuation of the data, so that the width of the resulting interval corresponds to the statistical uncertainty.

Finally, the significance quantifies the compatibility of the data with the background-only hypothesis and follows from the same statistic evaluated at $\mu = 0$, $q_0 = q(0)$, which compares how well the data are described without signal with how well they are described by the best fit. A large value of $q_0$ therefore indicates that the data require a signal contribution, whereas $q_0$ is set to zero when $\hat{\mu} < 0$, since a deficit of events cannot count as evidence for a signal. In the asymptotic regime, the significance in standard deviations is then simply $Z = \sqrt{q_0}$.\medskip

Upper limits require a modified version of the statistic, since only a signal strength larger than the one favored by the data should count as evidence against a given hypothesis:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{q}(\mu) =
    \begin{cases}
        -2\ln\dfrac{L(\mu,\hat{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_\mu)}{L(\hat{\mu},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})} & 0 \leq \hat{\mu} \leq \mu, \\[2ex]
        -2\ln\dfrac{L(\mu,\hat{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_\mu)}{L(0,\hat{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_0)} & \hat{\mu} < 0, \\[2ex]
        0 & \hat{\mu} > \mu.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:test_statistic_limits}
\end{equation}
From the observed value of this statistic, two probabilities are computed: $p_\mu$, the probability of obtaining a value at least as large under the hypothesis of a signal of strength $\mu$ on top of the background, and $1-p_b$, the same probability under the background-only hypothesis. The upper limit is then determined at the $95\,\%$ confidence level using the modified frequentist criterion~\cite{Junk:1999kv,Read:2002hq,ATLAS:2011tau}, which excludes those values of $\mu$ for which
\begin{equation}
    \text{CL}_s(\mu) = \frac{p_\mu}{1-p_b} < 0.05 .
    \label{eq:cls}
\end{equation}
Normalizing $p_\mu$ by $1-p_b$ prevents the exclusion of signals to which the analysis is not sensitive, which would occur when using $p_\mu$ alone in the presence of a downward fluctuation of the background, at the cost of a conservative result. The limit is computed for both the inclusive and VBF-exclusive measurements and, by repeating the same procedure for a sequence of coupling values, with the coupling fixed at each value, the limit as a function of that coupling is also obtained.\medskip

Each of these results is obtained twice, first in its expected version and then in its observed version. The expected version describes the outcome that the analysis would obtain under a given hypothesis in the absence of statistical fluctuations, and thus measures its sensitivity before examining the data in the signal regions. It is derived from an Asimov dataset, built by replacing the content of each bin with its prediction under that hypothesis, such that the fit reproduces exactly the parameter values used to construct it. In the asymptotic approximation, this dataset provides the median expected result together with the $68\,\%$ and $95\,\%$ confidence bands of their distribution~\cite{Cowan:2010js}. Expected limits are computed assuming the background-only hypothesis $\mu = 0$ — which indicate that could be excluded in the absence of signal —, whereas expected significance and confidence intervals are evaluated assuming the SM signal hypothesis, $\mu = 1$ — which indicate how significant the signal would be and how precisely it would be measured if the SM prediction holds.


The expected version was the only figure of merit available while the analysis was being built, since the data in the signal regions remained hidden until all the decisions of the previous chapter had been taken, so as to prevent any of them from being influenced by the observed fluctuations. Once the analysis was finalized, the signal regions were unblinded and the same quantities were computed using the observed data, which constitutes the result of the measurement. The comparison between the two, together with the expected bands, indicates whether the data deviate from the hypothesis under which the expected result was obtained.

All of these results are calculated not only for the entire dataset, but also separately for each data-taking period, for each channel, and for each category group, which allows the breakdown shown in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_results} and reveals the source of the sensitivity.\bigskip

The model constructed in this way is fitted to the data in a single operation, which simultaneously yields the signal strength value and the values of all the nuisance parameters. The fit therefore returns not only a measurement but also a revised prediction — the result of applying the shifts assigned to each process — and comparing that prediction with the data reveals whether the fit required deviating from the nominal parameter values. \section{Results from the Run 3 dataset}
\label{sec:bbtt_results}
%
The number of signal events produced in the analyzed dataset is the product of the integrated luminosity, the production cross section, and the branching fraction to the final state under consideration, $N = \mathcal{L}\,\sigma_{HH}\,\mathcal{B}(HH\to bb\tau\tau)$. Using the values given in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples} for the cross sections of the two production modes and a branching fraction of $7.3\,\%$, the $172$ fb$^{-1}$ collected during Run 3 contain about 450 signal events. Of these, the acceptance of the selection, together with the trigger, reconstruction, and identification efficiencies, leave only a small fraction, which is spread across the fifty signal regions and accompanied by a background several orders of magnitude larger. \textcolor{red}{cutflow} This is the magnitude of the excess whose extraction is the fit described in the previous section, and which constitutes the subject of the results presented below.

\subsection{Post-fit distributions}

The post-fit distributions are obtained by applying to each process the values that the fit has assigned to the nuisance parameters, so that they represent the prediction as the model leaves it once it has been confronted with the data. Figure \ref{fig:postfit_2024} shows them for the 2024 data-taking period, which has the highest integrated luminosity and is presented as a representative example, since the other perdios exhibit similar behavior. The ten signal regions are plotted along a single axis, grouped by category and, within each category, by channel, and in all of them the bins are ordered according to the discriminant score. The two signal contributions are superimposed, scaled to the signal strength that best fits the data.

The composition of the background is the one anticipated in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_controlplots}. The contribution from jets misidentified as $\tau_h$ dominates in all regions, followed by the production of top quark pairs and Drell--Yan, while the single Higgs boson production and all other processes constitute minor contributions. Within each region, the yield decreases sharply as one moves toward higher scores. This is the behavior imposed by the adaptive binning described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction}, which distributes the signal uniformly, so that the last bins in each group are both the least populated and the most sensitive. The signal, even when scaled to the fitted strength, remains several orders of magnitude below the background in all cases.\medskip

In contrast to the comparison of Section \ref{sec:bbtt_controlplots}, which was made before the fit and in which a discrepancy would have indicated a problem with the modeling, here the agreement is largely guaranteed by construction, since the fit used the nuisance parameters to achieve it. However, this capacity for absorption is not unlimited, since the parameters can only shift within the limits allowed by their constraints. Therefore, a discrepancy greater than that margin would still be visible, which justifies examining the post-fit distributions before interpreting any results. The simultaneous nature of the fit also requires checking each region separately, since a discrepancy localized in one region may be offset by the rest and not manifest itself in any of the final numbers.

The figure confirms that the ratio of the observed data to the prediction is consistent with unity along the entire axis, with the largest fluctuations concentrated in the least populated bins, and that none of the ten regions deviates from the expected behavior. The uncertainty band is also significantly narrower than that of the pre-fit distributions, because the bins populated almost exclusively by background contain enough events for the fit to determine several of the nuisance parameters with greater precision than the uncertainty initially assigned to them. This behavior is confirmed by the post-fit values of the nuisance parameters themselves, which remain compatible with their nominal ones, so that the agreement seen here does not rest on any single parameter having absorbed a large correction.

\subsection{Constraints on the Higgs boson couplings}

To fully leverage all available data, the next step involves combining both measurements at the \textit{datacard} level, which affects only the joint statistical interpretation and does not require reprocessing the Run 2 data using the techniques described here, which would be far from straightforward. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_combination} provides a detailed explanation of the process, along with a discussion of which uncertainties it is legitimate to correlate between two analyses that are separated by a change in collision energy and data-acquisition conditions, and of how much each of the results obtained here improves with it.




\section{Combination with the Run 2 dataset}
\label{sec:bbtt_combination}
%
The search for nonresonant production of Higgs boson pairs in the $bb\tau\tau$ final state using the Run 2 dataset \cite{CMS-HIG-20-010} analyzed the $138$ fb$^{-1}$ of data collected between 2016 and 2018 at a center-of-mass energy of $13$ TeV. It therefore constitutes a set of events disjoint from those collected during Run 3, recorded during different periods and at different energies, which makes them statistically independent and allows for the combination of both datasets.

The combination presented here is performed at the datacard level, which means that each analysis contributes its own binned distributions, with its own binning, templates, and nuisance parameters, and the joint likelihood results from the product of the contributions of all the regions of both. Under this approach the data are not reprocessed. Consequently, the events from Run 2 do not benefit from the triggers, identification algorithms, or discriminant developed for Run 3, and its contribution remains fixed at the sensitivity that analysis originally achieved.

However, as already noted, the strategy followed in Run 3 largely derives from the Run 2 search, so that both analyses share a similar structure. The combination, although it requires no reprocessing, is therefore not immediate, as two analyses that share methods and calibrations also share some of their sources of uncertainty.

Therefore, the combination must determine which of the nuisance parameters of the two analyses describe the same source of uncertainty and must consequently be identified with one another. Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comb_procedure} addresses this decision and how the joint likelihood is constructed, and Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comb_results} presents the results obtained from it, compared in each case with those provided from each dataset separately.


\subsection{The combined statistical model}
\label{subsec:bbtt_comb_procedure}
%
The combined likelihood is the union of all the binned distributions from the two analyses. In Run 3 these are the fifty outputs of the discriminant described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction}, one per signal region. In Run 2, these are the outputs of its own classifier, evaluated across the eight orthogonal categories defined by that search and broken down by channel and by year. The result is a single fit over all of them in which each region retains its own histogram and what is shared is the set of parameters. It is performed in one step, using the same parameters of interest defined in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_likelihood}, which scale the signal from both sets relative to the SM prediction corresponding to the energy of each, $13$ TeV in Run 2 and $13.6$ TeV in Run 3.\medskip


To ensure consistency in the combination, it is necessary to establish how the nuisance parameters of one analysis relate to those of the other. The criterion is the same as the one that governed the correlations within Run 3, i.e., the origin of the uncertainty. The theoretical uncertainties described in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_theory_unc} stem from the same calculations in both searches, so each of them is described by a single parameter common to the two. This group includes those associated with the Higgs boson branching fractions, both in their effect on the normalization and on the shape; the choice of the set of parton distributions; the value of $\alpha_s$; the renormalization and factorization scales; and, notably, the one that combines the latter with the ambiguity in the top quark mass scheme for the signal produced by gluon fusion, which is the largest of the theoretical uncertainties affecting the signal. In contrast, experimental uncertainties are handled as independent, as is appropriate for datasets with differing detector conditions and calibration procedures.\medskip

A second consideration is that the two datasets were recorded at different energies, so that the same value for the couplings does not yield the same prediction in one dataset as in the other, since both the signal and the background cross sections differ between $13$ and $13.6$ TeV. The signal parametrization described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples} is thus constructed separately for each dataset, based on its own simulated samples and the corresponding cross sections, but using the same matrix inversion method and following the recommendations of the LHC Higgs Working Group. In this way, each point in coupling space is assigned, in each dataset, the prediction corresponding to its own energy.

Furthermore, this dependence is not limited to the signal but also includes part of the background. The Higgs boson branching fractions and the single Higgs production rates also depend on the coupling modifiers — though with different functional dependencies — and their variation is likewise included in the model. If this were not done, a scan in $\kappa_\lambda$ would shift the signal while leaving unchanged a background that in reality moves with it.\medskip

One final clarification is in order regarding the Run 2 result shown in this combination. Since the publication of that search, some of its theoretical uncertainties have been updated, including through the introduction of an additional uncertainty on the heavy-flavor associated single Higgs production. The datacards used here therefore incorporate these updates, so the result presented separately for Run 2 may differ slightly from that published in Ref.~\cite{CMS-HIG-20-010}.

With the likelihood function constructed in this way, the fit and the extraction of the results follow the procedure described in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_teststat}, with the only difference being the set of regions over which they are performed.

\section{Comparison with other HH searches}
\label{sec:bbtt_comparison}
%
To ensure a fair comparison of different searches, it is necessary to specify the terms on which they are compared. While the quantities each of them reports are presented in this chapter — although not all searches cite all three — the measurement common to all of them is the upper limit on the production rate expressed in units of the SM prediction, which in all cases is reported as both the observed value and the expected value, along with the confidence intervals on the coupling modifiers. Of the two values, the expected value quantifies the sensitivity achieved by the analysis and is therefore the one on which the comparison rests, while the observed value additionally reflects the particular event yields recorded in each dataset. 

The sensitivity achieved in each case depends on a number of factors, including the integrated luminosity being analyzed, what the phase space of the final state allows from the outset, as described in Section \ref{subsec:HH_BR}, and the strategy with which that room has been exploited. Therefore, it is essential to consider these factors when evaluating the results of the comparison.

Given that the integrated luminosity on which the sensitivity depends may differ from one analysis to another, each value is quoted together with the luminosity it corresponds to, and, where the comparison calls for it, rescaled to a common luminosity. The composition of the dataset may also vary between searches, since not all of them cover the same data-taking periods or the same center-of-mass energy, so that a difference may simply reflect that one analysis includes data that the other has not yet incorporated.

Looking at other final states shows how this sensitivity is distributed across channels and what each one contributes to the whole. Within CMS, the $4b$ and $bb\gamma\gamma$ final states have been analyzed using Run 3 data, which are contemporaneous with the result obtained here, and are therefore the ones against which this result is placed. By contrast, keeping the final state fixed and changing the experiment brings the strategy into view, and this is addressed by the search published by the ATLAS experiment for the $bb\tau\tau$ decay channel.

On this basis the two comparisons that follow are built taking from each publication the observed and expected limits, as well as the confidence intervals on the couplings modifiers and the luminosity to which they correspond.

\subsection{The HH program at CMS in Run 3}
\label{bbtt_comp_cms}
%
The three final states that dominate the sensitivity to Higgs boson pair production are, according to the trade-off described in Section \ref{subsec:HH_BR}, $4b$, $bb\tau\tau$, and $bb\gamma\gamma$. Both $4b$ and $bb\gamma\gamma$ have published measurements using Run 3 data, and these measurements show how the sensitivity is currently distributed among the channels.\medskip

%limits
The $4b$ final state has been analyzed using the $62$ fb$^{-1}$ of data collected in 2022 and 2023, reconstructing each Higgs boson as either a pair of small-radius jets or a single large-radius jet, and treating the two topologies --- resolved and \textit{merged} --- as mutually exclusive \cite{CMS:2026znu}. The combination of the two excludes production rates greater than $4.4$ times the SM prediction, with an expected value of $4.4$, whereas separately their values are $5.5$ and $8.1$, respectively. Hence, the combination benefits from the complementary regions of phase space covered by each. This result was obtained using just over one-third of the luminosity analyzed in this work. When rescaled to $172$ fb$^{-1}$ for the years 2022 through 2024, the $4b$ channel would reach an expected limit of around $2.6$. It is the final state that extracts the most sensitivity per unit of luminosity, confirming that its disadvantage it faces from the multi-jet background is offset by the highest branching fraction of the three and by the improvements in trigger selection and jet identification that Run 3 brings.

The same analysis also provides an exclusive measurement of VBF production, for which it rules out rates greater than $60$ times the SM prediction, with an expected value of $81$. When rescaled to a luminosity of $172$ fb$^{-1}$, that expected value would be around $49$, compared to the $85$ obtained here. This represents a considerable advantage and stems from the merged topology, which is the one that accesses the high-$m_{HH}$ regime where this production is concentrated.\medskip

The $bb\gamma\gamma$ final state has also been analyzed using $61.9$ fb$^{-1}$ of data from the same period via a two-dimensional fit to the masses of the photon pair and the jet pair, which excludes rates greater than $11.0$ times the SM prediction compared to an expected value of $7.3$ \cite{CMS-PAS-HIG-25-007}\footnote{The search also presents an alternative strategy that does not fit the mass of the jet pair, yielding an observed limit of $7.4$ with an expected value of $8.7$.}. Scaled in the same way, its expected limit would be around $4.4$, so that its branching fraction — two orders of magnitude smaller — is largely compensated for by the resolution with which the photon pair is reconstructed, which concentrates the signal into a narrow peak against a smooth background.

Due to the small number of events expected from this production mode, whose cross section is $1.874$ fb and with a branching fraction of $0.3\,\%$, the $61.9$ fb$^{-1}$ analyzed contain fewer than one event produced by vector boson fusion even before applying the acceptance criteria, so that this search does not provide any exclusive qqHH measurement.\medskip

These channels are found to be within a factor of two of each other after accounting for luminosity, which confirms them as the three dominant channels, with none predominating over the others. \subsection{Comparison with the ATLAS search in \texorpdfstring{$bb\tau\tau$}{bbtautau}}
\label{subsec:bbtt_comp_atlas}
%
After thoroughly reviewing the capabilities of the CMS program, an external reference is provided by the search recently published by the ATLAS experiment in this same final state \cite{ATLAS-HH-bbtautau-Run3}. By looking at the same channel, the strategy followed here is contrasted with what can be achieved using a dataset of a comparable size. ATLAS analyzes $140$ fb$^{-1}$ from Run 2 and $56$ fb$^{-1}$ from Run 3, which does not include the 2024 period\footnote{Small differences in luminosity reflect the fraction of the luminosity delivered by the LHC that each experiment records}. Furthermore, in this comparison, it should be noted that ATLAS completely reanalyzes the Run 2 data using its latest strategy, whereas here the Run 2 data rely on the strategy emplyed in the original publication, as outlined in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comb_procedure}.\medskip

Both searches, ATLAS and CMS, share common analysis strategies: channels are distinguished based on the decay mode of the $\tau$ lepton pair; the two Higgs boson candidates are reconstructed from $b$-tagged jets and from the visible products of the $\tau$ leptons; and the signal is extracted from a simultaneous fit to the output distribution of a classifier.

However, there are several differences between the two analyses, both in the method used to identify signal regions — which ATLAS obtains in the same step as the discriminant, from a single network built on the transformer architecture introduced in Section \ref{subsec:PNet} — and in the granularity of the resulting fit, which comprises twelve regions compared to the fifty used here. Furthermore, the criterion used to construct the regions is based on the separation of the invariant mass spectrum of the $HH$ system into a low-$m_{HH}$ and a high-$m_{HH}$ region, and in the low-mass region, the observable being fitted is constructed using an anomalous configuration with $\kappa_\lambda = 5$, whose softer kinematics populate that region. The partition of phase space and the discriminant are thus organized around the variable on which the sensitivity to couplings depends, whereas here the categories are defined by topology and the quality of the $b$ tagging, and it is the network that must internally exploit that dependence.

In any case, these are not the only differences. ATLAS incorporates into the fit four control regions dedicated to its dominant backgrounds, reconstructs the mass of the $\tau\tau$ system using a likelihood method rather than a regression network, applies dedicated corrections to the tagged jets that improve their energy resolution, determines the signal strengths of the two production modes in a single fit with two parameters of interest, and validates the entire strategy by measuring $ZZ$ and $ZH$ production in this same final state\footnote{CMS has carried out the equivalent measurement with the Run 2 dataset \cite{CMS:2026zzzh}, where $ZZ$ and $ZH$ production is studied in this same final state with the explicit purpose of validating the techniques on which the search for Higgs boson pair production relies. Its extension to the Run 3 dataset is foreseen as part of the full analysis.}. The uncertainty breakdown presented below allows for the quantification of the factors that contribute most to the difference in sensitivity.\medskip

The two comparisons discussed in this section place the search presented here within the broader context of Higgs boson pair searches, the complete form of which is the combination of all final states in both experiments. The benchmark for this remains the combination performed by ATLAS and CMS using the complete Run 2 data \cite{ATLASCMS:2026HH}, which brings together both the final-state and experimental axes, excluding production rates greater than $2.5$ times the SM prediction, with an expected value of $1.7$, and restricts the trilinear coupling to the interval $[-0.7,\ 6.1]$ and the coupling between two Higgs bosons and two vector bosons to $[0.7,\ 1.3]$. The individual searches described in this section currently reach expected limits between $2.4$ and $3.0$ times the SM prediction, meaning that a single final state in a single experiment is already within a factor of $1.4$ of what was previously achieved by the combination of channels explored by the two collaborations. While part of this enhancement stems from the increased luminosity, such progress can only be attributed to the continuously evolving analysis strategies.

The subsequent step involves incorporating all available statistics into the comprehensive picture, and the analysis presented in this chapter constitutes one of the pieces with which that will be done. It represents the most sensitive result the experiment has obtained in this final state to date, while also leaving room for refinement through comparison with ATLAS — specifically, the reprocessing of Run 2 data using a revised strategy and the optimization of that strategy itself, which in this first iteration was carried over from the one used in Run 2 and is therefore open to reformulation to prioritize the recovery of signal acceptance and the reduction of experimental uncertainties. Section \ref{sec:bbtt_prospects} explores these avenues, the interpretations that these same data allow for and that this iteration has not explored, and what can be expected at the conclusion of the full LHC program.



\section{Beyond the current scope}
% Future perspectives, looking ahead, future directions, the next frontier
\label{sec:bbtt_prospects}
%
This chapter has described the scope of an initial iteration built upon the strategy inherited from Run 2 and applied to slightly more than half of the dataset that the period will ultimately provide. What falls outside that scope extends in three directions that are worth keeping apart. One addresses potential directions for improvement, guided by an analysis of the current strategy with a view to its application to the complete dataset. Another examines the insights that the same events can provide in BSM interpretations, which connects to the material presented in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}. The last shifts the focus to the scale of the future program, where the anticipated increase in luminosity changes the order of magnitude of the problem.


\subsection{Developments for the next iteration}
\label{subsec:bbtt_prospects_analysis}
% Medir mejor lo mismo
The dataset on which the future result will be based is largely available, so that what separate this analysis from that result is not waiting for more collisions, but revising the strategy with which they are analyzed. Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comp_atlas} already provided an estimate of how much an improved strategy could yield on the same set of events and highlighted some of the inefficiencies in the current strategy, which are subject to review.\medskip

The first factor to be reviewed is the acceptance, which contributes linearly to the sensitivity that the analysis can achieve, whereas the luminosity contributes only as a square root, so that recovering $20\,\%$ of the acceptance is equivalent to analyzing $44\,\%$ more data. The decisions quantified by ATLAS point to where to look for it, ranging from using a more efficient $b$ tagging working point to revising the vetoes applied to $\tau$-lepton candidates. However, these decisions require dedicated studies to determine whether a more permissive selection enables the discriminant to recover the lost purity, or whether, conversely, the background it brings hinders this recovery. In any case, these are the kinds of decisions that a first iteration inherits and that a second iteration is in a position to reexamine.

The partitioning of phase space is itself one of the key steps in the analysis chain, and one that ATLAS approaches in a very different way. Its partition is considerably coarser than the one used here, and its criterion is organized around the invariant mass of the $HH$ system, which is the variable on which the sensitivity to couplings depends, and reserves one of its regions for a discriminant constructed on an anomalous configuration with $\kappa_\lambda = 5$. In contrast, the categories defined in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_flow} correspond to the reconstruction topology and the quality of jet tagging, such that the dependence on the mass $m_{HH}$ is entirely handled by the discriminant. Revising that criterion requires no additional data, and it also paves the way for the BSM interpretations that this iteration has not pursued, which are the subject of Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_prospects_interpretation}.


%In addition, the most collimated corner of phase space, which the dedicated category of this analysis only partially covers, is being addressed by a specific search; thus, both approaches must be made orthogonal before they can be combined.

Another direction concerns the chain of neural networks used in the analysis, which encompasses the assignment of the jets forming the $H\to b\bar{b}$ candidate and that of the characteristic VBF jet pair, both of which are discussed in Chapter \ref{ch:taggers}, the regression of the mass of the $\tau\tau$ system in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_flow}, and the two signal extraction discriminants in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction}. None of these methods employ attention mechanisms, which in recent years have become the standard for processing sets of reconstructed objects, as previously introduced in Section \ref{subsec:PNet}. The scope for revision therefore extends to the whole chain, and the strategy on which ATLAS bases its current analysis confirms this: their paper identifies their new classifier as the single greatest gain of the iteration, with an improvement of between $15$ and $20\,\%$ depending on the region.

Finally, added to these is the reduction of the uncertainties that Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comp_atlas} identifies as dominant within the experimental block: the fake factors and the calibration of $b$ tagging, whose impact here is considerably greater than in the analysis of the neighboring experiment and whose investigation requires collaboration with the groups that determine these calibrations. In addition, the expansion of the simulated samples has a twofold effect, since it not only narrows the prediction uncertainty in the most sensitive bins, where the background is low, but it also relaxes the condition that forces the binning algorithm described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_extraction} to reduce the number of bins when the simulated statistics are insufficient, which would allow for finer binning precisely in the tail where the signal is concentrated.\medskip

These are just a few of the possible approaches aimed at raising awareness of the results presented in this chapter, which, in any case, address the question of how much of a signal consistent with the SM prediction is contained in the data. However, these same events give rise to different questions, which are addressed in the following section.

These are only some of the possible directions, and their benefit does not depend on the hypothesis being tested, since a more efficient selection, a better discriminant or a more precise background prediction improve any search in this final state. Nevertheless, all the results of this chapter answer how much signal compatible with the SM prediction the data contain. The same events, however, admit different questions, and these are the subject of what follows.


\subsection{Broadening the interpretation}
% broadening the interpretation / EFT interpretation
\label{subsec:bbtt_prospects_interpretation}
% preguntar más a los mismos datos
The parametrization described in Section \ref{sec:bbtt_samples} makes it possible to predict the signal at any point in the coupling space without having to repeat the analysis, and this is what has made it possible to report the intervals in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comb_results}. However, the coupling modifiers rescale the structures already contained in the SM and hence cannot account for interactions not described therein. Therefore, effective descriptions from Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling} are used, wich allow for contact terms with no counterpart in the SM parametrization — such as the one coupling two top quarks to two Higgs bosons — and with them a considerably broader parameter space in which to interpret the same measurement. Accordingly, the EFT interpretation can provide limits on the production rate evaluated at sets of reference points, chosen such that each represents a region of the parameter space with characteristic kinematics, thereby allowing the space to be covered without traversing it point by point; confidence intervals and two-dimensional contours on the coefficients of these contact terms and on their correlation with already known couplings; or the translation of both into the parameters of specific models, which the effective description allows to be put in correspondence with its own parameters. The combination of CMS with Run 2 data covers all three \cite{CMS_combination}, and the analysis of the final state $4b$ with Run 3 data already includes the first two \cite{CMS:2026znu}.

It is therefore natural to extend the interpretation of the measurement presented in this chapter in these same terms, which requires constructing the prediction of the signal entering the fit for each point in the parameter space. Furthermore, since the contact terms not only scale the production rate but also alter the shape that the signal takes on in the discriminant distribution, this prediction must be differential. Constructing it requires an effective description that establishes which terms contribute to the production amplitude, and making the result meaningful requires in addition knowing within what range of energies the use of that description remains legitimate — issues addressed in Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling}.. In this sense, the two parts of this work are complementary, since this chapter provides the measurement and that chapter provides the modeling, without which it is impossible to ask what these data reveal about interactions not included in the standard model.



However, despite that capacity to interpret, the sensitivity of this measurement is still determined by the decisions made during the design of the analysis since the parametrization only comes into play at the end of the chain, where it suffices to reweight templates that have already been built to obtain a prediction at any point. The selection, categories, and discriminant are then the set of decisions that determine which events populate those templates and within which bin of the distribution each one falls, and all of these were defined based on the standard model configuration. Therefore, even if the analysis correctly predicts the signal at an anomalous point, any interpretation formulated in that space is based on a measurement whose acceptance and response were never adjusted to the kinematics produced by those parameters.\medskip

Optimizing the analysis in these terms would involve extending the dependence on parameters to the design by separating regions based on the kinematics produced by each configuration and training discriminants on anomalous configurations in addition to that of the SM, as the ATLAS analysis does by reserving one of its regions for a discriminant built on a configuration with $\kappa_\lambda = 5$. However, this is a design decision whose worth depends on the hypothesis for which one wishes to maximize sensitivity and on the balance sought among the various scenarios considered. With the currently available dataset, the SM hypothesis remains the natural target, but as the dataset grows, specializing part of the analysis in alternative configurations will become increasingly worthwhile. The scope opened up by this growth constitutes precisely the last of the directions announced at the beginning of this section.

\subsection{The reach of the complete program}
\label{subsec:bbtt_prospects_hllhc}
% el programa completo
The horizon of this search is defined by the LHC high-luminosity phase, for which $3$ ab$^{-1}$ per experiment are projected at a center-of-mass energy of $14$ TeV. The projections presented by ATLAS and CMS for the update of the European strategy \cite{ATLASCMS:2025HLLHC} extrapolate the analyses published with Run 2 data to that scenario under two assumptions regarding systematic uncertainties: one in which theoretical uncertainties are halved, the statistical contribution from simulated samples becomes negligible and experimental uncertainties are reduced object by object according to the origin of each; and another that adds to the above a $5\,\%$ improvement in the $b$ tagging efficiencies and in the reconstruction efficiencies for hadronic $\tau$ leptons. The document itself characterizes both as conservative targets, based on previous projections indicating that experimental and theoretical advances consistently exceed what can be anticipated from luminosity growth alone.

Under these assumptions, the combination of the two experiments achieves an expected significance of $7.6$ standard deviations in the most favorable scenario, and exceeds five already at $2$ ab$^{-1}$, so that the observation of this process falls within the reach of the program. The trilinear coupling\footnote{The projections denote this modifier as $\kappa_3$, equivalent to the $\kappa_\lambda$ used in this chapter.} is determined with an uncertainty of between $26$ and $29\,\%$ depending on the sign of the deviation, which corresponds to the interval $0.74 < \kappa_3 < 1.29$ at the $68\,\%$ confidence level. Table \ref{tab:hllhc_projections} summarizes these numbers, and Figure \ref{fig:hllhc_projections} shows, in its left panel, the contribution of each final state to that determination, where $bb\tau\tau$ is the most sensitive of all, combining the highest sensitivity to the SM with a particular advantage in the low-$m_{HH}$ region, where the dependence on $\kappa_\lambda$ is concentrated, as discussed in Section \ref{subsec:bbtt_comp_cms}. The coupling between two Higgs bosons and two vector bosons, on the other hand, would be determined with an precision of about $13\,\%$ even in the more conservative of the two scenarios.

The right panel of the figure examines how accurately the trilinear coupling would be determined if its true value were not the SM one, and shows the effect of the destructive interference between the two contributions to ggHH production which makes the production rate minimal around a true value of $2.5$. Nevertheless, the combination of the two experiments would rule out the SM value at the $95\,\%$ confidence level for true values above $1.7$ or below $0.5$, and the gain in precision is sufficient for the likelihood profile to exhibit a single minimum regardless of the true value, so that the degeneracy currently visible in Figure \ref{fig:likelihood_scans_comb} would be resolved.

It is also interesting to compare these projections with those presented for the previous strategy update, a comparison that makes clear that the improvement between them does not stem from a change in the predicted luminosity but rather from the analysis techniques developed in the interim, and in particular from the architectures used for jet tagging. The document anticipates that this trend will continue for the remainder of Run 3 and throughout the high-luminosity phase, and that the uncertainty on the trilinear coupling will consequently be well below $30\,\%$, even without accounting for the improvements associated with the detector upgrade.

This expectation is not unrelated to the work presented in this chapter. The projections are based on published analyses using Run 2 data, and for $bb\tau\tau$ they assume the same sensitivity in both experiments, on the grounds that the trigger strategy deployed by CMS in Run 3 achieves performance comparable to that of the ATLAS experiment. That strategy is described in Section \ref{sec:triggers}, and the result obtained here is the first measurement to employ it, thereby testing against data one of the assumptions on which the projection rests.\bigskip

This chapter followed on from the previous one, addressing the question of how much signal the data can accommodate once the background is adjusted within its uncertainties. With all the available data combined, the analyzed $310$ fb$^{-1}$ allow for up to four times the rate predicted by the standard model, with a signal strength compatible with both the absence of production and the SM prediction, so that the predicted rate still lies below what this search is able to resolve. Interpreted in terms of the couplings governing the process, this same measurement constrains the Higgs boson self-coupling to the interval $-2.5 < \kappa_\lambda < 9.4$ and the coupling between two Higgs bosons and two vector bosons to $0.02 < \kappa_{2V} < 2.1$, and this is the first time that an interval obtained in this final state excludes zero. Overall, this is the most sensitive result the experiment has obtained in this channel to date.

That result, however, only becomes meaningful when viewed within the broader context: along with the other final states from CMS, which, once luminosity is accounted for, are distributed within a factor of two, so that none of them alone bears the burden of the search; and alongside the same search conducted by the ATLAS experiment, which highlights the approaches each experiment has taken and shows where there is room for improvement looking ahead to the analysis of the complete set of data from Run 2 and Run 3.

These directions all point to a revision of the strategy. Recovering the acceptance that the current selection leaves out, incorporating recent developments in learning architectures into the whole chain of networks, rethinking the criterion by which the phase space is partitioned so that each region contributes where it is most sensitive, and reducing the experimental uncertainties that weigh more heavily here than in the neighboring experiment are just some of the possibilities to explore. When applied to a dataset that will continue to grow, these revisions place the observation of this process within the reach of the program and bring the uncertainty on the trilinear coupling below $30\,\%$, numbers that the projection exercise itself characterizes as conservative targets.


The analysis described in this chapter is an iteration of that process — the first one based on data from Run 3 — and its value lies not only in the measurements it provides but also in the chain of methods it establishes and verifies using data for the iterations that will follow. The interpretation of this same measurement within the framework of effective descriptions is also part of this continuity, and relies on the differential prediction of the signal at any point in a broader parameter space, as Chapter \ref{ch:eft_modeling} addresses.
