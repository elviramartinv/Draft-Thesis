\chapter{The \texorpdfstring{$\tau_h$}{τh} identification algorithms and their performance}
\label{ch:Tau}
%
The precise and accurate reconstruction of $\tau$ leptons is a crucial part of the $HH\rightarrow bb\tau\tau$ analysis covered in Chapter \ref{ch:bbtt_analysis}. Furthermore, a significant number of physics analyses — such as measurements of Higgs boson production~\cite{CMS-HIG-16-043,CMS-HIG-19-010} and of the \CP structure of its Yukawa coupling to $\tau$ leptons~\cite{CMS-HIG-20-006}, and searches for new physics in final states with $\tau$ leptons~\cite{CMS-HIG-21-001,CMS-PAS-EXO-24-012,CMS-PAS-EXO-24-020} — rely on the adequate identification of $\tau$ decay products and their properties. This broad physics program necessitates continuous development of reconstruction and identification techniques, which in CMS for $\tau$ leptons are coordinated within the Tau Physics Object Group (POG). A central component of the study of $\tau$ leptons, which will be emphasized subsequently, involves validating the applied techniques to ensure their correct behavior in data.

In CMS, the reconstruction and identification of hadronic $\tau$ leptons constitute two complementary steps. The reconstruction stage, performed by the HPS algorithm~\cite{CMS-TAU-14-001} (Section \ref{subsec:Taus}), assembles the visible decay products into a $\tau_h$ candidate and determines its decay mode and kinematic properties. The identification step then classifies these reconstructed candidates as a genuine $\tau$ lepton or as a misidentified jet, electron or muon.

This chapter provides a detailed examination of the identification of hadronic $\tau$ leptons. Section \ref{sec:Tau_MisID} lays the groundwork and emphasizes the challenges associated with distinguishing genuine $\tau_h$ candidates from background objects, Section \ref{sec:DeepTau2.5} summarizes the identification algorithms and compares their performance, and Section \ref{sec:Tau_validation} presents their validation with the data recorded by CMS from 2022 to 2026. This validation is the contribution of this thesis to the Tau POG, and its results with the 2024 and 2025 data have been published in Ref.~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-074}.


\section{Challenges in \texorpdfstring{$\tau_h$}{τh} identification}
\label{sec:Tau_MisID}
%
As described in Section \ref{subsec:Taus}, the HPS algorithm is configured to prioritize the reconstruction efficiency of genuine $\tau_h$, which exceeds $80\%$ for $p_T > 30$ GeV and approaches $90\%$ above $100$ GeV~\cite{CMS-TAU-20-001}. Therefore, it inevitably suffers from contamination by other processes, meaning that a fraction of reconstructed $\tau_h$ candidates originates from objects that mimic the topology of hadronic $\tau$ decay. The dominant source of misidentification arises from jets, since the HPS algorithm is seeded by PF jets that can reproduce the charged-track multiplicity and electromagnetic signature characteristic of $\tau$ decays, i.e., a highly collimated quark or gluon jet can resemble a $\tau$ decay, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:tau_fakes}. Light leptons can also be misidentified as $\tau$ leptons. A muon can mimic a one-prong decay when it deposits a significant fraction of its energy in the calorimeters, while an electron can be taken as the charged hadron of the decay, with the photons it radiates through bremsstrahlung clustered into a strip, which reproduces the signature of a h$^{\pm}\pi^0$ decay. These signatures are also illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:tau_fakes}.

The reconstruction of genuine $\tau_h$ has its own limitations. Depending on the decay mode, between $11\%$ (h$^{\pm}$) and $25\%$ (h$^{\pm}\pi^0$) of the generated $\tau_h$ are not reconstructed in any of the HPS decay modes, mainly because the tracks of charged hadrons are reconstructed with an efficiency of about $90\%$~\cite{CMS-TAU-20-001}. Part of the three-prong decays in which one track is lost are recovered as two-prong candidates, but their charge is correctly assigned in only about $70\%$ of the cases, compared with $99\%$ for the other decay modes~\cite{CMS-TAU-20-001}, which is why they are not among the four decay modes used in analyses (Figure \ref{fig:tau_DMs}). These candidates, lost or misreconstructed by HPS, are one of the motivations for the identification algorithms that do not depend on it, presented in Section \ref{subsec:PNet}.

These effects reflect the challenges associated with the complex topology of hadronic $\tau$ decays, and highlight the need for a dedicated identification step to improve the purity of reconstructed $\tau_h$ candidates. To this end, various methods have been implemented in CMS over the years, ranging from cut-based isolation sum discriminators and Boosted Decision Tree discriminator~\cite{CMS-TAU-14-001,CMS-TAU-16-003} to more sophisticated deep learning-based algorithms, described in the following section.


\section{The \texorpdfstring{$\tau_h$}{τh} identification algorithms}
\label{sec:DeepTau2.5}
%
Given the aforementioned reconstruction limitations, a dedicated identification approach is needed. The challenge of discriminating between the reconstructed $\tau_h$ candidates, given the large number of correlated observables involved in this type of task, has led to the adoption of advanced deep learning techniques capable of fully exploiting the available information and ensure robust performance across different data-taking conditions. 

The \textsc{DeepTau} neural network-based algorithm was deployed at the end of Run 2, and has since become the standard $\tau_h$ identification method in CMS. The first version v2.1~\cite{CMS-TAU-20-001} was presented initially, and was then upgraded to v2.5~\cite{CMS-TAU-24-001} during the early Run 3 period, introducing major improvements that will be briefly discussed next. In parallel, alternative identification strategies for genuine $\tau_h$ candidates are being explored, leveraging modern architectures such as graph neural networks (\textsc{PNet}) and transformers (\textsc{UParT}) to treat $\tau_h$ candidates within a broader jet classification framework.



\subsection{The \textsc{DeepTau} algorithm}
\label{subsec:DeepTau}
%
\textsc{DeepTau} is a convolutional neural network that classifies each $\tau_h$ candidate reconstructed by HPS as a genuine $\tau_h$, a quark or gluon jet, an electron or a muon, so that the three discriminations described in Section \ref{sec:Tau_MisID} are performed by a single network~\cite{CMS-TAU-20-001,CMS-TAU-24-001}. To do so, it combines two types of information. The first is a set of high-level variables that describe the candidate as a whole, such as its kinematics, its isolation, or the compatibility of its tracks with the primary vertex. The second is the set of all particles reconstructed around the candidate, whose individual properties are arranged in two grids in the $\eta$-$\phi$ plane centered on the $\tau_h$ axis, an inner grid covering the region of the decay products and a wider outer grid that captures the surrounding activity, which is larger for jets than for genuine $\tau_h$. The input variables of both types are summarized in Table \ref{tab:deeptau_inputs}.

This set of input information is processed by a combination of dense and convolutional layers, and the final layer of the network acts as a multiclass classifier~\cite{CMS-TAU-20-001}, providing the probabilities that the reconstructed $\tau_h$ candidate corresponds to a genuine $\tau_h$, an electron, a muon, or a quark or gluon jet. From these probabilities, three discriminators are built, one against each type of background (\textsc{VSjet}, \textsc{VSe}, \textsc{VSmu}), by comparing the probability of the genuine $\tau_h$ class with that of the corresponding background. Each discriminator has its own set of working points, defined by the efficiency for genuine $\tau_h$, as described in Section \ref{subsec:Taus}.

The second novelty of v2.5 is the use of domain adaptation~\cite{CMS-TAU-24-001}. Since v2.1 was trained only on simulation, any mismodeling of its inputs translated into differences between data and simulation in its output. To reduce them, v2.5 is trained on a mixture of simulated events and collision data, so that the network learns to classify $\tau_h$ candidates while avoiding the features that are described differently in data and in simulation. The classification itself is still learned only from simulation, while the collision data come from a sample of $Z\to\tau\tau\to\mu\tau_h$ events rich in genuine $\tau_h$ — the same final state used for the validation in Section \ref{sec:Tau_validation}. As a result, the agreement between data and simulation improves in the highest-score bins, and the corrections that analyses must apply move closer to unity.

\subsection{Alternative strategies for \texorpdfstring{$\tau_h$}{τh} identification}
\label{subsec:PNet}
%
In recent years, a different approach to $\tau_h$ identification has emerged in CMS as part of a \textit{unified jet tagging} strategy, in which the $\tau_h$ is treated as one more type of jet, and a single network classifies each jet according to its origin, whether a $b$ quark, a $c$ quark, a light quark, a gluon, an electron, a muon or a hadronically decaying $\tau$ lepton. This approach consistently models the correlations between the identification of the different objects, allows the selection to be optimized against specific sources of contamination, and concentrates computing resources in a single algorithm, used both in the offline reconstruction and in the HLT. Two algorithms follow this strategy, \textsc{ParticleNet} (\textsc{PNet})~\cite{PNet} and the Unified Particle Transformer (\textsc{UParT})~\cite{Qu:2022mxj,CMS-UParT}, both already introduced in Section \ref{subsec:Jets} for the identification of $b$ jets. 


\subsubsection{The \textsc{ParticleNet} algorithm}
%
\textsc{PNet} represents the jet as a \textit{particle cloud}, an unordered set of its constituent PF candidates, in contrast with the image in the $\eta$-$\phi$ plane used by \textsc{DeepTau} and with the ordered lists of particles used by \textsc{DeepJet} (Section \ref{subsec:Jets}). This is a natural representation of a jet, whose constituents have no intrinsic order and are distributed irregularly in space. The particle cloud is processed by a graph neural network, a family of architectures suited to sets of elements without a fixed order or geometric structure, in which the features of each particle are updated using those of its nearest neighbors, so that the network learns the relations between the constituents. Unlike \textsc{DeepTau}, whose inputs rely on the intermediate reconstruction performed by HPS, \textsc{PNet} uses only the properties of the individual constituents of the jet, without any previous $\tau_h$-specific reconstruction.

At the time of its introduction, \textsc{PNet} achieved state-of-the-art performance in jet tagging, clearly surpassing the existing methods~\cite{PNet}, and it has since been widely adopted in CMS. Its main application in Run 3, however, has been the identification of $b$ and $c$ jets, for which it was the recommended algorithm in 2022 and 2023 (Section \ref{subsec:Jets}). For $\tau_h$, its use is so far limited to the HLT, where it replaced the online version of \textsc{DeepTau} in 2024 (Section \ref{sec:triggers}), while offline analyses, including the one presented in this thesis, still rely on \textsc{DeepTau}.




\subsubsection{The Unified Particle Transformer algorithm}
%
\textsc{UParT} also represents the jet as a particle cloud, but processes it with a transformer architecture, whose self-attention mechanism weighs the relation between every pair of constituents at once, instead of only between neighbors. Additionally, the \textsc{UParT} algorithm introduces variables that characterize the interaction between jet constituents and secondary vertices, known as pairwise interaction features, enhancing the sensitivity to internal structure of the jet

The first version of \textsc{UParT} was released in 2024~\cite{CMS-UParT}, and an updated version, v2, trained on a dataset twice as large and with a larger network, in 2025~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-081}. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:btag_evolution}, \textsc{UParT} improves on the performance of \textsc{PNet} in the identification of $b$ jets, and it has replaced it as the recommended algorithm for this task since 2024.\medskip



\noindent In both algorithms, the $\tau_h$ is treated as a one more type of jet, and the network returns the probability that the jet belongs to each of the classes considered in the training, which correspond to jets originating from $b$ quarks, $c$ quarks, light quarks and gluons, and to electrons, muons and $\tau_h$ reconstructed as jets, together with a regression of the jet energy~\cite{CMS-UParT}. Therefore, algthough both algorithms were originally designed and customized for AK4 jet classification, they both perform in an inclusive manner heavy flavor and hadronic $\tau$ identification combined with a flavor-aware jet energy and jet resolution regression~\cite{CMS-UParT}. The $\tau_h$ classes are further split according to the charge and the decay mode of the $\tau$ lepton, so that the decay mode of a candidate is not reconstructed from its constituents, as in HPS, but corresponds to the $\tau_h$ class with the highest probability — labeled with the convention of Equation \ref{eq:tau_DM} —, and its charge is obtained in the same way. Since these classes are defined from the generated decay, the networks can separate the h$^{\pm}\pi^0$ and h$^{\pm}\pi^0\pi^0$ modes, which HPS merges into a single decay mode (Section \ref{subsec:Taus}). The class probabilities are then combined into three discriminants against jets, electrons and muons, analogous to those of \textsc{DeepTau} and each with its own set of working points, while the energy regression provides the visible $p_T$ of the $\tau_h$. The whole description of the candidate thus comes from the network itself, without relying on any previous $\tau_h$-specific reconstruction.

Whether these techniques offer a performance competitive with or complementary to that of \textsc{DeepTau} cannot be inferred from their architectures alone, since each algorithm operates on a different reconstructed object, and is assessed in Section \ref{subsec:tau_IDcomparison}. The main features of the three algorithms are summarized in Table \ref{tab:algo_comparison}.

\subsection{Comparison between \texorpdfstring{$\tau_h$}{τh} identification algorithms}
\label{subsec:tau_IDcomparison}
%DP2025-073
To understand the complementarity of the described algorithms for identifying hadronic $\tau$ leptons based on advanced ML techniques, the subsequent section presents a comparison of the performance of each of them: \textsc{DeepTau} v2.5, \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT} v2~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-073}. The comparison uses simulated Drell--Yan events with a dilepton mass between $50$ and $120$ GeV, generated at NLO with \POWHEG{} interfaced with \PYTHIA{}, which contain genuine $\tau_h$ from $Z\to\tau\tau$ decays as well as electrons, muons and quark or gluon jets that can be misidentified as $\tau_h$, each reconstructed candidate being classified by matching it to the generated particles. The study covers theefficiency for genuine $\tau_h$, the rejection of each type of misidentified object and the assignment of the decay mode.

However, since \textsc{DeepTau} operates on HPS candidates, while \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT} operate on AK4 jets clustered after removing the pileup contribution with CHS or PUPPI (Section \ref{subsec:Jets}), their efficiencies are not directly comparable. Therefore, a unified collection of candidates is built, starting from the HPS candidates and adding the CHS and PUPPI jets that do not overlap with the candidates already included, to which a very loose selection is applied: $p_T > 20$ GeV, $|\eta| < 2.5$ and $|d_z| < 0.2$ cm. Accordingly, the efficiency of each algorithm is defined with respect to the genuine $\tau_h$ in this collection, so that it accounts for both the reconstruction on which the algorithm relies and the discriminant itself. Since analyses require a reliable charge assignment, the HPS candidates reconstructed in the two-prong decay modes, whose charge is often misassigned (Section \ref{sec:Tau_MisID}), are excluded, and the candidates recovered only as jets are required to have a charge assigned by the network with sufficient confidence.


The resulting ROC curves are shown in Figure \ref{fig:ROC_tauID}, where HPS+\textsc{DeepTau} is compared with \textsc{PNet} applied to CHS and PUPPI jets and with \textsc{UParT} applied to PUPPI jets. The clearest difference between the algorithms is the range of efficiency they can reach. Since \textsc{DeepTau} can only select the $\tau_h$ previously reconstructed by HPS, its curves end at an efficiency of about $70\%$, the efficiency of the HPS reconstruction itself,\footnote{This value is lower than the reconstruction efficiency given in Section \ref{sec:Tau_MisID} because it is defined with respect to a different reference, which also includes the $\tau_h$ recovered only as jets, and because of the charge requirements described above, without which it rises to about $75\%$~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-073}.} while those of the jet-based taggers extend up to about $90\%$, as they also identify $\tau_h$ that HPS does not reconstruct. Part of this loss is deliberate, since the requirements of HPS on the charge and invariant mass of each decay hypothesis (Section \ref{subsec:Taus}) already reject about $80\%$ of the quark and gluon jets, at the cost of also losing part of the genuine $\tau_h$~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-073}.

Beyond this difference in range, the curves can be compared at a fixed efficiency within the range accessible to all algorithms, where none of them dominates and the ranking depends on the type of misidentified object. Against quark and gluon jets, CHS+\textsc{PNet} achieves the lowest misidentification rate, between about $20$ and $45\%$ below that of HPS+\textsc{DeepTau}, the difference growing with the efficiency, while HPS+\textsc{DeepTau} remains the second best option in the high-purity regime, configuration used by most analyses. The same \textsc{PNet} network performs worse on PUPPI jets than on CHS jets, and PUPPI+\textsc{UParT} yields the highest misidentification rate, up to $50\%$ above that of HPS+\textsc{DeepTau}, which shows that the object on which each algorithm operates matters as much as its architecture. Against electrons and muons the picture changes, as PUPPI+\textsc{UParT} provides the best rejection, with misidentification rates up to several times lower than those of HPS+\textsc{DeepTau}, which in turn outperforms \textsc{PNet} at the lowest efficiencies.

These differences become smaller at the working points used in practice. In this study, the working points of the three algorithms are defined in the same way, as the thresholds that keep a fixed fraction of the genuine $\tau_h$ reconstructed by each of them, which for the Medium WP of \textsc{VSjet} is $70\%$ — the same target used in the definition of the \textsc{DeepTau} working points. The fraction of misidentified objects finally selected by each algorithm results from two factors, the fraction that its reconstruction turns into $\tau_h$ candidates and the fraction of these candidates that pass the discriminant. The second factor is similar for the three algorithms at the working points used in the analysis presented in this thesis (Section \ref{sec:bbtt_objects}), with between $2$ and $3\%$ of the reconstructed jets passing the Medium working point of \textsc{VSjet}, and similar rates among the three algorithms for electrons and muons at the VVLoose working point of \textsc{VSe} and the Tight of \textsc{VSmu}~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-073}. The first factor, in contrast, is very different for jets. HPS already rejects most of them through its requirements on the charge and invariant mass of each decay hypothesis, and reconstructs as candidates only about $15\%$ of the misidentified jets in the unified collection, compared with between $32$ and $48\%$ for the AK4 jets on which \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT} operate. As a result, at the same working point the jet-based taggers select more genuine $\tau_h$, but also about two to three times more misidentified jets.

This is shown in Figure \ref{fig:venn_combined}, which compares the candidates selected by each algorithm at the Medium WP of \textsc{VSjet}. The number in each region gives the fraction of all the candidates selected by at least one of the algorithms that falls in it, and the one below the name of each algorithm the total fraction it selects. For genuine $\tau_h$, about $60\%$ of the candidates are selected by all three algorithms, while about $30\%$ are selected only by \textsc{PNet}, \textsc{UParT} or both, and are therefore lost by HPS+\textsc{DeepTau}. For quark and gluon jets the picture is reversed, as HPS+\textsc{DeepTau} selects only about $30\%$ of all the misidentified jets, compared with $56\%$ for \textsc{PNet} and $72\%$ for \textsc{UParT}. HPS+\textsc{DeepTau} thus provides a purer selection, while the jet-based taggers recover genuine $\tau_h$ that would otherwise be lost, which makes the algorithms complementary rather than interchangeable.

The jet-based taggers also differ from HPS in the assignment of the decay mode, which has been assessed by comparing the decay mode assigned to each reconstructed candidate with that of the corresponding generated $\tau_h$~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-073}. Since \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT} obtain the decay mode from their output classes (Section \ref{subsec:PNet}), they can separate the h$^{\pm}\pi^0$ and h$^{\pm}\pi^0\pi^0$ modes merged by HPS, although only with limited precision. For the other modes, they assign the correct decay mode more often than HPS, in particular for three-prong decays in which one of the charged hadrons is not reconstructed, which HPS labels as two-prong candidates and the jet-based taggers assign to the correct three-prong mode. They also lose fewer $\tau_h$, as a smaller fraction of the generated decays is left without a reconstructed candidate. For the analysis presented in this thesis, which does not separate the events by decay mode, this improvement is of secondary importance, but it is relevant for measurements that rely on the individual decay products, such as that of the CP structure of the coupling between the Higgs boson and the $\tau$ lepton~\cite{CMS-HIG-20-006}.\medskip



% The relevance of this improvement depends on the analysis. In most analyses, including the one presented in this thesis, the accepted decay modes are combined, and the decay mode enters only indirectly, through the corrections to the simulation, which are derived separately for each mode (Section \ref{subsec:Taus}), and, in this thesis, as one of the inputs to the regression of the mass of the $\tau\tau$ system (Section \ref{sec:bbtt_flow}). In these cases, what matters is that the decay mode is assigned in the same way in data and in simulation, rather than how precisely it is assigned. A good separation becomes essential, instead, in measurements that exploit the individual decay products, such as that of the CP structure of the coupling between the Higgs boson and the $\tau$ lepton, where the CP-sensitive observable is built from the directions of the charged and neutral pions of each decay, and a dedicated classifier had to be added on top of HPS to separate the decay modes, in particular h$^{\pm}\pi^0$ from h$^{\pm}\pi^0\pi^0$~\cite{CMS-HIG-20-006}.

\noindent Overall, the revised comparison indicates that the performance of the algorithms varies across misidentified objects and in their description of the internal structure of the $\tau_h$ candidate. Each of the classifiers has been found to have different strengths. \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT} have been shown to outperform in terms of $\tau_h$ recovery. \textsc{PNet} provides the best rejection of misidentified quark and gluon jets, while \textsc{DeepTau v2.5} and \textsc{UParT v2} provide the best rejection of misidentified electrons and muons. When it comes to assign the DM, \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT v2} show overall superiority, as they provide finer assignment of the decay mode. The three algorithms are therefore complementary, and their combination could improve the identification of $\tau_h$ beyond what each of them achieves separately~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-073}. For the time being, however, \textsc{DeepTau} remains the standard for offline analyses, and validating the three algorithms in collision data, as done in the next section, is a necessary step before the jet-based taggers can be used in physics analyses.

\section{Performance with \texorpdfstring{$\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV}{√s = 13.6 TeV} data}
\label{sec:Tau_validation}
% DESTACAR MI TRABAJO AQUÍ
Validating the performance of the algorithms presented for reconstructing and identifying $\tau_h$ in real data is an essential step in ensuring the reliability of any physics analysis using hadronic $\tau$ particles. Therefore, although the algorithms detailed in the previous section have been extensively optimized and evaluated in simulations, it is imperative to verify their performance in each data-taking period, since the detector conditions, the pileup and the reconstruction evolve, and any difference between data and simulation in the reconstruction or identification of $\tau_h$ propagates directly to the analyses that use them.

As part of this thesis work, the $\tau_h$ reconstruction and identification performance of these algorithms has been systematically validated within the Tau POG with the data collected during Run 3, from 2022 to the beginning of 2026, and the results obtained with the 2024 and 2025 data have been published in Ref.~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-074}. The following sections present two fundamental aspects of this validation. Section \ref{subsec:Data-MC-agreement} compares data and simulation for the three identification algorithms, taking the 2024 data as reference, and Section \ref{subsec:TES_checks} studies the stability of the performance across the different data-taking periods. These studies do not measure the corrections that analyses apply to the simulation, which are derived centrally with dedicated methods and are summarized at the end of this chapter.


\subsection{Comparison between data and simulation}
\label{subsec:Data-MC-agreement}
%
To evaluate the performance of the $\tau_h$ modeling in CMS, the simulation is compared with data collected during different data-taking periods for various distributions of relevant variables. The validation is performed in a sample enriched in $Z\to\tau\tau$ events in which one $\tau$ lepton decays into a muon and the other hadronically — the $\mu\tau_h$ final state. It provides a large sample of genuine $\tau_h$, since the muon allows the events to be recorded with high efficiency and its reconstruction is well understood, so that differences between data and simulation can be attributed to the $\tau_h$. These events constitute the signal of the validation. The main backgrounds come from processes in which a quark or gluon jet is misidentified as $\tau_h$, mainly $W$+jets production, in which the muon comes from the decay of the $W$ boson, QCD multijet events and top quark pair production, together with $Z\to\mu\mu$ events in which one of the muons is misidentified as $\tau_h$.

The comparison between data and simulation has been performed for all the data-taking periods of Run 3, using the same simulated samples for all of them, from the Summer24 campaign, which are produced with the detector conditions of 2024. This year is therefore the only period in which data and simulation correspond to the same conditions, so that the differences between them can be attributed to the modeling of the $\tau_h$ rather than to changes in the detector, and it is the one presented in this section. The same comparison with the first $26.5$ fb$^{-1}$ of data collected in 2025 leads to the same conclusions~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-074}, and the other periods are discussed together in Section \ref{subsec:TES_checks}. In all periods, the data were recorded with single-muon triggers with $p_T$ thresholds of $24$ and $27$ GeV, and the 2024 data correspond to an integrated luminosity of $109$ fb$^{-1}$. The simulated samples are processed in the NanoAOD format (version 15), which includes the outputs of \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT} v2 for $\tau_h$ identification, and the data of all periods are processed with the same version, which for 2022 and 2023 required a dedicated reprocessing. The Drell--Yan process is simulated at NLO with \POWHEG{} interfaced with \PYTHIA{}, and the simulated samples also include $W$+jets, top quark pair and single top quark production, and diboson processes, each normalized to the most precise available cross section. Since the Drell--Yan process contributes both to the signal and to the background, it is shown in the figures split into three contributions according to the generated particle matched to the $\tau_h$ candidate, which can be a genuine $\tau_h$ — $Z\to\tau_\mu\tau_h$, the signal — an electron or a muon — $Z\to\ell\ell$, $\ell\to\tau_h$, mostly from $Z\to\mu\mu$ decays — or a jet — Drell--Yan, $j\to\tau_h$. The QCD multijet background, which is challenging to model in simulation, is estimated from data in a sideband with the same selection but with muon and $\tau_h$ candidates of the same charge, where the contributions of the other processes predicted by the simulation are subtracted, and the result is extrapolated to the signal region, with opposite charges.

The simulation is corrected for the differences with data that are not related to the $\tau_h$, namely the pileup profile, the muon identification and isolation efficiencies, and the $p_T$ spectra of the $Z$ boson and of the top quark pair. No correction specific to the $\tau_h$ is applied, neither to its identification efficiency nor to its energy scale, so that the residual differences between data and simulation directly reflect how well the $\tau_h$ is modeled before calibration.

Since the three algorithms reject each background to a different extent and only partially overlap in the misidentified objects they select (Section \ref{subsec:tau_IDcomparison}), their selections can also be combined to exploit the strengths of each one. In particular, \textsc{PNet} provides the strongest rejection of quark and gluon jets but is outperformed by \textsc{DeepTau} in the rejection of muons at the tightest working points, whereas \textsc{UParT} already provides the best rejection of electrons and muons. The combination is therefore most useful for \textsc{PNet}, and Figure \ref{fig:mvis_PNetDeepTau_comb} shows the $m_\text{vis}$ distribution obtained when the \textsc{PNet} selection of Figure \ref{fig:mvis_pnet} is complemented with the Tight working point of the \textsc{DeepTau} discriminant against muons. This requirement is applied only to the candidates reconstructed by HPS, the only ones for which this discriminant is available, while the $\tau_h$ recovered only as jets are selected with the requirements of \textsc{PNet} alone. The additional requirement removes most of the $Z\to\mu\mu$ events that form the peak close to $90$ GeV with \textsc{PNet} alone, while the $Z\to\tau\tau$ yield and the backgrounds from misidentified jets are barely changed. It also removes the local excess of data over simulation found around $90$ GeV with \textsc{PNet} alone, which indicates that the rate of muons misidentified by this algorithm is underestimated in the simulation, so that the ratio becomes flat at the same level as in the rest of the distribution. The combined selection therefore keeps the rejection of jets of \textsc{PNet} together with the rejection of muons of \textsc{DeepTau}, and selects a lower background than either of the two algorithms alone.\bigskip

\noindent Overall, the comparison between data and simulation in 2024 has confirmed the robustness of the $\tau_h$ reconstruction and identification algorithms. The following section extends this validation to the other periods of Run 3, with the aim of addressing possible effects arising from the evolving detector conditions.

\subsection{Stability across data-taking periods}
\label{subsec:TES_checks}
%
The previous section verified the modeling of the $\tau_h$ reconstruction and identification in simulation with the 2024 data, the period whose conditions the simulation reproduces. This section, in contrast, focuses on the response of the detector throughout the various periods of Run 3. Across these periods, the detector is subject to variations in its operating conditions due to the increase in instantaneous luminosity and the aging of its components, which can affect key aspects of the $\tau_h$ performance, such as the energy scale, the identification efficiency and the misidentification rate. Studying the stability of the data is therefore necessary to ensure that they remain reliably described by the simulation, and serves as an independent check that complements the comparison of the previous section.

The comparison between data and simulation is first repeated for each data-taking period of Run 3, from 2022 to the first $25.8$ fb$^{-1}$ of 2026, with the same selection and the same simulated samples as in the previous section, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:mvis_all_years}. The reference variable is the visible mass of the $\mu\tau_h$ system, $m_\text{vis}$, a robust observable that is sensitive to possible discrepancies in the $\tau_h$ reconstruction, since the behavior of the muon is well understood in CMS. Given that the simulation is the same for all periods, it provides a common reference, so that the changes in the agreement from one period to another reflect changes in the data. The shape of the distribution is well described in all periods, which confirms the good performance of the $\tau_h$ reconstruction and identification during Run 3, while the normalization varies between them, with the data about $15\%$ below the simulation in 2022 and in agreement within about $5\%$ in 2023, 2024 and 2025. These differences are expected, since the simulation does not reproduce the conditions of the other periods and no correction specific to the $\tau_h$ is applied; they are covered in analyses by the corrections derived for each period. The largest change in shape appears in the first data of 2026, where the ratio is about $10\%$ lower in the region of the $Z\to\tau\tau$ peak, between about $50$ and $80$ GeV, than in the tails of the distribution, which points to a lower efficiency for genuine $\tau_h$.

In addition to the comparison with simulation, the stability of the data is examined directly by comparing the data of the different periods with each other, without relying on the simulation. For this comparison, the selection of Section \ref{subsec:Data-MC-agreement} is tightened by requiring the Tight WP of the \textsc{DeepTau} discriminant against jets instead of the Medium one, which increases the purity in genuine $\tau_h$, so that the differences between periods reflect changes in the response of the detector rather than in the background composition. For these studies, \textsc{DeepTau} is the only algorithm used, as it is the algorithm used in analyses — including the one presented in this thesis — and the one for which the corrections to the simulation are derived for each period, so that its stability is the one that directly affects the physics results. The requirement $m_T(\mu,p_T^\text{miss}) < 65$ GeV is kept to preserve a region rich in genuine $\tau_h$.

Figure \ref{fig:mvis_ratio_years} shows the result of this comparison. The data of 2022 and 2023, which show almost no differences between them, are merged into a single sample, and the $m_\text{vis}$ distribution of each period is normalized to unit area before computing the ratios, so that only differences in shape are compared, independently of the integrated luminosity of each period. Each panel takes a different period as reference and shows the bin-by-bin ratio of the distribution of every period to that of the reference, which is therefore flat at unity, so that the evolution of the shape can be followed with respect to each of them. The statistical uncertainty of the ratios is between $1$ and $3\%$ in most bins, well below the observed differences, which can be grouped into two regions with a different behavior:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The region between about $50$ and $80$ GeV corresponds to the $Z\to\tau\tau$ peak and is therefore dominated by genuine $\tau_h$. The ratios are nearly flat in this region, without the slope across the peak that a change in the $\tau_h$ energy scale would produce, which shows that the position of the peak — and therefore the energy response to genuine $\tau_h$ — is stable. Their values decrease slightly with time, by up to about $9\%$ in 2026 with respect to 2022 and 2023. Since the distributions are normalized, this decrease is at least partly a consequence of the larger fraction of events in the tails, and this comparison alone cannot distinguish it from a lower efficiency for genuine $\tau_h$. However, the comparison with simulation of Figure \ref{fig:mvis_all_years} resolves this ambiguity since, up to 2025, the agreement with simulation in the peak region does not degrade\footnote{In 2022, the data lie about $15\%$ below the simulation, but uniformly over the whole distribution, affecting equally the genuine $\tau_h$ of the peak and the misidentified jets of the tails, so that this deficit reflects a difference in the overall normalization rather than a change in the response to genuine $\tau_h$.}, whereas in 2026 the deficit with respect to the simulation is concentred in the peak, which points to a genuine loss of efficiency in the first data of that year, to be corrected by the calibration of that period.
    \item Outside the peak, the tails of the distribution are dominated by jets misidentified as $\tau_h$, mainly from the $W$+jets and QCD multijet backgrounds. In these regions, the fraction of events increases steadily with time. Above $100$ GeV, it is about $5$--$10\%$ higher in 2024, $10$--$15\%$ in 2025 and $15$--$20\%$ in 2026 than in 2022 and 2023, and the increase is even larger at the lowest masses, which indicates a growing contribution of misidentified jets in the latest periods.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This chapter has described how hadronic $\tau$ leptons are reconstructed and identified in CMS, from the reconstruction of the candidates by the HPS algorithm to their identification with \textsc{DeepTau} v2.5 and with the more recent jet-based taggers, \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT} v2. The comparison of the three algorithms in simulation shows that the jet-based taggers recover $\tau_h$ that HPS does not reconstruct, reaching efficiencies of about $90\%$ instead of the $70\%$ allowed by the HPS reconstruction, that \textsc{PNet} rejects misidentified jets more effectively and \textsc{UParT} misidentified electrons and muons, and that the three algorithms only partially overlap in the objects they select, so that their combination can improve the rejection of the different backgrounds.

The performance of these algorithms has then been validated with the data collected during Run 3, which constitutes the contribution of this thesis to the Tau POG, and whose results with the 2024 and 2025 data have been published in Ref.~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-074}. The comparison between data and simulation in $Z\to\tau\tau\to\mu\tau_h$ events shows that \textsc{DeepTau} is the algorithm best described by the simulation, as a result of its domain adaptation, whereas the larger differences found for \textsc{PNet} and \textsc{UParT}, especially at the highest values of their discriminants, would require larger corrections before they can be used in analyses. The study of the different data-taking periods shows that the response to genuine $\tau_h$, and in particular its energy scale, remains stable throughout Run 3, apart from a loss of efficiency in the first data of 2026, while the contribution of misidentified jets grows with time. Taken together, these studies show that the simulation describes the $\tau_h$ well, but that residual differences with data remain, of up to about $15\%$ in the overall normalization, and that they change from one period to another as the detector conditions evolve.

The remaining differences between data and simulation are corrected in analyses through the calibration of the $\tau_h$ identification, performed centrally by the Tau POG for each data-taking period~\cite{CMS-TAU-24-001}. This calibration consists of scale factors for the identification efficiency, defined as the ratio between the efficiencies measured in data and in simulation, and of a correction to the $\tau_h$ energy scale, both measured for each decay mode, and the former also as a function of the $p_T$ of the $\tau_h$, with a tag-and-probe method in $Z\to\tau\tau\to\mu\tau_h$ events, a selection very similar to the one used in the validation and a maximum likelihood fit to the $m_\text{vis}$ distribution. Analogous scale factors are derived for electrons and muons misidentified as $\tau_h$, whereas the contribution of misidentified jets, which depends on the selection and, as shown in Section \ref{subsec:TES_checks}, on the data-taking period, is usually estimated directly from data in each analysis. For \textsc{DeepTau} v2.5, the scale factors are typically within $20\%$ of unity, closer to it than for the previous version thanks to the domain adaptation, and the energy scale corrections measured in 2022 are within $3\%$ of unity~\cite{CMS-TAU-24-001}.

The validation presented in this chapter is complementary to this calibration and a prerequisite for it. Since these corrections are factors on the efficiency and on the energy scale, measured in a few bins of $p_T$ and decay mode, they are only reliable if the simulation describes well the rest of the properties of the $\tau_h$ candidates, which is precisely what the validation verifies for each data-taking period and identification algorithm. In addition, it identifies the changes that require the corrections to be derived separately for each period, such as the loss of efficiency in 2026 or the growing contribution of misidentified jets, and it anticipates the larger corrections that the jet-based algorithms would require. The search for $HH\to bb\tau\tau$ presented in this thesis, which identifies the $\tau_h$ with \textsc{DeepTau} v2.5, applies the corrections of each data-taking period described above (Section \ref{sec:bbtt_corrections}), estimates the contribution of misidentified jets from data (Section \ref{sec:bbtt_ff}), and propagates the uncertainties of all of them as systematic uncertainties of the result (Section \ref{sec:bbtt_systematics}). Therefore, through these corrections, the validation of the $\tau_h$ performance underlies the results presented in the following chapters, as it does those of any CMS analysis with hadronically decaying $\tau$ leptons in the final state.
